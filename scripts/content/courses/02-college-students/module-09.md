### Module 1 · Your Portfolio Is a Story, Not a Folder

Recruiters spend seconds on your portfolio - it must tell a **story in that time**. A good portfolio answers three questions instantly: who you are in one line, what you have made with two to four strong real projects, and what you can do with proof in screenshots, demos, and results.

Every item should carry one sentence of impact: what it does, who used it, and what result it produced. That one-sentence value line is the whole game, and signal beats size every time.

## Learning Objectives

- Answer the three portfolio questions instantly
- Write a one-line who-you-are pitch
- Frame projects with one sentence of impact each
- Choose quality over quantity

## The Three Questions

- **Who you are:** one line, such as 'final-year B.Com student who automates the boring parts of finance work'
- **What you have made:** two to four strong real projects - quality over quantity
- **What you can do:** proof in screenshots, demos, and results
### Why seconds decide
- The first three seconds set everything that follows
- Signal, not size, wins that window

## The Impact Sentence

```text
The impact line formula:
1. What it does
2. Who used it
3. What result it produced
Example: 'A timetable-clash finder
used by 12 hostelmates every week'
```
- Impact lines let reviewers grasp value instantly
- One strong sentence beats a paragraph of features

## Quality Over Quantity

### The curation discipline
- Two strong projects beat a folder of half-dones
- Curated strength demonstrates judgment
### The prune habit
- Ask of every item: does it pass the one-line impact test?
- If you cannot write its impact, it does not belong
- Story, not inventory, wins the first thirty seconds

## Key Takeaways

- A portfolio answers who, what, and proof in seconds
- One impact line per item is the whole game
- Two to four strong projects beat a big folder
- Curated strength demonstrates judgment
- Signal beats size in the first thirty seconds

### Module 2 · The Three-Section Portfolio Plan

Structure minimises effort and maximises signal. The three-section plan is About, Projects, and Proof. Host it simply - a GitHub README with images, a free Notion page, or a one-page site - because a clean simple surface beats a feature-heavy half-finished one.

Each section has a job: About establishes identity in three sentences, Projects shows the two to four builds with thirty-second scannable summaries, and Proof pins your best artifact with a contact line.

## Learning Objectives

- Structure your portfolio as About, Projects, and Proof
- Write a three-sentence About block
- Make each project readable in thirty seconds
- Choose a simple host over a heavy one

## The Three Sections

1. **About** - three sentences: who, what you build, what you are looking for
2. **Projects** - your top two to four builds, each with a thirty-second scannable summary
3. **Proof** - one link to your best artifact, your credits or badges, and a clean contact line
### The scan rule
- A clean minimal structure maximises signal

## Where to Host It

```text
Simple hosting targets:
1. GitHub README with images
2. A free Notion page
3. A one-page static site
```
- Free tiers cover all three
- A clean simple surface beats a half-finished heavy one
- Shareability beats feature addiction

## Thirty-Second Scannability

### The reader's budget
- Reviewers scan before they read - design for that
- Short paragraphs, headings, and bullets rule
### The Proof block
- Links, credits or badges, and one clean contact line
- Your best artifact plus a clear way to reach you
- Speed to signal is the entire game

## Key Takeaways

- The structure is About, Projects, and Proof
- About is three sentences of identity and aim
- Each project must scan in thirty seconds
- Host simply - README, Notion, or a one-page site
- A clean simple surface beats a heavy half-finished one

### Module 3 · The STAR Case Study Frame

For each project, write a mini case study using **STAR**: Situation, Task, Action, and Result. The frame turns a project into a portable case narrative that works in a portfolio, in interviews, and in application essays.

AI is great at drafting STAR summaries from your reflection notes. Draft with AI, then read it out loud - if it does not sound like you, rewrite until it does. Your voice survives only if you revise it.

## Learning Objectives

- Structure a project summary with STAR
- Write a measurable Result line
- Use AI to draft, then revise for your voice
- Make each case study portable across contexts

## The Four Parts

1. **Situation:** the problem you chose and why it was real
2. **Task:** what you set out to make happen
3. **Action:** what you actually did - build, test, iterate
4. **Result:** a measurable outcome, such as 'cut research prep from four hours to one'
### The portable format
- STAR works on a portfolio page and in an interview the same way

## Measurable Results Convince

```text
Strong Result lines:
1st: 'Cut research prep from 4 hrs to 1'
2nd: 'Used by 12 classmates in a week'
3rd: 'Automated a weekly 40-minute manual report'
```
- Numbers like these convert effort into proof
- Measured results are instant credibility

## Draft With AI, Revise for Voice

### The drafting loop
- Feed your Mission 8 reflection notes into AI
- Ask for a STAR summary draft
### The voice gate
- Read the draft out loud
- If it does not sound like you, rewrite until it does
- Paste it verbatim and you lose the one thing recruiters trust

## Key Takeaways

- STAR means Situation, Task, Action, Result
- STAR turns a project into a portable case narrative
- A measurable Result line is instant proof
- AI drafts from your reflection notes
- Revise AI drafts until they sound like you

## Practice Challenge

1. Write a STAR summary for your strongest project
2. Note a measurable result you can defend
3. Draft with AI, then revise it out loud for your voice

### Module 4 · Using AI to Draft, Not Fabricate

Your portfolio is where honesty pays off most - because interviews will verify it. Use AI to polish the wording and structure of your own projects, but never to invent experience. An unverifiable claim unmasks instantly in a technical question-and-answer.

The rules are precise: claim nothing you cannot demonstrate, state your exact role in team projects, and describe how you used AI. A portfolio that survives scrutiny is worth more than a brilliant one that collapses under one question.

## Learning Objectives

- Use AI to polish, never to fabricate, portfolio content
- State your precise role in team projects
- Frame AI use as a directed strength
- Keep every claim verifiable

## Polish, Never Fabricate

- Use AI to **polish wording and structure** for projects that are really yours
- It cannot and must not invent experience
- Unverifiable claims unmask instantly in technical Q&A
### The verification test
- Could you demo or defend every claim?
- If not, soften or remove it

## Your Role in Team Projects

```text
The precise-role rule:
1. Name your part of the project clearly
2. Never use a vague 'we built everything'
3. Be ready for a question about YOUR part
```
- 'We built' invites questions about you
- Precision shows ownership and survives scrutiny
- Your exact contribution is your strongest story

## Framing AI Use as a Strength

### The honest-AI line
- 'AI for code scaffolding and drafts; I own the design and testing'
- Honest AI roles read as a strength, not a caveat
### Why this wins
- It shows you command the tool rather than rent your thinking
- Interviewers hear ownership and rigor
- A verifiable portfolio beats a flashy fragile one

## Key Takeaways

- AI polishes your real work; it never invents experience
- Unverifiable claims collapse in technical Q&A
- State your precise role in team projects
- Frame AI use as a directed strength
- A portfolio that survives scrutiny beats a fragile brilliant one

## Practice Challenge

1. Audit every claim in your portfolio for verifiability
2. Rewrite any 'we built' phrasing to name your part
3. Write your honest-AI-use line for your top project

### Module 5 · Make It Scannable in Thirty Seconds

Assume the reader has thirty seconds - design for it. Put at the top of each project page a one-line elevator pitch, one screenshot, one demo link, and three bullets covering what, why, and result. Format with short paragraphs, headings, and bullet lists, never walls of text.

Then loop the feedback: show your portfolio to a friend and mentor and time them. If they cannot answer 'what does this person do?' in thirty seconds, cut or move things. That feedback loop is how portfolios mature.

## Learning Objectives

- Design a scannable top block for each project
- Follow scannable formatting rules
- Run a timed thirty-second test
- Iterate based on the feedback loop

## The Scannable Top Block

- **One-line elevator pitch** on the first line
- **One screenshot** and **one demo link**
- **Three bullets:** what, why, result
### The layout job
- Scannable tops let reviewers decide fast
- The core message must survive a glance

## Formatting for Speed

```text
Scannable formatting rules:
1. Short paragraphs
2. Headings that carry the argument
3. Bullet lists over prose blocks
4. Actionable contact at the top too
```
- Walls of text bury the signal
- Structure is how thirty-second reviews find the signal

## The Timed Feedback Loop

### The test
- Time a friend or mentor reading your portfolio for thirty seconds
- Ask them to say back the story you tell
### The iteration rule
- If they cannot answer 'what does this person do?', cut or move things
- Real timers reveal what to cut
- Two rounds of this tighten a portfolio fast

## Key Takeaways

- Thirty seconds is the reader's budget - design for it
- Top each project with pitch, screenshot, demo, and three bullets
- Headings and bullets carry the signal
- Time a friend to test scannability
- Iterate until they can say your story back

## Practice Challenge

1. Build the scannable top for your main project
2. Run the thirty-second timed test with a peer
3. Apply one cut or move from what they revealed

### Module 6 · Honesty Rules That Protect You

A set of honesty rules keeps your portfolio an asset rather than a liability. Every project exists and works, every claim is verifiable, every metric is real, and your role is accurate. One unwritten rule under them all: exaggeration in the portfolio is a test of honesty, and the interview is the check.

Clean honesty is the cheapest competitive advantage you have. A portfolio built on real, verifiable work survives any interview - that is what makes it defendable and valuable.

## Learning Objectives

- Apply the four honesty rules to every claim
- Verify projects exist and work
- Use only real metrics and accurate roles
- Explain why clean honesty is a competitive advantage

## The Four Rules

- **Every project exists and works** - the recruiter can run it or you can demo it live
- **Every claim is verifiable** - screenshots only of real product output
- **Metrics are real** - 'twelve users' means twelve actual users
- **Your role is accurate** - no inflated solo credit on group work
### The audit frame
- Evidence must match claims exactly

## Real Numbers Only

```text
The metric audit:
1. Where did this number come from?
2. Can I show the logs or counters?
3. Does the screenshot match the claim?
4. Is the number specific and true?
```
- Real numbers are the only numbers
- Best-case estimates and AI-suggested figures are out
- A metric check is a two-minute de-risk

## Clean Honesty as an Edge

### Why honesty compounds
- Exaggeration in the portfolio is a test of honesty
- The interview is the check - and clean work passes it
### The advantage
- Clean honesty is the cheapest competitive advantage you have
- Verifiable and demonstrable claims are the rule
- A defended portfolio is worth more than an inflated one

## Key Takeaways

- Every project must exist and work
- Claims are verifiable; metrics are real
- Your role is accurate, never inflated
- Exaggeration is a test; the interview is the check
- Clean honesty is the cheapest competitive advantage

## Practice Challenge

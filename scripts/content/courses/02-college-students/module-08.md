### Module 1 · The One-Week Project Method

A one-week build teaches more than a semester of theory. The method runs on three rules: five days, small scope; boring-but-real beats clever-but-mock; and AI is your workforce while you own the quality. The whole mission is a product sprint.

The rhythm is simple: scope Monday, build Tuesday to Wednesday, test Thursday, package Friday. Every day's output feeds the portfolio piece you will present in Mission 9, so the sprint is never wasted effort.

## Learning Objectives

- State the three rules of the one-week project method
- Explain the five-day sprint rhythm
- Choose boring-but-real over clever-but-mock
- Position AI as your workforce under your direction

## The Three Rules

- **Five days, small scope** - you are aiming to ship, not to build the world's best anything
- **Boring-but-real beats clever-but-mock** - a tool your hostel-mates actually use beats a hypothetical app
- **AI is your workforce** - you direct it, it codes and drafts and designs, you own the quality
### The summing line
- Shipping beats grandiosity for learning and portfolio

## The Sprint Rhythm

```text
The week at a glance:
Monday: scope - pick one idea, cut it small
Tuesday-Wednesday: build the core flow
Thursday: test with one real user
Friday: package and share
```
- Every day's output builds the portfolio piece
- The sprint protects you from indefinite polish

## Why Real Wins

### Real pain makes real users
- Real daily problems yield used tools and learning
- A hostel-mates tool gets actual feedback
### The workforce relationship
- AI accelerates; you own scope, quality, and outcome
- Boring-but-real beats clever-but-mock every time
- A tool someone uses is the difference between a project and a toy

## Key Takeaways

- One week, small scope, ship something real
- Boring-but-real beats clever-but-mock
- AI is your workforce; you own quality
- The five-day rhythm feeds the portfolio
- A tool someone uses beats a hypothetical app

### Module 2 · Day 1: Pick and Scope

Day one is decision day: pick ONE idea that fits your slice of life, and scope it brutally. The counter-scope rule is sharp - if you cannot build a v1 in three days, cut the scope in half. A half-scoped project that ships beats a grand one that does not.

The deliverable is a one-page brief: three user stories, the ONE flow that must work, and a written definition of done. This brief is what you show a classmate for the go-ahead.

## Learning Objectives

- Choose one buildable idea from the candidate list
- Apply the three-day scoping rule
- Write three user stories
- Define done for your v1

## The Idea Menu

- **Flashcard generator** from your notes
- **Syllabus or deadline tracker** that nags you
- **Study-time splitter** - pomodoro with AI plans
- **Weekly lecture summariser** for your course group
- **Mini chatbot** that answers 'where do I find X' for your college
### The picking rule
- Choose the one that solves pain you personally feel weekly

## The Brutal Scope

```text
The scoping test:
1. Can I build a v1 in three days?
2. If no, cut the scope in half
3. Repeat until the answer is yes
4. Write it down as the v1 goal
```
- A half-scoped project that ships beats a grand one that dies
- The core flow decides what done means

## The One-Page Brief

### What the brief holds
- Three user stories: who, what, why
- The ONE flow that must work
- A written definition of done
### Why the brief matters
- It is your approval gate with a classmate or mentor
- It keeps the week scoped when you feel the urge to add
- It becomes the first line of your portfolio case study

## Key Takeaways

- Day one picks one idea and scopes it brutally
- The three-day test cuts scope ruthlessly
- Three user stories define who the tool serves
- The ONE flow and a written done definition anchor the week
- A half-scoped project that ships beats a grand one that dies

### Module 3 · Days 2-3: Build the Core

Build the single core flow first - everything else is a stretch goal. The build order is: skeleton, core function, error handling, and daily commits. A working-but-ugly core on Wednesday night is a project you can present.

Resist the urge to add features. Feature creep is the number one project killer - an unpolished core on Friday beats half-built extras every time.

## Learning Objectives

- Scaffold the app or script with AI
- Make the one core function work end to end
- Handle the common ways users break it
- Commit daily to keep a presentable state

## The Build Order

1. **Skeleton:** let the AI scaffold the app or script for your one flow
2. **Core function:** make the one thing work - input to processing to output
3. **Error handling:** ask AI what the five ways a user could break this are, and handle them
4. **Commit daily** - a working-but-ugly core each night is presentable

## Defence Against Feature Creep

```text
The feature-creep brake:
1. Every new idea goes to the 'later' list
2. Nothing joins the build until the core works
3. Ask: does this serve the definition of done?
4. If no, defer it
```
- Adding options before the core works kills projects
- The 'later' list is where good ideas wait safely

## The Presentable State

### Why daily commits matter
- A working-but-ugly core each night is presentable
- Git saves you from midnight rollback disasters
### The Wednesday test
- If the core works by Wednesday night, you can present it
- An unpolished core on Friday beats half-built extras
- Scope survives; polish is a final-day luxury

## Key Takeaways

- Build skeleton, one core function, error handling, daily commits
- Input to processing to output is the whole core
- Feature creep is the number one project killer
- Daily commits guarantee a presentable state at day end
- An unpolished working core beats half-built extras

## Practice Challenge

### Module 4 · Day 4: Real-User Test (Even One Friend Counts)

Ship to a real human before you call it done. Give it to one friend or classmate with zero explanation, watch rather than coach them use it, and ask three questions: what did you try first, where did you get stuck, and would you use this again?

'Works on my machine' is not a product. Even a ten-minute test with one person surfaces the fix that makes the difference between a folder and a project - because an uncoached observer reveals real hesitation and drop-off.

## Learning Objectives

- Test your build with one real user before calling it done
- Observe without coaching
- Ask the three feedback questions
- Turn a ten-minute test into concrete fixes

## The Zero-Explanation Test

- Give it to one friend or classmate with **zero explanation**
- Watch - do not coach - as they use it
- Note where they hesitate or give up
### Why no coaching
- Coached users hide the real friction
- Real users are the only honest usability test

## The Three Questions

```text
The feedback triad:
1. What did you try first?
2. Where did you get stuck?
3. Would you use this again?
```
- The first answer reveals your core flow's obviousness
- The second pinpoints the friction to fix
- The third predicts whether it lives or dies

## From Folder to Project

### The one-test payoff
- A ten-minute test surfaces the fix that matters most
- Small tests yield the fixes that matter most
### The deep truth
- 'Works on my machine' is not a product
- Even one user turns a folder into a project
- Observation plus those three questions is a real test

## Key Takeaways

- Test with a real user before calling it done
- Watch without coaching to reveal real friction
- Ask the triad: tried first, stuck where, would you reuse it
- A ten-minute test yields the fixes that matter most
- One user turns a folder into a project

## Practice Challenge

1. Give your build to one friend with zero explanation
2. Record where they hesitated or gave up
3. Apply the triad and fix the top two issues

### Module 5 · Day 5: Package and Share

Package it like it matters - because it will be your portfolio. The deliverable is a README with what it does, how to run it, and one screenshot; a sixty-to-ninety-second demo; a deployed link if it is a web app; and a share in your course group and on LinkedIn.

A packaged project with a real user is ten times the recruiting story of an unshared folder. The packaging is the work - it is what makes your build visible, usable, and credible.

## Learning Objectives

- Write a complete README for your project
- Record a short demo of the core flow
- Deploy v1 on a free tier
- Share it publicly and collect feedback

## The Packaging Set

- **README:** what it does, how to run it, one screenshot
- **Demo:** a sixty-to-ninety-second screen recording of the core flow
- **Deploy:** a link that opens - Netlify, Streamlit, Colab, or GitHub Pages
- **Share:** your course group and LinkedIn - feedback is the point

## Why Packaging Is the Work

```text
What packaging buys you:
1. A stranger can run it from the README alone
2. A sixty-second demo proves it works
3. A live link makes it real
4. Public feedback makes it better
```
- A caller must run it from the README alone
- A short walkthrough is the fastest proof of working

## The Share Loop

### Free deploy targets
- Netlify and GitHub Pages for web apps
- Streamlit for data tools, Colab for notebooks
### Post and reflect
- Post it where peers and recruiters will see it
- Feedback is the point; every reply teaches something
- A packaged project with a real user is ten times your recruiting story

## Key Takeaways

- Package a README, a demo, a deploy, and a share
- A stranger must run it from the README alone
- A sixty-to-ninety-second demo proves the core flow
- Free tiers host a shareable link in minutes
- Public feedback is the point of sharing

### Module 6 · The Reflection Loop: What Makes It Portfolio-Worthy

After shipping, run the reflection that makes projects impressive. Capture what you built and why it matters, the hardest technical problem and how you solved it, what you would improve with two more weeks, and AI's exact role. This five-minute reflection becomes your case study in Mission 9.

Never skip it - it is the difference between 'I built a thing' and an interview story. Reflection turns a build into a narrative with judgment, recovery, and clarity.

## Learning Objectives

- Write the four-part reflection for your project
- Frame the hardest problem as judgment and recovery
- Separate your role from AI's role precisely
- Turn the reflection into a mission-9 case study

## The Four Lines

- **What I built** (one line) and **why it matters** (to whom)
- **Hardest technical problem** - the error or gap that cost the most time, and how you solved it
- **What I would improve** with two more weeks - shows judgment, not failure
- **AI's exact role** - which parts AI did, which you did, and why
### Why each line
- Judgment, recovery, and clarity are what recruiters score

## Hard Problem as Strength

```text
The hard-problem frame:
1. The error or gap that cost the most time
2. The steps you took to diagnose it
3. What you tried and what finally worked
4. What that taught you for next time
```
- How you handled the hard part is the signal
- It shows judgment and recovery, not weakness

## From Build to Interview Story

### AI's role, honestly
- State which parts AI did and which you did
- Clarity about AI's role is a strength in interviews
### The case-study link
- The reflection is your Mission 9 STAR raw material
- Five minutes now becomes a story you can tell in any room
- Reflection turns a toy into a portfolio piece

## Key Takeaways

- Reflect on build, hardest problem, improvements, and AI's role
- The hardest problem is a judgment and recovery story
- Two-more-weeks wishes show maturity, not failure
- Honest AI roles read as a strength in interviews
- The reflection becomes your Mission 9 case study

### Module 1 · Why This Course Matters for YOU
Whether you're studying engineering, commerce, medicine, arts, or law — AI is now a standard tool in every field. It's not a fancy extra anymore; it's how work gets done. Learning it now gives you three edges:

- **Studies:** faster, better research and assignments, and smarter revision.
- **Portfolio:** projects that impress recruiters during internships and placements.
- **Career:** roles are increasingly "AI-augmented" — *your skills plus AI beat AI alone*.

But this course is not about replacing your thinking with AI. It's about **augmenting** it — working *with* AI the way a pilot works with an autopilot: you stay in command, and the tool amplifies you.

### Module 2 · What AI Really Is (A College-Level Honest View)
- **AI** is the broad field of systems that perform tasks requiring intelligence — understanding language, images, data, and decisions.
- **Machine Learning (ML)** is how AI learns: from data and examples, not hand-written rules.
- **Large Language Models (LLMs)** like GPT, Claude, and Gemini are trained on vast text corpora to understand and generate language.
- **Generative AI** creates new content: text, code, images, audio, and video.
- **Agents & automation** chain AI actions into workflows that do real work.

The mental model that matters: an LLM is a *very fast, very confident text predictor*. It has read enormous amounts of writing and can continue almost any pattern you start — which is why it is brilliant at drafting and dangerous if you treat it as truth.

### Module 3 · What AI Can NOT Do (Be Honest, It Matters)
- It **hallucinates** — confidently states false information. Always verify.
- It has a **knowledge cutoff** — without tools, it may know nothing recent.
- It **doesn't know your context** — your college, your assignment rubric, your deadline, your professor's preferences.
- It **won't make you learn** — passive copy-pasting creates "fluent ignorance" that surfaces in exams and interviews.

Your university years are about building judgment. AI is the sharpest tool you'll ever own for that — but only if **you** supply the judgment. Treat every output as a draft from a clever but careless intern.

### Module 5 · Getting Set Up (Week 1 Toolkit)
Create accounts — free tiers are fine — for **one or two** of each:

- **Chat assistant:** ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini.
- **Research helper:** Google Scholar plus one AI research tool.
- **Coding companion:** GitHub Copilot (student tier) or another AI code assistant.
- **Notes:** Notion or Obsidian with an AI note-taker.

Then **calibrate one tool** this week: ask it 5 questions from your current semester and rate how useful each answer is. This builds your personal "trust meter" — the skill of knowing when AI is reliable and when to double-check.

### Module 6 · The Academic Rule (Non-Negotiable)
- Follow **your university's** AI policy. Many allow AI with disclosure; some restrict or ban it in specific assessments.
- Cite AI use like any source when required — your department's guide governs; Mission 4 covers the mechanics.
- Never submit AI output as your own assessed work unless the policy explicitly permits it.
- When in doubt — **ask your professor**. Transparency is a feature, not a bug.

Start one habit now: keep a short **AI-usage log** for each assignment (what you asked, what you changed). It makes disclosure easy and honest — and it impresses in interviews.

## Real-World Examples

- A commerce student uses AI to summarise 10 annual reports into a comparison table, then verifies every key number against the originals.
- A final-year engineering team drafts their thesis outline with AI, then each member defends and rewrites every section in their own words.
- A law student generates flashcards from a week's readings and logs that AI use in their study journal.
- An intern automates their weekly report formatting with a simple AI prompt, freeing two hours for real analytical work.

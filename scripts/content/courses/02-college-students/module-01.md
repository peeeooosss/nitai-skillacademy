### Module 2 · What AI Really Is (An Honest College-Level View)

Lets replace the hype with a clear mental model. **Artificial Intelligence (AI)** is the broad field of systems that perform tasks requiring intelligence - understanding language, recognising images, analysing data, and making decisions. Under that umbrella sit machine learning, large language models, generative AI, and agents, each doing a distinct job.

The single mental model that matters: an LLM is a *very fast, very confident text predictor*. It has read enormous amounts of writing and can continue almost any pattern you start. That single idea explains why AI is brilliant at drafting and dangerous if you treat it as a source of truth.

## Learning Objectives

- Define AI, ML, LLMs, generative AI, and agents in one line each
- Hold the 'fast confident text predictor' mental model
- Explain why the predictor model makes AI both great and risky
- Place these concepts in real academic workflows

## The Vocabulary Stack

- **AI** - the broad field of systems that perform intelligent tasks
- **Machine Learning (ML)** - how AI learns, from data and examples, not hand-written rules
- **Large Language Models (LLMs)** - models like GPT, Claude, and Gemini trained on vast text corpora
- **Generative AI** - creates new content: text, code, images, audio, and video
- **Agents and automation** - chain AI actions into workflows that do real work
### The mental model
- An LLM is a very fast, very confident text predictor
- It continues patterns it has seen - it does not 'know' or 'verify' anything

## Why the Predictor Model Matters

```text
Predictor model consequences:
1. Brilliant at drafting - continuing your pattern fluently
2. Dangerous if treated as truth - it optimises plausibility, not accuracy
3. Great for brainstorming - many starting points fast
4. Bad for facts - it guesses what text would look like
```
- When you understand the predictor model, AI stupidity stops surprising you
- You learn to use the tool for its strength (generation) and never for its weakness (verification)

## From Models to Workflows

### Single tools compound into systems
- One LLM can draft; a workflow of tools can research, draft, check, and cite
- Agents take these workflows and run them with a goal and tools
### Your academic toolkit
- Chat assistants for drafting and explaining
- Research helpers that couple databases with AI summaries
- Coding companions that write, explain, and debug scripts
- The model is the engine; your workflow design is the vehicle

## Key Takeaways

- AI is a stack: AI, ML, LLMs, generative AI, and agents work together
- An LLM is a very fast, confident text predictor - not a truth machine
- The predictor model explains both the brilliance and the danger
- You use AI for generation and verification yourself
- Workflow design turns single tools into an academic system

### Module 3 · What AI Can NOT Do (An Honest Look)

To use AI well you must know its limits as precisely as its powers. The limits are not temporary bugs - they are structural features of how these models work. The four that matter most in college are hallucination, knowledge cutoffs, missing context, and the learning trap.

Your university years are about building judgment. AI is the sharpest tool you will ever own for that - but only if you supply the judgment. Treat every output as a draft from a clever but careless intern, and the tool becomes an asset instead of a liability.

## Learning Objectives

- Name the four structural limits of today's AI
- Explain hallucination and why it cannot be fully fixed
- Identify what AI cannot know about your personal context
- Explain the 'fluent ignorance' trap of passive copy-paste

## Hallucination and Cutoffs

- It **hallucinates** - confidently states information that is false. Always verify
- It has a **knowledge cutoff** - without tools, it may know nothing recent
- Hallucination is not malice; it is the predictor optimising for plausibility
### To protect yourself
- Verify every fact you intend to rely on
- For recent events, use tools or manual checking
- When a number matters, re-find it in the primary source

## The Context Gap

- It **does not know your context** - your college, your assignment rubric, your deadline, your professors preferences
- No model has ever seen your lecture slides, your syllabus, or your markers expectations
```text
The context you must supply:
1. Your course or level (first year vs master's)
2. The assignment brief and rubric
3. Your deadline and word limit
4. The sources you are allowed to use
5. What 'good looks like' in your discipline
```
- Supplying context is exactly the judgment work AI cannot do

## The Learning Trap

### Fluent ignorance is the real danger
- Passive copy-pasting creates false confidence that collapses in exams and interviews
- Output you never interrogate fades the moment you are tested on it
### The fix in one line
- Treat AI output as a draft to interrogate, not an answer to submit
- Rewriting in your own words is where the learning actually happens
- An answer you can explain without the AI is an answer you own

## Key Takeaways

- AI hallucinates - confidently false info is a structural feature
- Knowledge cutoffs mean recent facts need tools or manual checks
- AI never knows your personal academic context - you supply it
- Passive copy-paste breeds fluent ignorance that surfaces in testing
- You are the judgment layer; that is the whole point of college

### Module 5 · Getting Set Up (Your Week-1 Toolkit)

Before any deep work, you need a small toolkit. The goal is not to collect every AI tool - it is to calibrate **one or two** per category and learn where each is trustworthy. A tiny stack you actually use beats a massive stack you abandon.

The setup has two parts: create accounts on the free tiers you need, then calibrate one tool with real questions from your own semester. That calibration builds your personal trust meter - the skill of knowing when AI is reliable and when to double-check.

## Learning Objectives

- Assemble a minimal toolkit across four categories
- Create free-tier accounts for the tools you pick
- Calibrate one AI tool against your own semester questions
- Define what a trust meter is and why it matters

## The Four-Category Stack

- **Chat assistant:** ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini - your daily workhorse
- **Research helper:** Google Scholar plus one AI research tool such as Elicit or Scite
- **Coding companion:** GitHub Copilot (student tier) or another AI code assistant
- **Notes:** Notion or Obsidian with an AI note-taking integration
### Keep it minimal
- One or two tools per category is plenty
- Free tiers cover essentially everything in this course
- Add tools only when a real workflow demands them

## Calibrate One Tool This Week

```text
The calibration drill:
1. Pick your main chat assistant
2. Ask it 5 questions from your current semester subjects
3. Rate each answer's usefulness from 1 to 5
4. Note where it was wrong, vague, or excellent
5. Repeat monthly as your courses change
```
- Calibration builds your personal trust meter
- You learn each tool's strengths and blind spots in your own field
- A calibrated tool is a tool you can trust at speed

## Why Setup Matters More Than It Looks

### Setup is the difference between use and abandonment

- A calibrated, minimal toolkit survives semester pressure
### Your first output goal

- Small, low-friction setup is what makes tools survive the semester

### Module 6 · The Academic Rule (Non-Negotiable)

Using AI well in college is not only about technique - it is about integrity. The academic rule is simple and absolute: follow your institution's policy, disclose your use honestly, and never submit AI output as your own assessed work unless the policy explicitly allows it.

This rule protects you and your degree. When you follow it, AI becomes a skill you can talk about openly - in tutorials, in interviews, in reference letters. Transparency is a feature, not a bug.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why the academic rule is non-negotiable
- Locate and follow your university's AI-use policy
- Keep an AI-usage log for every assignment
- State how to disclose AI use when required

## Know Your Policy

- Follow **your university's** AI policy - it varies by institution and by assessment
- Many programs allow AI with disclosure; some restrict or ban it in specific assessments
- Your department's guide governs - Mission 4 covers citation mechanics
### The questions to answer
- Is AI permitted for this assessment?
- If yes, with what disclosure and citation steps?
- Are there banned tools or banned stages (planning, drafting, editing)?

## The Usage Log Habit

```text
Your per-assignment AI-usage log:
1. What you asked (copy the prompt)
2. Who produced the text (you or the AI)
3. What you changed and why
4. Which pieces you verified against a source
5. The final AI-use disclosure line you will submit
```
- A short log per assignment makes disclosure easy and honest
- It impresses in interviews - it shows maturity and control
- Started now, the habit costs seconds and saves crises

## Disclosure and Doubt

### Transparency beats evasion
- When in doubt, disclose - and when required, cite AI use like any source
- Ask your professor when a policy is ambiguous; that is a sign of maturity
### The line that protects you
- 'AI was used for drafting and feedback; all arguments, decisions, and final wording are my own.'
- Adjust the line to your actual usage and your department's rules
- A disclosed, verified assignment is a defensible assignment

## Key Takeaways

- The academic rule is non-negotiable: know, follow, disclose
- Policies vary by institution and by assessment - always check the brief
- Never submit AI output as your own work unless policy allows it
- An AI-usage log makes honest disclosure easy and impresses interviewers
- When in doubt, ask your professor and disclose more, not less

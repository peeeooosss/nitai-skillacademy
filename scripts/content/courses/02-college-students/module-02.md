### Module 1 · Why Prompting Is a Core Academic Skill

Tools do not hand you intelligence - they hand you drafts in response to a request. A vague question produces a vague answer; a precise prompt produces work you can actually use. That asymmetry is why prompting is the single highest-leverage academic skill with AI.

You already hold the raw material for great prompts - your assignments, rubrics, and lecture notes. This mission teaches you to turn them into prompts on purpose, so that every interaction with AI starts from your context, not a guess.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why prompt quality controls output quality
- Compare vague versus precise prompts side by side
- Identify the raw material your own course provides for prompts
- Reframe prompting as a skill to practice, not a trick to memorise

## The Vague Versus Precise Gap

- **Vague:** 'Explain AI' - returns a 500-word ramble
- **Precise:** 'Explain supervised vs unsupervised learning in 5 bullets, aimed at a second-year computing student, with one example each' - instantly usable
- The tool is identical; only the prompt changed
### Why the gap exists
- The model predicts what you plausibly want; it cannot read your intent
- Specificity about role, scope, and format changes the outcome

## Your Academic Raw Material

- **Assignment briefs** - the exact scope and requirements
- **Rubrics** - what the marker scores determines what you ask for
- **Lecture notes** - the concepts and terms already in your course's language
```text
A great prompt pipeline:
1. Copy the relevant rubric row
2. Paste in your own notes or draft
3. State the output format and audience
4. Ask for the specific deliverable
```
- The best prompts quote your materials directly

## Prompting as Deliberate Practice

### A skill to build, not a trick to copy
- Each subject has its own terminology; prompts tuned to your field work best
- Track which prompts produced useful output and why
### The compounding effect
- Every good prompt is a reusable asset for your next assignment
- Prompting well turns generic tools into subject-specific assistants
- This entire mission is about building that deliberate habit

## Key Takeaways

- Prompt quality controls output quality more than any other factor
- A precise prompt is instantly usable; a vague one is generic
- Your rubrics, assignments, and lecture notes power strong prompts
- Quoting your own course material makes prompts subject-specific
- Prompting is deliberate practice - it compounds into reusable templates

### Module 2 · The 4-Part Prompt Framework (RCTF)

There is a professional frame used across AI work that makes prompts dramatically more useful: **RCTF** - Role, Context, Task, Format. Writing all four parts routinely doubles the usefulness of the output, and the frame is short enough to use under time pressure.

The idea is simple: tell the AI who to be, what you know and need, exactly what to produce, and how the result should look. Each part answers a question the model would otherwise guess at.

## Learning Objectives

- Name the four parts of RCTF in order
- Write one complete RCTF prompt for your own subject
- Explain what each part prevents or improves
- Compare an RCTF prompt against your usual style

## The Four Parts

1. **Role** - who should the AI act as. 'Act as a patient tutor in econometrics'
2. **Context** - what does it need to know. 'I'm a first-year business student; I have 30 minutes; I'm confused about hypothesis testing'
3. **Task** - precisely what to produce. 'Explain it in 6 short bullets, then give me 2 practice questions with answers'
4. **Format** - how the output should look. 'No jargon without a definition; end with a 5-word summary'
### The frame in one line
- Role defines the voice; Context defines the constraints
- Task defines the deliverable; Format defines the structure

## Why Each Part Works

```text
What each part prevents:
1. Role prevents generic, lecture-bot tone
2. Context prevents wrong-level explanations
3. Task prevents vague, unactionable answers
4. Format prevents unusable structures
```
- Role such as 'patient tutor' biases the answer toward a useful voice
- Context like 'first-year business student' shapes the level and depth
- Task specificity is what makes the output usable in your workflow

## Write One Right Now

### The ninety-second drill
- Pick one concept from your current courses
- Write all four RCTF parts for a prompt about it
- Run it, then run your old-style prompt on the same concept
### Compare the two outputs
- Note the difference in level, structure, and usefulness
- That gap is the return on the ninety seconds you invested
- The frame becomes automatic with repetition

## Key Takeaways

- RCTF = Role, Context, Task, Format
- All four parts routinely double the usefulness of a prompt
- Role sets the voice; Context sets the level and constraints
- Task sets the deliverable; Format sets the structure
- The frame is fast to write and becomes automatic with practice

### Module 3 · The Research to Draft to Refine Chain

The best academic result does not come from a single magical prompt - it comes from a **chain** of prompts, each feeding the next. The professional loop is Research, then Draft, then Refine, and each step stays anchored to your topic instead of drifting.

This mirrors how real writers actually work: you draft, then you ask better questions of your material. One prompt cannot do discovery, structure, and polish at once; splitting the work is what makes each stage excellent.

## Learning Objectives

- Name the three stages of the research-draft-refine chain
- Explain why separate prompts outperform a single one
- Apply the chain to one of your real course topics
- Recognise how the chain mirrors professional writing practice

## The Three Stages

1. **Research:** 'List the 5 key debates in {topic} for a college essay, with the main authors on each side'
2. **Draft:** 'Using the above, write an outline with a thesis, 3 sections, and a conclusion'
3. **Refine:** 'Now rewrite section 2 for a sharper argument - tighten the logic and add a counter-argument'
### Why each stage is separate
- Research discovers the skeleton; draft builds on it; refine polishes it
- Each stage can be critiqued and redone without wrecking the others

## Staging Prevents Drift

```text
What chaining avoids:
1. Drifting off the question (single giant prompts wander)
2. Premature polish (refining before research is wasted effort)
3. Generic output (steps stay tied to your material)
4. Untraceable claims (each stage can be checked)
```
- Clean steps keep the AI anchored to your topic and argument
- Drift is the number one reason one-shot prompts fail at long tasks

## Becoming a Better Questioner

### The skill underneath the chain
- The chain trains you to ask increasingly specific questions of your material
- That is the same muscle real researchers and writers use
### Applying it this week
- Run the full chain once on a real course topic
- Save each stage's output and note how quality improved
- You become the editor-in-chief of your own research process

## Key Takeaways

- The chain is Research, then Draft, then Refine
- Each stage feeds the next, keeping the output on-topic
- Splitting stages avoids drift, premature polish, and generic output
- The chain mirrors how professional writers actually work
- Run the chain on a real topic this week and watch quality climb

### Module 4 · Controlling Outputs Like a Pro

The fastest path to usable output is making the AI obey constraints. Length, depth, style, and hard rules are all things you can specify - and the model follows explicit instructions far more reliably than it reads hints between the lines. This module turns vague preferences into hard requirements.

A special tool in this kit is the **failure instruction**: telling the model what to do when it is unsure. One line - 'if my question is ambiguous, ask for clarification before answering' - prevents whole categories of useless output.

## Learning Objectives

- Apply length, depth, style, and hard-rule constraints
- Write failure instructions that prevent useless output
- Explain why explicit constraints beat implied hints
- Use constraints to make AI output directly usable

## The Four Constraint Levers

- **Length:** 'in under 200 words', 'exactly 5 bullets', 'one paragraph'
- **Depth:** 'explain at first-year level', 'at master's level', 'as if to a 12-year-old'
- **Style:** 'neutral academic tone', 'persuasive but fair', 'business memo format'
- **Hard rules:** 'do not invent statistics', 'flag anything you are unsure about', 'only use the sources I provided'
### The rule that ties them
- Explicit length constraints are obeyed far more reliably than hints
- Each lever converts a soft preference into a testable requirement

## Failure Instructions

```text
The single line that saves you:
'If my question is ambiguous, ask for clarification before answering.'
----
Why it works:
1. It stops silent misinterpretation
2. It flips the model from guessing to asking
3. It protects every later part of the conversation
```
- Permission to ask instead of guess stops whole categories of bad output
- Combine it with sourcing rules for safer academic work

## Constraints in Your Real Work

### Turn rubrics into constraints
- A rubric's bullet points are already constraints - paste them into the prompt
- Word limits, citation styles, and banned phrases become hard rules
### Test the levers yourself
- Ask the same question at two different depth levels and compare
- Add one failure instruction and notice how the output changes
- Constraints turn a chat tool into a disciplined study assistant

## Key Takeaways

- Explicit constraints beat implied hints every time
- The four levers are length, depth, style, and hard rules
- Failure instructions prevent silent misinterpretation
- Rubrics are ready-made constraint sets for your prompts
- Test the levers side by side to see the difference yourself

### Module 5 · Build a Reusable Prompt Bank

Stop rewriting prompts from scratch. The professionals keep a personal library of prompts they reuse - one master prompt per recurring task, stored with a note on when to use it and versioned as it improves. This is your **prompt bank**, and it compounds.

The bank is also more than convenience: it is a portfolio of AI skill. Interviewers increasingly ask *how* you work with AI, and a documented, evolved collection of prompts is concrete, visible evidence that you work with AI deliberately.

## Learning Objectives

- Create one master prompt per recurring academic task
- Store prompts with a 'when to use this' header
- Version prompts when results improve
- Position the bank as evidence of AI skill

## What Goes in the Bank

- **Essay outline** - turn a brief into a structured, rubric-aligned outline
- **Literature search** - generate Boolean strings and discipline keywords
- **Flashcard generator** - turn a chapter into self-test cards
- **Code reviewer** - have a script or snippet reviewed for bugs and style
- **Self-quiz** - generate practice questions with answers
### One master prompt per task
- A master prompt is your best version, refined over time
- One polished, tested prompt beats ten half-written ones

## Store, Header, Version

```text
The bank entry template:
1. Header: 'When to use this' (one line)
2. The prompt itself, fully written
3. Output notes: what worked, what to tweak
4. Version history: v1, v2, v3 with dates
```
- Store entries in your notes app so they are searchable
- When a prompt gives a poor result, tweak it and save the improved copy
- A versioned bank compounds - each improvement is permanent

## The Bank Is Your AI Portfolio

### How interviewers see it
- Hiring panels ask how you work with AI, not just whether you use it
- A documented prompt process is concrete evidence of skill
### Make it visible and evolving
- Review your bank monthly and retire prompts that no longer work
- Add one prompt per new recurring task
- The bank grows with your courses and your career

## Key Takeaways

- A prompt bank holds one master prompt per recurring task
- Store each entry with a use-case header and version history
- Version prompts when results improve - the bank compounds
- The bank is a portfolio of AI skill for interviews
- Review it monthly; add one improvement per task per term

### Module 6 · The Self-Critique Loop (The 30-Second Upgrade)

Most bad AI output is a *first* output - and you can fix it in a single round. The self-critique loop is three quick moves: ask the AI to critique its own answer, ask it to repair the weak parts, then cross-check the result yourself. Thirty seconds of this routinely upgrades a C-quality reply into something worth handing in.

The loop matters because it turns every AI interaction into a learning moment. You are not just getting a better answer - you are practising the judgment of spotting weakness, which is precisely the skill that stays with you.

## Learning Objectives

- Run the three-step self-critique loop
- Explain what the critique step asks of the AI
- Explain why the final cross-check is non-negotiable
- Use the loop on an important prompt this week

## The Three Moves

1. **Critique it:** 'Rate your own answer on accuracy, clarity, and depth out of 10, then list the two weakest parts'
2. **Repair it:** 'Redo those two parts, using the sources I gave'
3. **Cross-check:** skim the final version yourself - you are still the expert in the room
### Why the order matters
- Critique finds the gaps; repair fixes them; you verify the result
- Skipping any step leaves the loop toothless

## Why It Works

```text
The mechanics of the loop:
1. A second pass always improves on the first draft
2. Asking for rated weaknesses forces evaluation, not repetition
3. Tying repair to your sources keeps it grounded
4. Your cross-check adds the judgment the AI lacks
```
- Small critique rounds deliver disproportionate quality gains
- The loop is cheap enough to run on every important prompt

## Make It a Habit

### Thirty seconds is the point
- The loop costs about thirty seconds - not a rewrite marathon
- Use it on every important prompt this week
### The habit stack
- Pair the loop with your prompt bank: save any before-and-after that helped
- Over time your first prompts improve, so the loop gets faster
- Judgment you practise now is judgment you keep forever

## Key Takeaways

- The loop is critique, repair, then cross-check yourself
- Rated weaknesses force evaluation instead of repetition
- Your final cross-check is non-negotiable - you are the expert
- Thirty seconds of self-critique routinely upgrades quality
- Run the loop on every important prompt until it is automatic

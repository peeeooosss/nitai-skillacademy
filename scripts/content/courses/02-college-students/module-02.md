# Mission 2: Prompt Engineering for Academics

## Learning Objectives
- Write structured prompts using a professional framework
- Chain prompts to research, draft, then refine academic work
- Control format, tone, depth, and audience in outputs
- Design one reusable prompt template per assignment type
- Critique and improve your own prompts systematically

---
### Module 1 · Why Prompting Is a Core Academic Skill
Tools don't hand you intelligence — they hand you drafts in response to a request. A vague question produces a vague answer; a precise prompt produces work you can actually use. That's why prompting is the single highest-leverage academic skill with AI.

Good prompts are the difference between:

- **"Explain AI"** (a 500-word ramble) and...
- **"Explain supervised vs unsupervised learning in 5 bullets, aimed at a second-year computing student, with one example each."** (instantly usable)

You already have the raw material for great prompts — your assignments, rubrics, and lecture notes. This mission teaches you to turn them into prompts on purpose.

### Module 2 · The 4-Part Professional Prompt Framework
Use the **RCTF** frame for academic prompts:

1. **Role** — who should the AI act as? *"Act as a patient tutor in econometrics."*
2. **Context** — what does it need to know? *"I'm a first-year business student; I have 30 minutes; I'm confused about hypothesis testing."*
3. **Task** — precisely what to produce? *"Explain it in 6 short bullets, then give me 2 practice questions with answers."*
4. **Format** — how should the output look? *"No jargon without a definition; end with a 5-word summary."*

Adding all four parts routinely doubles the usefulness of the output. Write one RCTF prompt for your own subject right now and compare it with your usual style.

### Module 3 · The Research → Draft → Refine Chain
The best academic result comes from a chain of prompts — not a single one:

1. **Research:** *"List the 5 key debates in {topic} for a college essay, with the main authors on each side."*
2. **Draft:** *"Using the above, write an outline with a thesis, 3 sections, and a conclusion."*
3. **Refine:** *"Now rewrite section 2 for a sharper argument — tighten the logic and add a counter-argument."*

Each step feeds the next, so the AI stays on your topic instead of drifting. This mirrors how real writers work: draft, then ask better questions of the material — not one magical prompt.

### Module 4 · Controlling Outputs Like a Pro
Make the AI obey constraints — it's the fastest way to usable output:

- **Length:** "in under 200 words", "exactly 5 bullets", "one paragraph".
- **Depth:** "explain at first-year level", "at master's level", "as if to a 12-year-old".
- **Style:** "neutral academic tone", "persuasive but fair", "business memo format".
- **Hard rules:** "do not invent statistics", "flag anything you're unsure about", "only use the sources I provided".

Add **failure instructions** too: *"If my question is ambiguous, ask for clarification before answering."* This single line prevents whole categories of useless output.

### Module 5 · Build a Reusable Prompt Bank
Stop rewriting prompts from scratch. Keep a personal library:

- Save **one master prompt** per recurring task: essay outline, literature search, flashcard generator, code reviewer, self-quiz.
- Store them in your notes app with a one-line "when to use this" header.
- Version them: when a prompt gives a poor result, tweak it and save the improved copy — the bank compounds.

Your prompt bank is also your **portfolio of AI skill** — interviewers increasingly ask *how* you work with AI. A documented, evolved bank is concrete evidence.

**Example Quiz**

### Module 6 · The Self-Critique Loop (The 30-Second Upgrade)
Most bad AI output is a *first* output — you can fix it in one round:

- **Critique it:** *"Rate your own answer on accuracy, clarity, and depth out of 10, then list the two weakest parts."*
- **Repair it:** *"Redo those two parts, using the sources I gave."*
- **Cross-check:** skim the final version yourself — you are still the expert in the room.

Thirty seconds of self-critique routinely upgrades a C-quality reply into something you'd be proud to hand in. Use it on every important prompt this week.

## Real-World Examples

- An economics student uses RCTF to turn one lecture slide into a one-page study summary every week.
- A psychology student builds a 'flashcards from textbook chapter' prompt she reuses across six courses.
- A finalist asks AI to critique her own essay outline, then sharpens her argument before writing.
- A group project team shares a prompt bank so every member drafts reports in a consistent structure.

## Key Takeaways
- Use **SCoPE**: System, Context, Objective, Parameters, Example.
- Chain **Research → Draft → Refine**; never one-shot the final product.
- Control tone, depth, structure, and format explicitly.
- Build a **prompt bank** of reusable academic templates.
- Iterate: 30 seconds of self-critique dramatically improves results.

## Practice Challenge

3. **Bank two templates:** Create a reusable "Essay" and "Literature summary" template in your notes, ready for your next two assignments.
4. **~30-second critique:** Pick an AI answer from today and improve it with one context addition — then show the before/after to a study partner.

### Module 1 · AI Is a Fast Assistant, Not Magic

AI tools are best understood not as magic but as a very fast, careful assistant who has read a great deal and follows instructions precisely. It does not think the way you do - it predicts the most useful next text based on your instructions and its training. Your job is simple: give clear instructions, check the output, and improve it. Nothing in this course needs a technical degree, only a real work task and a habit of checking.

The single principle that powers everything is this: **input quality decides output quality.** The better you describe your situation, your audience, the format you want, and your constraints, the more useful the answer becomes. A vague question produces a vague answer, not because the tool is weak, but because it had nothing specific to work with.

Keep one golden rule in mind for every interaction: **AI drafts; you decide.** AI can produce a draft in seconds, but the final result carries your name. That is why checking - not typing speed - is the real professional skill in this course.

## Learning Objectives

- Understand what AI actually is - a fast, instruction-following tool, not a thinking machine
- Apply the golden rule that you remain responsible for every final result
- Explain why the quality of your input decides the quality of the output
- Recognise when AI output needs a human check before it is used

## What AI Really Is

- AI predictions are based on patterns in the text it was trained on - there is no hidden intelligence or intent
- It has read enormous amounts of written material, so it is excellent at language, structure, and style
- It does not know your latest internal updates, yesterday's meeting, or this week's figures - you must provide that context
- It can sound confident even when wrong, which is exactly why the check step exists
- Treat it like a very capable but unsupervised junior: brilliant drafts, occasional confident mistakes

## The Golden Rule: You Decide

The professional standard is simple - the work that leaves your desk carries your judgment.
```text
You are my senior reviewer. Review this draft paragraph for
accuracy, tone, and missing detail before I send it to the
leadership team. List each issue and a suggested fix.
```
- You confirm facts, numbers, and names before anything is sent
- You choose the tone and the audience, not the tool
- You take credit for the wins - and responsibility for the output
- Nothing in this course changes that; AI only makes the drafting faster

## Three Habits of Fast Assistant Users

### Give clear instructions
- Describe the task, the audience, and the format you want in plain words
### Provide the context
- Paste or summarise the relevant background the model cannot know by itself
### Check before you use
- Skim for errors, missing detail, and anything that sounds too confident

## Key Takeaways

- AI is a fast, instruction-following assistant - not magic and not a mind reader
- Input quality decides output quality, so invest time in clearer prompts
- You are responsible for the final result, so always check before you use
- Provide context the model cannot know - your figures, your audience, your latest updates
- Confident-sounding output can still be wrong, which is why the check step matters

### Module 2 · The Four-Step Loop: Ask, Check, Improve, Save

Every activity in this course follows the same simple loop, and by the end of this mission it should feel automatic. The loop is: **Ask, Check, Improve, Save.** It turns a one-off chat with AI into a repeatable professional workflow, and it is the difference between using AI as a toy and using it as a working tool.

Most people only do step one - they ask, get an answer, and ship it raw. A professional runs all four steps every time. That small difference is where almost all of the quality gain in this course comes from.

## Learning Objectives

- Run the Ask, Check, Improve, Save loop fluently on a real task
- Explain why skipping the check step is the most dangerous shortcut
- Turn good prompts and outputs into a reusable personal toolkit

## The Four Steps in Practice

### Ask
- Write a clear prompt using the Context - Task - Format frame you will learn next
### Check
- Read the output for accuracy, tone, and missing detail before you use it
### Improve
- Ask for one specific revision, or fix the issue yourself in the draft
### Save
- Keep the good prompt and the good output where you can reuse them

## The Loop on a Real Example

Watch how the loop plays out on a routine task - a project status update.
```text
You are a project coordinator. Summarise this week's progress into a
status update for stakeholders. Include: 1) what finished, 2) what is
blocked and why, 3) the plan for next week. Keep it under 150 words.
```
- **Check:** does it mention the blocked item and next week's plan? Is the tone calm and concrete?
- **Improve:** ask for one change, e.g. "Make the next-week plan a numbered list".
- **Save:** keep the prompt - next week you swap in the new progress notes only

## Why All Four Steps Matter

- Skipping **Check** ships errors under your name - the most expensive shortcut in this course
- Skipping **Improve** accepts a mediocre first draft when one revision would fix it
- Skipping **Save** means redoing the work of writing the prompt next time
- Running all four takes sixty seconds longer and makes the output worth using at work

## Key Takeaways

- Ask, Check, Improve, Save is the daily loop behind every workflow in this course
- The check step protects your reputation - never ship an unchecked draft
- Improve with one specific instruction so you can see what changed
- Save good prompts and outputs - they compound into a personal toolkit
- Four steps take a minute longer and turn AI into a work tool rather than a toy

### Module 3 · The Context - Task - Format Frame

Most professional prompts fit one simple frame with three parts. Once you can write prompts this way, you can apply the same structure to emails, reports, summaries, and decks - almost everything else in this course is a variation on these three ingredients.

The frame is: **Context** (who you are, your audience, and the situation), **Task** (exactly what you want done), and **Format** (how to deliver it). Name all three and you will rarely get a useless answer again.

## Learning Objectives

- Write any prompt using the three-part Context - Task - Format frame
- Recognise which part of a weak prompt is missing and fix it
- Convert a vague everyday request into a frame that produces usable output

## The Three Parts, Named

- **Context** - who you are, your audience, and the situation. Example: "I am a finance analyst preparing a quarterly review for non-finance managers"
- **Task** - exactly what you want done. Example: "Summarise these 20 expense lines into the top 5 exceptions and what they mean"
- **Format** - how to deliver it. Example: "A one-page memo with a short paragraph and a table"
- Note that Context answers the questions a smart assistant would ask before starting

## A Weak Prompt, Fixed

The fastest way to learn the frame is to see it repair a bad request.
```text
Weak: Summarise these numbers for me.

Strong: I am a finance analyst preparing a quarterly review for
non-finance managers. Summarise these 20 expense lines into the top 5
exceptions and what each one means. Deliver as a one-page memo with a
short paragraph and a table.
```
- The weak version leaves the AI guessing who reads it, what matters, and what shape the answer should take
- The strong version names all three and gets usable output on the first try

## Applying the Frame to Any Task

### Writing
- Context: who reads it and why. Task: draft the message. Format: an email under 120 words
### Analysis
- Context: your role and the decision at stake. Task: identify patterns and risks. Format: findings plus a recommendation
### Learning
- Context: your current knowledge level. Task: explain the topic. Format: plain language with examples

## Key Takeaways

- Context - Task - Format is the master frame used across the whole course
- Context gives the AI your role, audience, and situation so the answer fits
- A precise Task prevents the vague, generic answers that waste a round-trip
- Naming the Format makes the output usable without extra edits
- Any weak prompt can be repaired by identifying the missing part of the frame

### Module 4 · First Workflow: Your Own Real Task

Theory is useful, but this is where you start building. Your first workflow is one small, real task from your own week - a routine email, a meeting summary, a short report section - run end to end through the loop.

This is deliberately small. A real, boring, repeatable task teaches the loop better than any impressive demo, and it produces something you can reuse tomorrow.

## Learning Objectives

- Choose a real task from your own week rather than a hypothetical example
- Run the full loop on it: prompt, check, one revision, save
- Explain why the task being real is what makes the workflow stick

## How to Pick the Right First Task

- Pick something you genuinely did this week, not a task you will never do again
- Choose a task that takes five to thirty minutes and repeats at least weekly
- Prefer something easily verifiable - a routine email or summary beats a complex judgement call
- Avoid the hardest or most sensitive task for your first attempt; build confidence first

## Running the Loop on It

Follow the four steps in order; the whole thing should take under fifteen minutes.
```text
1. Write a Context - Task - Format prompt for the task.
2. Get the draft and CHECK it: facts, tone, anything missing.
3. Ask for one revision: "Make it shorter and more practical."
4. SAVE the prompt and the final output to your toolkit.
```
- If the draft is already good, the revision step can be a light polish rather than a rewrite

## Why This Builds the Habit

- A real task comes back in your real week, so the loop gets repeated naturally
- You see the before/after value immediately - which is what motivates the next workflow
- You end the mission with one saved prompt, one real output, and one working habit

## Key Takeaways

- Pick a small real task from your own week - that makes the first workflow stick
- Small and verifiable beats impressive and complicated for a first attempt
- Run all four loop steps in order, even when the task feels simple
- One revision is enough - it teaches iteration habits without added complexity
- The real goal is a working habit, not a finished masterpiece

## Practice Challenge

### Module 5 · Where to Find Your First Hour Back

In every working day there is lost time hiding in small, repetitive tasks - drafting emails, summarising notes, structuring plans, reformatting documents. These are exactly what AI is best at, and they are the easiest place to create your first hour back.

You do not need a work-study to find them. You need one honest, boring day of noticing what feels mechanical - and a simple audit to capture it.

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the 3-5 mechanical tasks in your week that AI can offload
- Run a one-day audit to build your personal automation list
- Explain why a visible time saving is the fastest credibility win at work

## The 5-Minute Audit

- For one working day, note anything you did that took more than five minutes and felt mechanical
- Keep the list raw: drafting, copy-pasting, reformatting, summarising, updating trackers
- At day's end, group the entries into repeated task types - those are your automation candidates
- Most people find 3-5 such tasks in a single day; that is enough to start

## What Counts as 'Mechanical'

### Drafting
- Routine emails, status updates, and short notes you compose again and again
### Summarising
- Meeting notes, articles, long threads, and reports you condense by hand
### Reformatting
- Turning paragraphs into bullets, bullets into tables, notes into documents

## The Credibility Payoff

- Turning a half-hour routine into five minutes of checking and editing is visible and measurable
- Colleagues and managers notice concrete time savings on real work, not a list of tools
- Each saved task becomes evidence for the report you will build in Mission 6

## Key Takeaways

- Drafting, summarising, and reformatting are AI's sweet spot - and a daily time drain
- A one-day audit of mechanical tasks is the fastest way to build your automation list
- Most people find 3-5 repeatable candidates in a single working day
- A visible time saving - half an hour to five minutes - is the fastest credibility win
- Save the audit list; it becomes your roadmap for the rest of the course

## Practice Challenge

3. Add the list to your toolkit as your automation candidate list.

### Module 6 · Your Prompt Toolkit and Next Steps

Prompts that work are assets, exactly like a good spreadsheet template or a well-designed email signature. If you do not save them, you pay the cost of writing them again from scratch every single time.

So this mission ends with a small, real habit: a running file - or even a notebook - where every working prompt and every good output lives. The course will grow this file into a genuine personal toolkit.

## Learning Objectives

- Create and maintain a running personal prompt toolkit
- Save the reusable template from this mission in exactly reusable form
- Explain how the toolkit compounds across future missions

## Setting Up Your Toolkit

- Use any tool you already work in: a document, a note app, a simple text file
- Use one line per entry: the prompt, what it is for, and when it works best
- Save good outputs next to their prompts as reference examples
- Keep it reachable - the toolkit is useless if it is two clicks deep in your private folder

## Your First Toolkit Entry

The reusable template from this mission extends to almost every task in the course.
```text
Act as a [role] advising [audience]. Given [context], produce
[task]. Deliver as [format].
```
- Fill the blanks once per task; the structure stays the same
- Every time a prompt works at work, paste it here before you close the AI tool

## Where the Course Goes Next

- Mission 2 applies this loop to automating repetitive tasks safely
- Mission 3 sharpens the prompting skills behind better drafts
- Mission 4 builds full report, email, and presentation workflows
- Missions 5 and 6 go department-specific and measureable

## Key Takeaways

- Working prompts are reusable assets - save them like spreadsheet templates
- The mission template - role, audience, task, format - extends to nearly every task
- One line per entry: the prompt, its purpose, and when it works
- By mission's end you should have one saved prompt, one real output, and one habit
- The toolkit compounds: every later mission adds entries and makes work faster

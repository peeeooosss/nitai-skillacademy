### Module 1 · What 'Automate' Really Means Here

Automating with AI does not mean building full robot workflows or replacing your entire job. For most professionals it means a much simpler division of labour: **the AI does the drafting, formatting, and summarising; you do the deciding, checking, and sending.**

A realistic first step is a prompt you reuse. The same kind of email, summary, or update, produced from simple inputs each time, with you in control at the final step. Keep your expectations right: you are removing the tedious eighty percent, not your judgement.

## Learning Objectives

- Define automation for a professional as AI drafting while you decide and send
- Identify where your own judgement still sits in an automated workflow
- Explain why the professional who automates best still checks everything

## The Division of Labour

### AI handles
- First drafts of emails, summaries, updates, and documents
- Formatting, repurposing, and structure - the mechanical parts of writing
### You handle
- Deciding what is worth doing and what gets sent
- Checking facts, tone, and completeness before anything leaves
- Owning the final decision and the result

## What a Reusable Prompt Looks Like

The same task type, week after week, produced from simple inputs each time.
```text
AUTOMATION PATTERN
1. Keep one prompt per recurring task type (e.g. weekly status email).
2. Change only the situation and points in each new run.
3. Check and approve the output before it is sent.
4. Save the approved version next to the prompt.
```
- You are not building software pipelines - you are building prompt habits

## Setting Realistic Expectations

- You are removing the tedious 80%, not your judgement - checking is still your job
- A prompt that saves five minutes daily beats a complex pipeline that saves an hour monthly
- Automation grows one task type at a time; that is the sustainable path

## Key Takeaways

- Automate the drafting, formatting, and summarising - keep deciding and sending
- A reusable prompt for one recurring task type is a realistic first step
- Your judgement is the point of the workflow, not the thing being replaced
- Small daily savings compound into hours; start with one task type
- The professional who automates best is the one who still checks everything

### Module 2 · Safety Rules Before You Automate

Safety is not something to bolt on later - it is the first step of any automation. Before you paste anything into an AI tool, run the three rules in this module. They are not fear; they are what a professional does by default, and they protect both you and your organisation.

The rules are short: no sensitive data, no auto-sending, and check the policy. Two minutes of caution at the start saves you from problems that take weeks to undo.

## Learning Objectives

- Apply the three safety rules before any AI automation
- Classify which inputs must never be pasted into an AI tool
- Explain the 'no auto-sending' principle and why it protects quality and trust

## Rule 1 - No Sensitive Data

- Never paste passwords, account numbers, client financial details, medical records, or internal secrets into an AI tool
- Assume anything you paste is not private unless your company has approved the tool for it
- When in doubt, refer to your organisation's approved-tools list

## Rule 2 - No Auto-Sending

- Automate drafting - never blind sending. You approve before anything leaves your desk
- Auto-send turns a quality tool into a reputation risk in one click
- The approval step is where your judgement re-enters the workflow

## Rule 3 - Check the Policy

### Where to look
- Your company's AI policy or IT guidelines usually names allowed tools and data types
### Why it matters
- A two-minute check protects you from accidentally breaking rules you did not know existed
### When to ask
- If the policy is unclear about a specific data type, ask IT or your manager before proceeding

## Key Takeaways

- Apply the three safety rules before any automation - they are the professional default
- Sensitive data - passwords, financials, medical records, secrets - stays out of AI tools
- Automate drafting, never blind sending; you approve everything
- A two-minute policy check protects you and your work
- Safety rules are not bureaucracy - they are what makes automation safe to trust

3. Write the no-auto-sending rule in your own words under your toolkit safety page.

### Module 3 · Automating Routine Emails

Routine emails are the easiest first automation in any role, because they follow the same shape every time: an audience, a situation, and a few points to land. Once you have a reusable prompt for one email type, you will never write that email from a blank page again.

The pattern is simple: give the AI a role, name the audience, list the points, and set the tone. Each time you send that type of email, you change only the situation and the points - the structure stays.

## Learning Objectives

- Build a reusable prompt for one recurring email type in your role
- Identify which inputs change each time and which stay fixed
- Estimate the realistic time saving from reusing one email prompt

## The Reusable Email Pattern

```text
You are a [role]. Given this situation, write an email to [audience].
Include these points: [points]. Tone: [tone].
```
- The role sets the level of detail and formality
- The audience decides vocabulary, warmth, and what to lead with
- The tone word - direct, polite, brief - controls the feel of the message

## Common Email Types to Automate

### Status updates
- Progress, blockers, and next steps for stakeholders - same shape every week
### Follow-ups
- Gentle reminders that reference the last conversation and add one new value
### Introductions and hand-offs
- Structured notes that pass context cleanly from one person to the next

## Making It Stick

- Keep the prompt in your toolkit and open it when that email type recurs
- After sending, save your best output next to the prompt as a reference
- One well-reused email prompt can save about an hour a week - and the quality stays consistent

## Key Takeaways

- Routine emails follow the same shape every time - perfect for a reusable prompt
- The pattern: role, audience, situation, points, and tone
- You change only the situation and points; the structure stays fixed
- Keep prompts in your toolkit and open them when the email type recurs
- One well-reused email prompt can save roughly an hour a week

## Practice Challenge

2. Run it once on this week's inputs, check, and refine the prompt once.
3. Save the prompt and the approved output to your toolkit.

**Answer:** A — Situation + audience + points lets one prompt serve many similar emails.

2. What changes each time you reuse the email prompt?

- D) Everything

**Answer:** A — You swap in the new situation and points; the structure stays.

3. What is a realistic saving from one reused email prompt?

**Answer:** A — One well-reused prompt for a frequent email type can save about an hour a week.

### Module 4 · Automating Summaries and Notes

Summarising meetings, articles, and reports is where AI genuinely shines. Raw notes that would take half an hour to condense can become a structured summary in seconds - decisions, actions, open items - so a good summary turns a two-hour meeting into a two-minute read for everyone involved.

The discipline is the same as everywhere else: the AI produces the summary, and you check it against the source. AI can miss nuance, so the summary is a strong starting point, not the final word.

## Learning Objectives

- Build a structured summary prompt that extracts decisions, actions, and open items
- Explain why every summary must be checked against its source
- Turn meeting notes into a concise, useful brief in minutes

## The Structured Summary Prompt

Ask for the structure you actually need rather than a generic recount.
```text
Summarise the attached notes into: 1) the key decisions, 2) the open
actions with owners and dates, 3) what still needs discussion.
```
- Decisions give the meeting its point
- Actions with owners and dates make the summary actionable
- Open items keep the loop honest about what is unresolved

## Types of Summaries You Can Automate

### Meeting notes
- Decision lists, action owners, and open topics instead of a transcript
### Articles and reports
- Main argument, key evidence, and conclusions in under a page
### Long email threads
- The current status, open asks, and who is waiting on whom

## The Check Step for Summaries

- Skim the source alongside the summary before relying on it - especially names, numbers, and commitments
- Watch for tone shifts and emphasis the source would disagree with
- If a summary will be shared widely, read it once fully before handing it on

## Key Takeaways

- Summaries via AI shine - structured asks give instantly usable results
- Request decisions, actions with owners and dates, and open items
- A good summary turns a two-hour meeting into a two-minute read
- Always check the summary against the source - AI can miss nuance
- Meeting notes, articles, and long threads are all routine summary targets

## Practice Challenge

1. Take last week's messiest set of notes or emails and run the structured summary prompt.
2. Check the summary against the source and note any missed or misstated items.
3. Add the summary prompt to your toolkit with a note on when it works.

### Module 5 · Automating Formatting and Repurposing

A huge hidden time cost in every role is reformatting - turning a paragraph into bullets, bullets into a table, a report table into a narrative, or an internal note into a client-friendly version. None of this changes the underlying facts, and none of it needs real writing skill. That makes it the perfect job for AI.

The one rule that governs all of it: **reformatting must never change facts.** Tell the AI explicitly to keep facts identical, and then check that numbers and wording survived the trip.

## Learning Objectives

- Repurpose content between formats while keeping facts identical
- Use a single conversion prompt for paragraphs, bullets, tables, and narratives
- Check that numbers and wording survive every reformatting pass

## The Repurposing Workbench

### Paragraph to bullets
- Extract the key points of a dense paragraph into a scannable list
### Bullets to a table
- Turn a list of items into rows with columns you name
### Table to narrative
- Convert a data table into a short story for an email or briefing
### Internal to external
- Recast an internal note so a client reads it clearly, without jargon

## The Facts-Stay-Identical Rule

Every repurposing prompt should state the rule out loud.
```text
Convert the following into [format]. Keep the facts identical.
Do not add information. Do not change numbers, names, or meaning.
```
- Stating the rule changes behaviour - the AI keeps numbers and wording intact
- After conversion, spot-check numbers and key names against the original

## Where the Time Goes Missing

- Reformatting repeats daily across formats and audiences - it compounds quietly
- These tasks are the most delegation-friendly work in your week because they are mechanical
- Once a reformatting prompt is saved, the same job takes seconds instead of minutes

## Key Takeaways

- Reformatting is the largest hidden time cost for most professionals
- One rule governs it: keep the facts identical, never add information
- Repurposing spans paragraphs, bullets, tables, and audience changes
- State the facts rule in the prompt and check numbers survived
- Saved reformatting prompts turn a minutes-long job into a seconds-long one

## Practice Challenge

1. Take a report table you produced recently and convert it into a short narrative summary.
2. Take the same table and convert it into five bullet points.
3. Compare both outputs against the original and confirm the numbers survived.

**Example Quiz**

1. What is a strong rule when reformatting content?
- A) Keep the facts identical; never add information
- B) Improve the numbers a little
- C) Rewrite in a new language
- D) Summarise freely

**Answer:** A — Reformatting must keep facts identical - tell AI and check numbers survived.

2. Which of these is a valid repurposing task?
- A) Turning a report table into a narrative summary
- B) Deleting your drafts
- C) Changing account passwords
- D) Formatting a hard drive

**Answer:** A — Repurposing - table to narrative, bullets to slides - is a fast, real win.

### Module 6 · What NOT to Automate

Knowing what to automate is only half the skill. The other half is knowing what must never run on autopilot. Every role has a short list of tasks that are confidential, impossible to verify, or consequential enough that they must stay human-managed - and a professional recognises them on sight.

The professional line you will carry through this course is: **automate the tedious and reversible; keep the consequential and irreversible human.**

## Learning Objectives

- Classify tasks into safe-to-automate and must-stay-human categories
- Explain why confidential, unverifiable, and high-stakes tasks stay human
- Apply the reversible-versus-consequential test to any automation candidate

## What Stays on the Human Side

- **Anything confidential** - contracts, unannounced financials, personal data
- **Anything you cannot verify** - if you cannot check the output against a source, do not ship it blind
- **Anything with legal or reputational weight** - final external claims, regulatory filings, high-stakes communications
- **Decisions you must own** - firing, hiring shortlists, pricing; AI can help draft, but the decision is yours

## The Reversibility Test

Apply one question to every candidate: can a mistake here be undone cheaply?
### Automate
- Tedious, repeatable, easy to verify - like routine status emails and summaries
### Keep human
- Confidential, irreversible, or hard to verify - the consequential work
- When the test is unclear, err on the human side until you are sure

## Automation is Acceleration, Not Delegation

- You are not outsourcing decisions - you are speeding up the drafting around them
- The final judgement on anything that matters still happens in your head
- Colleagues and managers trust automation more when your oversight is obvious

## Key Takeaways

- Confidential, irreversible, and unverifiable tasks stay human-managed
- Safe to automate means tedious, repeatable, and easy to verify
- Decisions with real consequences - hiring, pricing - stay with people
- Use the reversibility test before adding any task to autopilot
- Automation accelerates the drafting; your judgement accelerates nothing - it decides

### Module 1 · Beat the Blank Page with an Outline

Staring at an empty document is expensive - it costs time, momentum, and confidence. The fastest fix is to ask AI for an outline first, not a draft. An outline gives you a skeleton to fill, cut, and reorder, and it turns writing from an act of creation into an act of editing.

The habit to carry forward: always outline before drafting. Outlines are cheap to change; drafts are expensive to throw away and rewrite.

## Learning Objectives

- Open any writing task with an outline request instead of a blank page
- Explain why outlines are cheaper to change than drafts
- Build an outline prompt with role, audience, topics, and structure

## The Outline Prompt

```text
Act as a [role]. Outline a [report/deck] for [audience] covering
[topics]. Structure it with sections and the key point of each.
```
- The sections give the document its architecture
- The key-point line keeps each section purposeful

## Why Outlines Win

- Outlines are short and structural - reordering costs a minute, not a rewrite
- Gaps show up early, when they are cheap to fix rather than buried in prose
- An approved outline means the draft writes itself into a pre-approved shape

## From Outline to Draft

### Fill
- Draft each approved section one at a time with its key point as a prompt
### Cut
- Remove anything that does not serve the outline - ruthlessly
### Reorder
- Move sections freely while they are still cheap to move

## Key Takeaways

- Start with an outline, not a draft - it is cheap to change and gives the skeleton
- Role, audience, topics, and structure produce a usable outline
- Outlining first turns writing into editing
- Gaps surface early, when fixing them is free
- An approved outline leads to a draft that fits the brief

## Practice Challenge

1. Take a document you must produce this week and write its outline prompt.
2. Approve the skeleton, then draft each section against its key point.
3. Save the outline prompt to your toolkit as a template.

**Example Quiz**

- A) An outline, not a draft
- B) A full report
- C) Another meeting
- D) Speakers' notes

**Answer:** A — Start with an outline - it is cheap to change and gives the skeleton.

2. Why are outlines better to change than drafts?
- A) They are short and structured
- B) They are final
- C) They hide gaps
- D) They are a type of AI app

**Answer:** A — Outlines are short and structural - reordering costs a minute, not a rewrite.

3. What does the outline prompt include?
- A) Role, audience, topics, structure
- B) Just a title
- C) Your diary
- D) The AI's model name

**Answer:** A — Role, audience, topics, and desired structure produce a usable skeleton.

### Module 2 · Emails That Get Read

A work email that does not get read - or gets replied to with confusion - costs far more than the minutes it took to write. The pattern in this module is built around one idea: make the reader's job trivial. Clear situation, one clear ask, tone that fits, and a length that respects the reader's time.

AI is brilliant at producing emails when you feed it these four ingredients. The result is an email that lands better, faster, every time.

## Learning Objectives

- Build email prompts from context, the ask, tone, and length
- Place the main ask early so the reader sees it fast
- Check a draft as its receiver before sending

## The Four Ingredients

### One-sentence context
- The situation in a single line - enough for the reader to place the email
### The ask
- What you need the reader to do - one clear verb
### Tone
- Direct, polite, urgent, brief - matched to your relationship
### Length
- 'No more than 120 words' keeps the email respectful of attention

## Put the Ask Early

Readers decide in the first lines whether to reply, defer, or delete.
- Tell the AI: "Put the main ask in the first line"
- A reader who misses the ask takes a meeting, then a follow-up, then a phone call
- The ask early plus the context around it covers both busy and careful readers

## The Receiver Check

- Read the draft as the person receiving it - would you reply?
- Would you understand the ask in five seconds?
- If not, tighten the ask line before sending - do not ask the AI again in this case

## Key Takeaways

- Context, the ask, tone, and length produce an email that gets read
- Put the main ask early - readers decide quickly whether to reply
- State length explicitly; attention is the reader's scarcest resource
- Match tone to your relationship, not to a rulebook
- Read the draft as the receiver; if the ask is unclear in five seconds, fix it

## Practice Challenge

1. Write the prompt for an email you need to send this week.
2. Run it and place the ask in the first line.
3. Apply the receiver check before sending, and save the prompt.

**Example Quiz**

1. What are the core parts of an email prompt?
- A) Context, the ask, tone, length
- B) Greeting, signoff, date
- C) Salutation, font, colour
- D) Subject, attachment, CC

**Answer:** A — Context plus the ask, tone, and length produces an email that gets read.

2. Where should the main ask land?
- A) Early, so the reader sees it fast
- B) Hidden in the last paragraph
- C) In the signature
- D) Nowhere

**Answer:** A — Put the ask early and clearly - readers decide quickly whether to reply.

3. How 'professional' should a work email be?
- A) Match the audience - direct and respectful
- B) Always formal
- C) Always casual
- D) Ignore tone

**Answer:** A — Tone should fit your audience and relationship - not everything is formal.

### Module 3 · Reports from Messy Inputs

Reports usually start as messy notes, spreadsheets, and chat threads - not as clean data. That mess is exactly where AI earns its keep: you hand over the raw material plus a structure, and the AI organises the patterns into a report shape you can check and finalise.

The one discipline that never changes: AI organises the narrative, your systems stay the source of truth for the numbers, and you verify before anything circulates.

## Learning Objectives

- Feed messy inputs into a structured report prompt
- Request a report with summary, metrics, drivers, and risks-and-actions sections
- Verify figures against the source and ask AI to flag what it cannot check

## The Report Prompt

```text
Here are the inputs for the monthly report. Produce: 1) a two-line
executive summary, 2) key metrics in a table, 3) three things that
moved this month and why, 4) risks and actions.
```
- The executive summary gives leadership the headline in two lines
- The metrics table keeps the numbers scannable
- Drivers and risks-and-actions make the report about decisions, not data

## Warning the AI About Numbers

- Ask the AI to flag anything it cannot verify rather than guess
- Fictitious but confident numbers are the report writer's worst failure mode
- A flag is always cheaper than an audit after publication

## Your Verification Pass

### Check the drivers
- Do the three 'moved' items match the metrics you can see?
### Check the table
- Spot-check figures against the source, especially totals and percentages
### Check the risks
- Are the risks real, owned, and dated - or generic filler?

## Key Takeaways

- Structure - summary, metrics, drivers, risks-and-actions - makes a report usable
- AI organises the narrative; your systems stay the source of truth for numbers
- Ask the AI to flag anything it cannot verify instead of guessing
- Spot-check figures against the source before anything circulates
- Messy inputs plus a clear structure beats clean inputs plus a vague ask

## Practice Challenge

1. Take a report you must produce and gather its raw inputs into one document.
2. Run the structured report prompt and review each of the four sections.
3. Verify three numbers from the metrics table against your source.

**Example Quiz**

1. What does a good report prompt ask for?
- A) A summary, key metrics, what moved and why, risks and actions
- B) More raw data
- C) A PowerPoint animation
- D) A longer report

**Answer:** A — Structure - summary, metrics, drivers, risks and actions - makes the report usable.

2. What must you do with numbers before sending a report?
- A) Verify them against the source
- B) Round them up
- C) Delete them
- D) Trust the AI blindly

**Answer:** A — Always verify figures - AI can misread a table or miss an update.

3. How should the AI handle uncertain numbers?
- A) Flag them for your review
- B) Make them up
- C) Hide them
- D) Delete the row

**Answer:** A — Ask AI to flag anything it cannot verify - you close the loop.

### Module 4 · Presentations That Flow

Bad decks fail in the storyline, not the slide design. Most presentation time is wasted on colours and animations while the story meanders. The professional move is to plan the storyline first - every slide carries one headline finding, one visual idea, and one takeaway.

Slides are a visual outline. Generate the storyline before any text or design work touches the page.

## Learning Objectives

- Plan a deck as a storyline before working on any slide
- Keep every slide to one headline finding, one visual idea, one takeaway
- Test and tighten a deck outline from the audience's side

## The Storyline Prompt

```text
Act as a [role]. Plan a 10-slide deck for [audience] on [topic].
Each slide: one headline finding + one visual or table idea + one
takeaway.
```
- One headline per slide keeps the deck scannable
- The visual idea prevents a wall-of-text slide before you write it

## What Makes a Slide Work

- One finding per slide - if a slide has three ideas, split it
- A visual or table that carries the evidence
- One takeaway the audience should act on or remember
- Forty words per slide is a warning sign, not a target

## Checking the Flow

- After the outline, read it like an audience member, start to finish
- Does each slide advance the story, or repeat the previous one?
- Cut anything that does not advance - a deck is a story, not an archive

## Key Takeaways

- Plan the storyline first - each slide is one headline finding
- One finding, one visual, one takeaway - that is a scannable slide
- The outline comes before design; flow beats decoration
- Read the deck like an audience and cut what does not advance the story
- A 10-slide storyline approved in one review beats a dense deck in three

## Practice Challenge

1. Take a deck you must present and run the storyline prompt.
2. Rewrite every slide's headline as a single finding.
3. Cut or merge any slide that does not advance the story.

**Example Quiz**

1. What is the right first step for a deck?
- A) Plan the storyline and slide headlines
- B) Design the colours
- C) Add animations
- D) Write 40 words per slide

**Answer:** A — Plan the storyline first - each slide is one headline finding.

2. What belongs on a well-designed slide?
- A) One headline finding plus one visual idea and one takeaway
- B) A full paragraph
- C) Every metric you have
- D) A thank-you slide

**Answer:** A — One finding, one visual, one takeaway - that is a scannable slide.

3. What should you do after outlining the deck?
- A) Read it like an audience and cut anything that does not advance the story
- B) Add more bullets
- C) Send it immediately
- D) Start over

**Answer:** A — Test flow from the audience side and cut, reorder, or tighten.

### Module 5 · Tone and Language for Your Audience

The same content changes completely with its audience. Leadership wants decisions and risks; a technical team wants methods and numbers; customers want benefits in their words. None of this is guessable from the topic alone - which is exactly why most generic outputs miss the mark.

The fix: state the audience explicitly, and let the AI adjust tone, depth, and emphasis for you. When you are unsure of tone, ask for two versions and choose.

## Learning Objectives

- State the audience explicitly in every prompt
- Match depth and emphasis to the audience's real needs
- Use the two-versions trick to settle tone quickly

## Four Audiences, Four Shapes

### Non-technical leadership
- Decisions, risks, costs, and next steps - not methods
### Technical team
- Methods, numbers, edge cases - not persuasion
### Customers
- Benefits in their words, no jargon, no internals
### Senior officials
- One page, evidence-backed, formal, decision-ready

## How the Audience Changes the Same Facts

- The same budget story becomes a cost warning for leadership and a line-item breakdown for finance
- The same product feature becomes a capability for engineers and a benefit for customers
- Claiming the audience out loud stops the AI from guessing a default register

## When in Doubt, Generate Two Versions

- Ask for a formal and a conversational version of the same content
- Compare them side by side and pick what fits your relationship
- This two-minute trick settles tone debates that meetings fail to resolve

## Key Takeaways

- Audience changes tone, depth, and emphasis - state it in the prompt
- Leadership wants decisions, risks, costs, and next steps
- Customers want benefits in their words, free of jargon
- Claiming the audience stops the AI from guessing a default register
- Generate two tone versions when unsure - then choose fast

## Practice Challenge

1. Take one piece of content and run it for two very different audiences.
2. Compare what the AI changed in tone, depth, and emphasis.
3. Save the audience line pattern to your toolkit.

**Example Quiz**

1. How should the same content differ by audience?
- A) Tone, depth, and what is emphasised change
- B) It should stay identical
- C) Only the font changes
- D) Only the title changes

**Answer:** A — Audience changes tone, depth, and emphasis - state it in the prompt.

2. What should leadership content emphasise?
- A) Decisions, risks, costs, next steps
- B) Every code detail
- C) Your typing speed
- D) Long sentences

**Answer:** A — Leadership audiences want decisions, risks, costs, and next steps.

3. What is a good trick when unsure of tone?
- A) Ask for formal and conversational versions
- B) Guess and ship
- C) Email both versions to everyone
- D) Use a dictionary

**Answer:** A — Generate two tone versions and choose - fast and low risk.

### Module 6 · One-Session Report + Deck Workflow

Reports and decks usually share the same source material, yet most people build them as two separate painful projects. This module combines them into one focused session with a shared outline - and the second time you run it, it takes a fraction of the effort because your prompts become your templates.

## Learning Objectives

- Run a report-and-deck production workflow from a shared outline
- Structure the six steps: outline, draft, build, tune, verify, save
- Explain why saved prompts make the next cycle dramatically faster

## The Six-Step Session

### 1. Outline
- Build the outline for report and deck together - one shared skeleton
### 2. Draft
- Draft the report structure from your messy inputs
### 3. Build
- Build the deck as a visual summary of the report
### 4. Tune
- Match tone and length to the audience
### 5. Verify
- Check numbers and decisions against sources
### 6. Save
- Save the prompts you used - they are next month's templates

## Why the Shared Outline Matters

- One skeleton means the report and deck cannot contradict each other
- Approving the outline once removes a full round of rework at the end
- The deck becomes a by-product of the report instead of a second project

## The Compounding Effect

- The second run of this session is faster than the first, because only inputs change
- The third run is faster still, because the prompts are tuned to your formats
- After a few cycles, the session is a routine rather than a project

## Key Takeaways

- A shared outline keeps report and deck consistent and halves the work
- Six steps: outline, draft, build, tune, verify, save
- Saved prompts make the next report-and-deck cycle far faster
- The deck becomes a by-product of the report, not a second project
- Repeatable sessions beat heroic single efforts - templates compound

## Practice Challenge

1. Run the six-step session on a real report-and-deck pair this week.
2. Save every prompt from the session into your toolkit.
3. Note what you would shave next time the session runs.

**Example Quiz**

1. What is the biggest advantage of a repeatable workflow?
- A) The second time takes far less effort
- B) It never needs checking
- C) It replaces your manager
- D) It auto-sends to everyone

**Answer:** A — Saved prompts make the next report-and-deck cycle far faster.

2. What should come first in the workflow?
- A) An outline shared by report and deck
- B) Slide colours
- C) The signature block
- D) The filename

**Answer:** A — A shared outline first - then report, deck, tone, verify.

3. What should you save at the end?
- A) The prompts you used
- B) The raw data
- C) Only the email
- D) The passwords

**Answer:** A — Save the prompts - they become your repeatable report-and-deck templates.

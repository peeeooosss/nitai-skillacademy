# Mission 4: AI Reports, Emails & Presentations

## Learning Objectives

- Use AI to beat the blank page and start with a strong outline
- Draft emails that get read: clear subject, one ask, right tone
- Turn messy data into reports with structure and a summary
- Build presentation outlines that actually flow
- Match tone and language to your real audience
- Produce a complete report-and-deck product in one session

---
### Module 1 · Beat the Blank Page with an Outline

Staring at an empty document is expensive. The fastest fix is to ask AI for an outline first - not a draft, an outline:

'Act as a [role]. Outline a [report/deck] for [audience] covering [topics]. Structure it with sections and the key point of each.'

An outline gives you a skeleton. You then fill, cut, and reorder - and the writing feels like editing, not creation.

**The habit:** always outline before drafting. Outlines are cheap to change; drafts are not.

**Example Quiz**

1. What should you ask for first to beat the blank page?
- A) An outline, not a draft
- B) A full report
- C) Another meeting
- D) Speakers' notes

**Answer:** A — Start with an outline - it is cheap to change and gives the skeleton.

2. Why are outlines better to change than drafts?
- A) They are short and structured
- B) They are final
- C) They hide gaps
- D) They are a type of AI app

**Answer:** A — Outlines are short and structural - reordering costs a minute, not a rewrite.

3. What does the outline prompt include?
- A) Role, audience, topics, structure
- B) Just a title
- C) Your diary
- D) The AI's model name

**Answer:** A — Role, audience, topics, and desired structure produce a usable skeleton.

### Module 2 · Emails That Get Read

The pattern for a good work email prompt:

1. **One-sentence context** - the situation.
2. **The ask** - what you need the reader to do.
3. **Tone** - direct, polite, urgent, brief.
4. **Length** - 'no more than 120 words'.

Then say: 'Put the main ask in the first line' - so the reader's eye lands on it.

**Check:** read it as the receiver. Would you reply? Would you understand the ask in five seconds?

**Example Quiz**

1. What are the core parts of an email prompt?
- A) Context, the ask, tone, length
- B) Greeting, signoff, date
- C) Salutation, font, colour
- D) Subject, attachment, CC

**Answer:** A — Context + the ask + tone + length produces an email that gets read.

2. Where should the main ask land?
- A) Early, so the reader sees it fast
- B) Hidden in the last paragraph
- C) In the signature
- D) Nowhere

**Answer:** A — Put the ask early and clearly - readers decide quickly whether to reply.

3. How 'professional' should a work email be?
- A) Match the audience - direct and respectful
- B) Always formal
- C) Always casual
- D) Ignore tone

**Answer:** A — Tone should fit your audience and relationship - not everything is formal.

### Module 3 · Reports from Messy Inputs

Reports usually start as messy notes, spreadsheets, and chats. Give AI the raw material plus a structure:

'Here are the inputs for the monthly report. Produce: 1) a two-line executive summary, 2) key metrics in a table, 3) three things that moved this month and why, 4) risks and actions.'

AI finds the patterns in the mess and organises them. Then you - still - check numbers against the source.

**Warn the AI before numbers:** tell it to flag anything it cannot verify.

**Example Quiz**

1. What does a good report prompt ask for?
- A) A summary, key metrics in a table, what moved and why, risks and actions
- B) More raw data
- C) A PowerPoint animation
- D) A longer report

**Answer:** A — Structure - summary, metrics, drivers, risks/actions - makes the report usable.

2. What must you do with numbers before sending a report?
- A) Verify them against the source
- B) Round them up
- C) Delete them
- D) Trust the AI blindly

**Answer:** A — Always verify figures - AI can misread a table or miss an update.

3. How should the AI handle uncertain numbers?
- A) Flag them for your review
- B) Make them up
- C) Hide them
- D) Delete the row

**Answer:** A — Ask AI to flag anything it cannot verify - you close the loop.

### Module 4 · Presentations That Flow

Slides are a visual outline. Generate the storyline first:

'Act as a [role]. Plan a 10-slide deck for [audience] on [topic]. Each slide: one headline finding + one visual or table idea + one takeaway.'

One headline per slide keeps the deck scannable. Then produce the supporting bullet text per slide.

**Check flow:** after the outline, read it like an audience. Does each slide advance the story? Cut anything that does not.

**Example Quiz**

1. What is the right first step for a deck?
- A) Plan the storyline and slide headlines
- B) Design the colours
- C) Add animations
- D) Write 40 words per slide

**Answer:** A — Plan the storyline first - each slide is one headline finding.

2. What belongs on a well-designed slide?
- A) One headline finding + one visual idea + one takeaway
- B) A full paragraph
- C) Every metric you have
- D) A thank-you slide

**Answer:** A — One finding, one visual, one takeaway - that is a scannable slide.

3. What should you do after outlining the deck?
- A) Read it like an audience and cut anything that does not advance the story
- B) Add more bullets
- C) Send it immediately
- D) Start over

**Answer:** A — Test flow from the audience side and cut, reorder, or tighten.

### Module 5 · Tone and Language for Your Audience

The same content changes completely with audience. Tell the AI the audience explicitly:

- **Non-technical leadership** - decisions, risks, costs, next steps.
- **Technical team** - methods, numbers, edge cases.
- **Customers** - benefits in their words, no jargon.
- **Senior officials** - one-page, evidence-backed, formal.

Add audience to every prompt and the words, depth, and length change automatically.

**Rule:** when in doubt about tone, ask the AI for two versions - formal and conversational - and pick.

**Example Quiz**

1. How should the same content differ by audience?
- A) Tone, depth, and what is emphasised change
- B) It should stay identical
- C) Only the font changes
- D) Only the title changes

**Answer:** A — Audience changes tone, depth, and emphasis - state it in the prompt.

2. What should leadership content emphasise?
- A) Decisions, risks, costs, next steps
- B) Every code detail
- C) Your typing speed
- D) Long sentences

**Answer:** A — Leadership audiences want decisions, risks, costs, and next steps.

3. What is a good trick when unsure of tone?
- A) Ask for formal and conversational versions
- B) Guess and ship
- C) Email both versions to everyone
- D) Use a dictionary

**Answer:** A — Generate two tone versions and choose - fast and low risk.

### Module 6 · One-Session Report + Deck Workflow

Combine everything into a full workflow you can run in one focused session:

1. **Outline** the report and deck together (share the skeleton).
2. **Draft** the report structure from your messy inputs.
3. **Build** the deck as a visual summary of the report.
4. **Tune** tone and length for the audience.
5. **Verify** numbers and decisions against sources.
6. **Save** the prompts you used for next month.

The second time this takes a fraction of the effort - your prompts become your templates.

**Example Quiz**

1. What is the biggest advantage of a repeatable workflow?
- A) The second time takes far less effort
- B) It never needs checking
- C) It replaces your manager
- D) It auto-sends to everyone

**Answer:** A — Saved prompts make the next report/deck cycle far faster.

2. What should come first in the workflow?
- A) An outline shared by report and deck
- B) Slide colours
- C) The signature block
- D) The filename

**Answer:** A — A shared outline first - then report, deck, tone, verify.

3. What should you save at the end?
- A) The prompts you used
- B) The raw data
- C) Only the email
- D) The passwords

**Answer:** A — Save the prompts - they become your repeatable report/deck templates.

## Real-World Examples

**The monthly report rebuild —** A manager feeding messy notes, a dashboard export, and meeting chats into one structured prompt received an executive summary, a metrics table, three drivers, and risks and actions - verified against the dashboard in twenty minutes.
**The deck that finally flowed —** Instead of forty dense slides, a 10-slide storyline with one heading and one takeaway per slide got approved in one review round, where earlier versions took three.
**The two-tone email —** Stuck between formal and direct, a professional asked for both versions from the same prompt, picked the conversational one, and sent it - faster than agonising over tone.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Outline before you write - outlines are cheap to change, drafts are not.
- Put the main ask early in emails and state tone and length.
- Structure report prompts: summary, metrics table, what moved and why, risks and actions.
- Plan decks as storyline + one headline per slide; cut what does not advance the story.
- State the audience; tone, depth, and emphasis follow automatically.
- Save every prompt from the session - they become your templates.

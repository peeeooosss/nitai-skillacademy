### Module 1 · Why Resumes Fail Before a Human Reads Them

Recruiters and Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) skim in seconds, so your resume fails silently unless it is built for their grammar. A resume that looks fine to your eyes can be invisible to a machine and unreadable to a stressed recruiter. The fix starts with understanding the three qualities that matter: **scannable**, **relevant**, and **quantified**.

Most resumes are written as a list of duties - 'Handled customer complaints.' That tells no one how well you did it. ATS software scans for keywords, measurable outcomes, and structural clarity, and a recruiter scans the same three things in a six-second glance. Miss any of them and you are not rejected - you are simply not seen.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why resumes fail before a human ever reads them
- Define the three qualities: scannable, relevant, quantified
- Describe the six-second test a recruiter runs
- Understand what ATS systems scan and why

## The Three Qualities That Survive

- **Scannable:** structure no machine fights and a human can follow in seconds
- **Relevant:** keywords a recruiter for the role actually expects
- **Quantified:** achievements with numbers, not a list of duties
### The invisible failure
- A resume can be perfect English and still fail - if it lacks these three qualities
- The failure is silent: no rejection reason, just no response
- That silence is what the three qualities exist to prevent

## The Six-Second Test

- A recruiter glances for about six seconds before deciding to read or discard
- In those seconds they scan: name and contact, current role, one or two measurable achievements, and clean formatting
- If none of that stands out, it is in the reject pile - politely deleted
```text
What the six-second glance checks, in order:
1. Name + contact info (can I reach them?)
2. Current role / headline (what do they do now?)
3. One or two quantified achievements (did they deliver?)
4. Clean formatting (is this professional?)
5. If three of four fail, this resume is discarded
```
- Use the test on your own resume before you submit it

## What ATS Actually Scans

- **Keywords:** the exact terms from the job posting - mirror them honestly
- **Measurable outcomes:** numbers and metrics that prove impact
- **Structural clarity:** standard headings like Experience, Education, Skills
- AI can speed up the fix, but you must steer it with truth
### The honesty guardrail
- A polished resume that exaggerates will fail in the interview and damage your reputation
- Every number you use must survive the question 'show me how you got that'
- Polish the truth - do not polish over a fabrication

## Key Takeaways

- Resumes fail silently unless scannable, relevant, and quantified
- The six-second test decides read or discard
- ATS scans keywords, measurable outcomes, and clear structure
- AI speeds up the fix, but you must steer it with truth
- Every claim must survive 'show me how you got that number'

### Module 2 · The PAR Method for Strong Bullets

Every resume bullet should follow the PAR structure: **Problem, Action, Result**. It is the difference between telling a recruiter you had a job and proving you could deliver one. A duty bullet describes activities; a PAR bullet demonstrates impact - and impact is what gets interviews.

PAR is simple to learn and transforms even ordinary experience. The trick is practise: take your raw duties, run them through the format, and verify every number is real before the bullet ever touches your resume.

## Learning Objectives

- Define the three elements of PAR: Problem, Action, Result
- Rewrite weak duty bullets as strong PAR bullets
- Use an AI prompt to transform resume bullets under 18 words
- Apply the honesty rule: never let AI invent numbers

## The PAR Structure

- **P - Problem:** the challenge you faced, briefly
- **A - Action:** what you did to address it - include AI when it was genuinely part of the work
- **R - Result:** the measured outcome, with a number where possible
### Weak to strong in one line
- Weak (duty): 'Handled customer complaints.'
- Strong (PAR): 'Resolved 40+ complex escalations per quarter, cutting repeat calls 25% with a 5-step triage checklist.'
- Notice the second line tells a story the first one never could

## Transforming Your Bullets

- Another example: 'Responsible for social media' becomes 'Grew LinkedIn engagement 180% in 3 months by posting AI-drafted content, then manually editing for brand voice'
- The AI-true version shows both the tool and your human control of it
- Keep bullets under 18 words where you can - density wins
```text
Rewrite each of my raw duties as PAR bullets. Keep them honest,
specific, and under 18 words. Quantify where I gave real numbers;
leave a [ ] placeholder where I must verify or fill in a metric.
```
- Feed AI your real job duties - never a fabricated history
- The placeholders are your to-do list: every [ ] becomes a verified number

## The Numbers Rule

- Never let AI invent numbers - every claim must survive the question 'show me how you got that number'
- If a number cannot be shown, remove it - a smaller true number beats a big fake one
- Real numbers you can defend: 'resolved 40+ escalations', 'cut repeat calls 25%', 'grew engagement 180%'
### How to gather real numbers
- Check dashboards, reports, and old emails for figures you can verify
- Ask former colleagues for a corroborating detail
- Use approximations honestly: 'about 40 per quarter' is fine - a precise fake is not

## Key Takeaways

- PAR = Problem, Action, Result - every bullet in that shape
- Duty bullets tell activities; PAR bullets prove impact
- Use AI to transform, but verify every number it suggests
- Keep bullets under 18 words with a measured outcome
- A claim must survive 'show me how you got that number'

## Practice Challenge

1. Take your 3 weakest current bullets and rewrite them as PAR
2. Run the transform prompt on your duties and fill every [ ] placeholder with a real number
3. Cross-check each number: can you prove it in one sentence?

### Module 3 · The 3-Pass AI Resume Workshop

A single pass rarely produces a great resume. Three passes with AI - match, quantify, tailor - create a resume that is honest, targeted, and read-ready. Each pass has a specific job, and the order matters: target first, then harshen the language, then customise the final version for each application.

This workshop assumes one thing: your real experience is the raw material. AI never adds experience; it only sharpens the presentation of what already happened. Keep that boundary and the workshop produces a resume you can defend in any interview.

## Learning Objectives

- Run Pass 1: match the resume to your target role
- Run Pass 2: quantify achievements and add keywords
- Run Pass 3: tailor the resume per application
- Apply non-negotiable honesty guardrails throughout

## Pass 1: Match to Target Role

- Paste your resume plus your Mission 1 gap list into AI
- Ask it to rewrite toward your target role, inferring the 10 skills that role wants
- Flag every place it makes a stronger claim so you can verify - the flagging is the guardrail
### The inference risk
- AI will happily infer skills you do not have - that is what 'flag' is for
- Only claims you can verify survive into the final resume
- The gap list keeps the rewrite aligned with your actual upskilling plan

## Pass 2: Quantify and Add Keywords

- Add ATS keywords from the job post; improve action verbs; kill weak language like 'responsible for' and 'helped with'
- Strong action verbs: delivered, launched, reduced, resolved, built, automated
- Every bullet ends with a number or a measurable outcome where one exists
### Keyword discipline
- Keyword-stuffing skills you cannot discuss in an interview is a self-own
- Each keyword you add must have matching proof behind it
- The goal is accurate density, not volume

## Pass 3: Tailor Per Application

- For each serious application, feed the job description and adjust the resume
- One size never fits - the best resumes are customised to the role
- Change the summary, reorder strengths, and match the exact terminology of the post
```text
The honesty guardrails - non-negotiable:
1. Never let AI invent numbers, titles, companies, dates, or skills
2. If AI uses stronger language, verify it is still true first
3. No keyword-stuffing skills you cannot answer in an interview
4. You may note 'I use AI-assisted workflows' if it is true - it is valued
```
- Pin this list next to your keyboard while you work

## Key Takeaways

- Three passes: match to role, quantify and add keywords, tailor per application
- Pass 1 infers what a role wants - but flags every stronger claim
- Strong action verbs and verified keywords win the ATS screen
- Pass 3 tailors each application - one size never fits
- Guardrails: never invent facts; honest AI-use notes are valued

## Practice Challenge

1. Run Pass 1 with your gap list and flag every claim AI strengthened
2. Run Pass 2 - replace 3 weak verbs with strong ones and add 2 verified keywords
3. Run Pass 3 on a real posting - produce one tailored resume version

**Example Quiz**

1. What is Pass 1 of the 3-pass workshop?
- A) Match resume to target role
- B) Quantify keywords
- C) Tailor per application
- D) Proofread for typos

**Answer:** A — Pass 1 matches the resume to the target role using your gap list and real experience.

2. What does Pass 3 add for each serious application?
- A) Tailoring to that specific job description
- B) A new font
- C) A second page
- D) More colours

**Answer:** A — Pass 3 tailors the resume to each application - one size never fits.

3. Is listing 'AI-assisted workflows' on a resume allowed?
- A) Yes, if it is true - it is increasingly valued
- B) Never
- C) Only for engineers

**Answer:** A — You can note honest AI use in skills - it is increasingly valued, not penalised.

4. What should AI flag during Pass 1?
- A) Every place it made a stronger claim you must verify
- B) Only typos
- C) Your fonts
- D) Your age

**Answer:** A — Flagging stronger claims is the guardrail that keeps the rewrite honest.

### Module 4 · Building a Resume That Compiles

A resume needs to compile for two different readers: a stressed human and an ATS parser. The good news is that both want the same thing - clean, standard structure with standard headings and simple formatting. If you design for the ATS, you automatically design for the human, because both struggle with the same layouts.

This module walks through the skeleton of a resume that compiles everywhere: header, summary, skills, experience, education, and extras - with length rules and formatting choices that keep parsers happy.

## Learning Objectives

- Build a standard six-section resume skeleton
- Apply the one-page / two-page length rules
- Choose ATS-friendly formatting and PDF output
- Test a resume with the plain-text readability check

## The Six-Section Skeleton

- **Header:** name, phone, email, city, LinkedIn, portfolio/GitHub - no photos in most markets
- **Summary (3 lines):** your story sentence from Mission 1 plus 3 value points with measurable outcomes
- **Skills:** 6-10 grouped skills (tech/tools/soft), keywords from the target posting here
- **Experience:** reverse-chronological, PAR bullets, 4-6 per role, each with a number or measurable outcome
- **Education + Certifications + Projects** at the end
### Why the order matters
- Header, summary, skills answer the six-second glance in under 3 seconds
- Experience proves what the summary promised
- Education and certifications support, not lead, unless you are a fresher

## Length and Formatting Rules

- **Length:** one page for junior levels; two pages maximum otherwise
- **Headings:** standard terms - Experience, Education, Skills - parsers recognise them
- **Avoid:** tables, columns, graphics, and photos - parsers struggle with them all
- **Format:** save as PDF unless told otherwise - PDF preserves your layout
```text
The plain-text compile test:
1. Copy your entire resume text
2. Paste into Notepad or Notes (plain text)
3. Read it from top to bottom
4. Clean headings, readable bullets, logical order? -> it compiles
5. Columns collapsed into gibberish? -> simplify the layout
```
- Run this test before every submit - it takes one minute

## The Fresher vs Experience Trade-Off

- Freshers lead with education and projects; experienced candidates lead with achievements
- Either way, the summary must state the story - never let a recruiter hunt for it
- Projects on a fresher resume replace the experience section - make them quantified
### The one universal truth
- Every bullet should carry a number or a measurable outcome
- 'Built a dashboard' becomes 'Built a KPI dashboard used by 3 teams daily'
- Numbers are the universal language resumes compile in

## Key Takeaways

- Six sections: header, summary, skills, experience, education, extras
- One page for juniors; two maximum otherwise
- Standard headings + simple layout satisfy ATS parsers
- Save as PDF; avoid tables, columns, graphics, photos
- Run the plain-text compile test before every submit

## Practice Challenge

1. Build your resume skeleton in the six-section order with a 3-line summary
2. Audit your current resume for ATS traps - tables, columns, photos, non-standard headings
3. Run the plain-text compile test and fix any garbled section

**Answer:** A — Photos are excluded from standard resumes in most markets.

**Answer:** A — One page for junior levels; two maximum otherwise.

### Module 5 · LinkedIn: Building for Recruiters

LinkedIn is searchable - so it needs keywords in the right places. A recruiter searches by skill and title; if those words are not in your profile, you simply do not appear. The good news is that the whole profile is optimisable in an afternoon, starting with the headline, the About section, the Featured section, and the Skills list.

Think of LinkedIn as a lighter, public version of your resume. It rewards detail where resumes stay tight, and its search engine rewards keywords the way ATS does with resumes. Build it once, and it works for you while you sleep.

## Learning Objectives

- Write a 120-character keyword-rich LinkedIn headline
- Structure the About section as hook, proof, and transition
- Place your proof project in the Featured section
- Use Open to Work settings to control visibility

## Headline: Role + Transition + Tools

- Structure the headline as **role + transition + tools** - e.g. 'Operations Specialist -> Process Automation | AI Workflows | Data Analysis'
- Never write 'Seeking new opportunities' - it wastes your most visible 120 characters
- Recruiters search those exact terms - your headline is searchable, so make it keyword-dense
### The headline checklist
- Does it say what you ARE, not just what you want?
- Does it name 2-3 tools or skills a recruiter would type?
- Is it under 120 characters when previewed?

## About: Hook, Proof, Transition

- Write three short paragraphs: the hook (your story), the proof (2-3 achievements with numbers), and the transition (where you are heading)
- Ask AI for three drafts, then edit them until they sound like you - recruiter-proof but human
- End with a soft call to action: 'Open to AI-ops roles - message me'
```text
The About-section one-ask prompt:
Rewrite my headline and About for [role]. Headline: 120-char,
formatted role + transition + tools. About: 3 scannable paragraphs
using my real facts only - hook, proof with numbers, transition.
Flag suggested keywords separately so I can verify them.
```
- Keep the phrasing yours - AI polish that breaks your voice reads as spam

## Featured, Skills, and Settings

- **Featured:** put your proof project front and centre - this is your public portfolio showcase
- **Experience:** mirror your resume PAR bullets - LinkedIn rewards detail, expand where you can
- **Skills:** add the 10 skills from your target posting; get 3 real endorsements, mutual is best
- **Settings:** turn on 'Open to Work' for recruiters-only visibility to stay private at a current job
### The compounding effect
- A keyword-rich profile gets found; a found profile gets messaged
- Each weekly proof post adds a touchpoint recruiters can see
- Your profile is now a landing page, not a chore to update

## Key Takeaways

- Headline = role + transition + tools, under 120 characters
- About = hook, proof with numbers, and transition
- Featured showcases your proof project - your public portfolio
- Skills mirror the target posting; endorsements add social proof
- Open to Work (recruiters-only) keeps current employment private

### Module 6 · The Two Readability Tests

Before you submit, two quick tests catch the failures that happen silently - the ones that cost calls. The first is a human test of the top of your page; the second is a machine test of your layout. Both take less than five minutes and should be part of every application routine.

The tests are cheap and the information they give is decisive: either a stranger can tell what you are good at in six seconds, or you have more work to do before that resume ships.

## Learning Objectives

- Run the six-second scan with a real person
- Run the ATS plain-text test on any resume
- Add a final proofread for typos and key-term errors
- Build the two tests into your application routine

## Test 1: The Six-Second Scan

- Show your resume to one person who knows nothing about your work
- Give them six seconds to glance, then ask: 'What am I good at? What value would I bring?'
- If they cannot answer clearly, your top-of-page is not working
### What counts as passing
- The person names your role and a strength without prompting
- They can say what value you would bring in one sentence
- Anything less means your summary and leading bullets need work

## Test 2: The ATS Plain-Text Test

- Copy your resume text into a plain-text editor (Notepad, Notes)
- Read it through: logical headings, readable bullets, no jumbled columns
- If the structure is clear, an ATS will parse it; if sections garble, simplify the layout
```text
The combined pre-submit routine:
1. Six-second scan with one real person
2. Plain-text compile test on the file
3. Proofread for typos - especially skill and company names
4. Confirm the PDF opens and the fonts embed
5. Submit - then log the application in your tracker
```
- These five steps take five minutes and prevent silent failures

## The Typos and Key-Names Check

- One spelling error in a key skill or company name can trigger a rejection - it signals carelessness
- Proofread out loud, or ask a friend - fresh eyes catch what your brain skips
- Auto-correct trust is not enough; check the exact terms recruiters will search
### When to run the pair
- Run both tests every time you change the resume materially
- Add them to the same 45-minute routine as tailoring and applying
- Treat a failing test as useful data, not a personal verdict

## Key Takeaways

- Six-second scan checks whether top-of-page value is obvious
- Plain-text test reveals ATS parseability
- Typos in key names trigger rejections - proofread carefully
- Both tests live inside your daily application routine
- A failing test is data, not a verdict

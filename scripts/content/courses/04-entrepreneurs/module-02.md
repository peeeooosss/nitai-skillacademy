### Module 1 · Validation = Talking, Not Googling

Desk research cannot tell you if people will pay. **Only conversations reveal truth.** You can read a hundred market reports and still miss the one fact that matters: what your customer actually does with their money and time today.

This mission's goal is simple and demanding: 10 real customer conversations plus cheap demand signals, producing a defensible GO / PIVOT / KILL decision.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why conversations beat desk research
- Commit to 10 real customer conversations
- Combine conversations with demand signals
- Produce a defensible go/no-go decision

## Why Talking Beats Googling

- Desk research cannot tell you if people will pay
- Reports describe markets, not your customer's week
- Only conversations reveal actual behaviour
### The output
- 10 conversations plus cheap demand signals
- A defensible GO / PIVOT / KILL decision
- Evidence you can act on

## The Validation Goal

```text
Validation in one line:
10 real customer conversations
+ cheap demand signals
= a defensible GO / PIVOT / KILL decision
```
- Ten is enough to see patterns
- Signals add behaviour to words
- The decision is the deliverable

## What Validation Is Not

- Not asking friends if they like your idea
- Not counting likes or sign-ups alone
- Not building first and hoping
### The trap
- 'I will just build it, maybe people will come'
- That is how months disappear
- Conversations are cheaper than code

## Key Takeaways

- Only conversations reveal whether people will pay
- Goal: 10 real conversations plus cheap demand signals
- Desk research describes markets, not your customer's week
- Validation is not asking friends if they like it
- Build after validation, not before

### Module 2 · Step 1: Turn Assumptions Into Hypotheses

Assumptions are comfortable; hypotheses are testable. Turn your Mission 1 one-liner into 3-5 falsifiable hypotheses, each with a clear definition of what would prove it true and what would prove it false.

Falsifiable means you can design an interview or a signal that could disprove it. If nothing could disprove it, it is a belief, not a hypothesis.

## Learning Objectives

- Write 3-5 falsifiable hypotheses
- Cover problem, solution, and market
- Define evidence that proves and disproves each
- Make them testable with a 10-interview plan

## The Three Hypothesis Types

- **Problem hypothesis:** 'My customer, [type], feels [pain] at least once a week.'
- **Solution hypothesis:** 'They would use [X] if it costs [price] and saves [time/money].'
- **Market hypothesis:** 'There are enough of them reachable via [channel] in [region].'
### Why three types
- Problem, solution, and market can fail independently
- Each needs its own evidence
- Together they cover the whole bet

## Making Them Falsifiable

```text
Sharpen my hypotheses:
for each, what evidence would make it TRUE,
and what would prove it FALSE?
Make them testable with a 10-interview plan.
```
- Predefine the pass and fail conditions
- A testable hypothesis names its evidence
- Writing the falsifier in advance prevents bias

## Testable by Design

- Each hypothesis maps to interview questions
- Each maps to a demand signal
- The 10-interview plan tests all of them at once
### The discipline
- If nothing could disprove it, rewrite it
- Predefine what would change your mind
- Beliefs are not hypotheses

## Key Takeaways

- Turn assumptions into 3-5 falsifiable hypotheses
- Cover problem, solution, and market
- Define evidence that proves and disproves each
- Make them testable with a 10-interview plan
- If nothing could disprove it, it is a belief, not a hypothesis

## Practice Challenge

1. Write 3 hypotheses from your one-liner
2. Define the pass and fail evidence for each
3. Map each to an interview question

**Example Quiz**

1. What makes a good hypothesis?
- A) It is falsifiable with defined evidence
- B) It is optimistic

- D) It is popular

**Answer:** A — A hypothesis names evidence that would prove it true and false.

2. Which hypothesis type is 'customers feel this pain weekly'?

**Answer:** A — The problem hypothesis is about how often the customer feels the pain.

3. Why predefine the falsifier?
- A) To prevent bias and know when to change your mind
- B) To look scientific
- C) To fill a template
- D) To impress investors

**Answer:** A — Writing what would disprove it in advance keeps you honest.

4. How many hypotheses should you write?
- A) 3-5
- B) One
- C) Twenty
- D) None

**Answer:** A — Three to five falsifiable hypotheses cover problem, solution, and market.

### Module 3 · Step 2: Find 10 Real Customers (Not Friends)

Do not handpick friendly people. Find people who genuinely have the problem - through LinkedIn, communities, directories of your target industry, local business groups, or suppliers to that industry.

Expect to send 30+ outreach messages to get 10 conversations. End every call with 'Who else should I talk to?' - one warm intro beats five cold DMs.

## Learning Objectives

- Find real targets, not friendly contacts
- Write a low-commitment outreach message
- Send 30+ messages to book 10 chats
- Use snowballing for warm introductions

## Finding Real Targets

- LinkedIn and professional communities
- Directories of your target industry
- Local business groups
- Suppliers to that industry
### The rule
- Do not handpick friends
- Find people who genuinely have the problem
- Nose-nibbling math: 30+ sends for 10 replies

## The Reach-Out Message

```text
Write a short DM/email asking [customer type]
for a 15-minute chat about [pain].
End with a low-commitment ask. No selling.
Tone: curious peer, not pitch.
```
- Short, specific, and about their pain
- No selling in the first message
- Low commitment gets more replies

## Snowballing

- End every call with 'Who else should I talk to?'
- One warm intro beats five cold DMs
- Ask for two names, not a favour
### The compounding
- Each conversation opens the next
- Warm intros convert far better
- Your network grows as you validate

## Key Takeaways

- Find real targets who have the problem, not friends
- Expect 30+ sends for 10 conversations
- Keep outreach short, specific, and low-commitment
- Never sell in the first message
- Snowball every call for warm introductions

## Practice Challenge

1. List 30 real potential customers
2. Write and send your short outreach note
3. Book 10 chats, snowballing each one

### Module 4 · Step 3: The Interview (Listen More Than You Talk)

The interview is where validation succeeds or fails. The rule is simple: **90% their story, 10% your idea.** Ask about their week, their workflow, and the last time this problem hurt them.

Never pitch your solution during validation. Describe it only if asked, then immediately test willingness to pay with a make-believe offer.

## Learning Objectives

- Run interviews that listen more than they talk
- Ask about workarounds and money paid
- Avoid pitching during validation
- Use a repeatable interview script

## The Interview Rules

- 90% their story, 10% your idea
- Ask about their week and their workflow
- Ask about the last time the problem hurt them
- Ask for their workarounds - workarounds mean pain
### Why listening wins
- Pitching contaminates the data
- Stories reveal real behaviour
- Workarounds show willingness to act

## Money Questions and Scripts

```text
Interview script shortcuts:
Give me a 15-minute validation interview script
for [customer] and [pain]: opening, 6 questions
on current behaviour + money, and 2 'would you pay' probes.
```
- Ask money questions early: 'What have you already paid for?'
- Test with a make-believe: 'Would you pay [price]/month for [X]?'
- Then ask 'What would stop you?'

## Testing Willingness to Pay

- Behaviour beats opinions: what have they already paid for?
- Describe the offer only if asked
- Follow immediately with a price probe
### The signal
- Past payment predicts future payment
- Polite interest predicts nothing
- Money talk separates curiosity from demand

## Key Takeaways

- Listen 90% of the time - their story, not your pitch
- Ask about workarounds; workarounds reveal pain
- Ask what they have already paid for
- Never pitch during validation
- Test willingness to pay with a make-believe offer

## Practice Challenge

**Answer:** A — Behaviour - what they have already paid for - beats opinions.

### Module 5 · Step 4: Cheap Demand Signals (Before You Build)

Conversations give you words; signals give you behaviour. Before you build anything, gather cheap demand signals: search and marketplace data, pre-orders, and competitor reactions.

A landing page with 'pre-pay to reserve' is stronger proof than any survey. If incumbents exist, that is validation (there is demand) and a warning (you must beat them on something specific).

## Learning Objectives

- Gather search and marketplace signals
- Use pre-orders as the strongest cheap proof
- Read competitor presence correctly
- Combine signals with conversations

## Search and Marketplace Signals

- Search volume trends (relative, not absolute)
- How many competing offers exist
- The prices actually charged
### Why these
- They show existing demand
- They reveal price anchors
- They cost nothing to check

## Signals Prompt and Pre-Orders

```text
Ask about demand signals:
What search and marketplace signals exist for [solution],
and what do the price ranges tell me?
Then build a landing page with 'pre-pay [price] to reserve'.
Pre-orders over opinions.
```
- A pre-order is behaviour, not opinion
- Reservations are stronger than surveys
- Real money is the strongest signal

## Reading Competitors

- Incumbents mean there is demand - validation
- They also mean you must beat them on something specific
- Study their pricing, positioning, and gaps
### The lesson
- No competitors can mean no market
- Competitors are a map of what works
- Differentiation is the requirement

## Key Takeaways

- Signals add behaviour to conversational words
- Check search trends, competitor count, and prices
- Pre-orders are stronger proof than surveys
- Incumbents validate demand but demand differentiation
- Gather signals before you build

## Practice Challenge

1. Research search and marketplace signals for your solution
2. Note competitor prices and gaps
3. Plan a pre-order or reservation offer

**Example Quiz**

1. Why are pre-orders stronger than surveys?
- A) They are real behaviour, not opinion

**Answer:** A — A pre-pay reservation is behaviour - stronger proof than any survey.

2. What do incumbents signal?
- A) Demand exists, but you must differentiate

- C) Easy win
- D) Nothing

**Answer:** A — Competitors validate demand and warn that you must beat them on something specific.

3. Which is a cheap demand signal?
- A) Search trends and marketplace prices
- B) A finished product
- C) A patent
- D) A large ad budget

**Answer:** A — Search volume, competing offers, and actual prices are free signals to check.

4. When should you gather signals?
- A) Before you build
- B) After launch
- C) Never
- D) Only after raising money

**Answer:** A — Gather cheap signals before building so you do not waste months.

### Module 6 · Step 5: The Go / Pivot / Kill Meeting

After 10 conversations and signals, grade the evidence honestly and decide. This is the moment most founders avoid - and avoiding it is why months disappear.

Use a scoreboard, not a feeling. The decision is GO, PIVOT, or KILL, and each has clear criteria.

## Learning Objectives

- Grade evidence with a clear scoreboard
- Distinguish GO, PIVOT, and KILL criteria
- Decide without sunk-cost loyalty
- Document the decision and reasons

## The Evidence Scoreboard

```text
Grade evidence after 10 conversations:
Signal          Strong if...
Pain frequency  >= 8/10 report the problem actively
Money           >= 3 have paid for something similar
Pre-order       >= 5 sign-ups/reservations on your page
Workaround      Many use manual/fragmented fixes
```
- Thresholds turn feelings into evidence
- Each signal is observable
- The scoreboard guides the decision

## The Three Decisions

- **GO:** pain strong + some willingness to pay + reachable channel
- **PIVOT:** pain real but wrong slice, message, or channel - change who/what/where, not the whole idea
- **KILL:** customers shrug, no money behaviour, no channel - let go now
### The rule
- Sunk cost is not a strategy
- A fast KILL frees your runway
- A sharp PIVOT keeps the method

## Avoiding the Comfort Trap

- 'I will just build it, maybe people will come' is the trap
- Soft interest is not validation
- Paid behaviour is the only signal that counts
### The honesty test
- If you keep collecting likes and zero payment conversations, you are avoiding the truth
- Decide on evidence, in writing
- Write three reasons for your call

## Key Takeaways

- Grade evidence with a scoreboard, not a feeling
- GO: strong pain, willingness to pay, reachable channel
- PIVOT: change who/what/where, not the whole idea
- KILL: no money behaviour, no channel - let go now
- Sunk cost is not a strategy; decide in writing with three reasons

## Practice Challenge

1. Run the scoreboard on your 10 conversations
2. Hold your GO/PIVOT/KILL meeting
3. Write the decision and three reasons why

3. What is the KILL criterion?
- A) Customers shrug, no money behaviour, no channel

**Answer:** A — If there is no money behaviour and no channel, let go now.

4. Why is sunk cost a bad reason to continue?

- B) It is illegal

- D) It is slow

**Answer:** A — Past investment does not justify future spending; decide on evidence.

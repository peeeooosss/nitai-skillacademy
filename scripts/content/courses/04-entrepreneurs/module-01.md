### Module 1 · The Founder's First Job: Choose the Right Problem

Most failed startups did not die from bad execution - they died from **unvalidated ideas**. Founders fall in love with a solution before confirming anyone has the problem. Your first job is deliberately boring: build a wide pool of ideas, filter it hard, and pick one thing worth your next 90 days.

This mission is about choosing the problem, not perfecting the pitch. A great team on the wrong problem loses to a mediocre team on the right one.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why unvalidated ideas kill startups
- Treat idea selection as a deliberate filtering process
- Avoid falling in love with a solution too early
- Commit to one idea for the next 90 days

## Why Ideas Fail

- Startups die from unvalidated ideas, not bad execution
- Founders build solutions before confirming the problem
- Enthusiasm replaces evidence
### The trap
- 'I had this dream' with zero customer contact
- 'Uber for X' with no distribution reason
- 'I will figure out the market later'

## The Selection Process

- Generate a wide pool first - quantity before quality
- Filter hard with clear criteria
- Pick one idea worth 90 focused days
### Why boring is right
- Idea selection is upstream of everything
- A wide pool reduces your own bias
- Hard filtering prevents months of wasted build

## Commit to One Idea

```text
The founder's first job:
1. Generate a wide pool (quantity first)
2. Filter with the 4 criteria: pain, pays, reachable, timely
3. Read real trend signals, not hype
4. Pick ONE idea you would enjoy for 60 days
5. Write the one-liner and commit to a 90-day runway
```
- One idea, one focus, one runway
- The one-liner becomes the spine of the whole course
- Commitment beats optionality at this stage

## Key Takeaways

- Most startups die from unvalidated ideas, not bad execution
- Build a wide pool, then filter hard
- Avoid falling in love with a solution before the problem
- Pick one idea worth 90 focused days
- Commitment beats endless optionality

### Module 2 · Generate a Wide Pool (Quantity First)

Ideas are a numbers game. The founders who win generate many ideas and filter ruthlessly, rather than polishing the first one that arrives. Generate across categories to avoid your own bias.

Then add your **unfair advantage filter**: which ideas use skills, networks, or resources you already have? Ideas you are uniquely suited for beat objectively better ideas you cannot win.

## Learning Objectives

- Generate a wide pool across five categories
- Use AI to expand idea quantity
- Apply the unfair-advantage filter
- Shortlist ideas you can actually win

## Generate Across Categories

- Local services
- B2B tools
- AI-native content
- E-commerce niches
- Skill-based offerings
### Why categories
- They force breadth beyond your habits
- Each category has different economics
- Breadth reveals non-obvious options

## The Generation Prompt

```text
Act as a startup strategist.
Given my skills [X], interests [Y], and region [Z],
generate 20 business ideas in 5 batches:
local services, B2B tools, AI-native content,
e-commerce niches, and skill-based offerings.
For each: one-line pitch and who it serves.
```
- Twenty ideas is a minimum, not a target
- One-line pitches keep them comparable
- 'Who it serves' forces customer thinking early

## The Unfair Advantage Filter

- Which 5 ideas use skills you already have?
- Which use networks or communities you are in?
- Which use resources, data, or access you already hold?
- Ideas you are uniquely suited for beat 'better' ideas you cannot win
### The principle
- Advantage compounds; generic ideas do not
- Start where you are strong
- Shortlist 5, then apply the 4-filter screen

## Key Takeaways

- Ideas are a numbers game - generate wide first
- Generate across five categories to avoid bias
- Ask for one-line pitch and who it serves
- Apply the unfair-advantage filter
- Ideas you are suited for beat better ideas you cannot win

### Module 3 · The 4-Filter Screen (Kill Bad Ideas Fast)

With a shortlist in hand, kill bad ideas fast. The 4-filter screen scores each idea on pain, willingness to pay, reachability, and timing. AI can score them, but you apply the judgement.

**Red-flag ideas to skip:** a dream with zero customer contact, 'Uber for X' with no distribution reason, an AI wrapper with no moat, or 'I will figure out the market later' - market first.

## Learning Objectives

- Apply the 4-filter screen to each idea
- Score ideas with AI, then judge the scores
- Recognise the common red-flag ideas
- Kill weak ideas quickly and decisively

## The Four Filters

- **Pain?** Does someone feel this problem weekly? (Not 'nice to have')
- **Pays?** Will someone actually pay money? (Free users are not customers)
- **Reachable?** Can you reach and price your customer cheaply?
- **Timely?** Is the market accelerating? (AI tools, remote work, regulation)
### Why four
- They cover demand, money, distribution, and timing
- Each is a common cause of failure
- All four must hold for a viable idea

## Scoring With AI

```text
Score these 5 ideas against:
pain (1-5), willingness-to-pay (1-5),
reachability in my market (1-5), timing/trend (1-5).
Flag strengths and blind spots for each.
```
- AI gives consistent scores across ideas
- You judge whether the scores are credible
- Blind spots are as useful as strengths

## Red Flags to Skip

- 'I had this dream' with zero customer contact
- 'Uber for X' with no distribution reason
- 'AI wrapper with no moat' and no special access
- 'I will figure out the market later' - no, market first
### The discipline
- Killing fast is a skill, not a failure
- Every kept idea must pass all four filters
- Sunk enthusiasm is not evidence

## Key Takeaways

- Score every idea on pain, pays, reachable, and timely
- Free users are not customers - test willingness to pay
- Reachability and timing are as important as pain
- Skip the classic red-flag ideas
- Kill weak ideas fast - it is a skill

## Practice Challenge

**Answer:** A — Score each idea on pain, willingness to pay, reachability, and timing.

### Module 4 · Read the Trend, Not the Hype

Trend research done wrong is reading AI summaries of other people's hype. Done right, it starts from **primary sources**: marketplaces, job boards, regulator pages, and real communities. What employers pay for is what businesses need.

Classify every signal as **evidence** (you can open the source) or **hype** (AI's confident guess). Only evidence should influence your decision.

## Learning Objectives

- Start trend research from primary sources
- Ask AI for verifiable signals, not opinions
- Separate evidence from hype
- Use signals to judge market timing

## Primary Sources First

- Marketplaces - what is selling and at what price
- Job boards - what employers pay for is what businesses need
- Regulator pages - new rules create new needs
- Real communities - where practitioners complain and ask
### Why primary
- AI summaries compress and distort
- Primary sources are verifiable
- They show money changing hands, not opinions

## Asking for Verifiable Signals

```text
Ask for verifiable signals:
1. What signals indicate [trend] is growing?
2. Job posts mentioning it, spending reports from published sources,
   platform API usage trends?
3. Which of these are verifiable vs opinion?
Classify each signal as evidence or hype.
```
- Demand signals must be openable sources
- Ask AI to separate verifiable from opinion
- Evidence beats confident assertion

## Evidence vs Hype

- **Evidence** - you can open the source and check the number
- **Hype** - a confident guess with no traceable origin
- Only evidence should shape your timing decision
### The habit
- Open at least three sources per trend
- Note which signals you verified
- Discard the rest

## Key Takeaways

- Start trend research from primary sources, not AI summaries
- Job posts, marketplaces, and spending reports are real signals
- Ask AI to separate verifiable signals from opinion
- Classify every signal as evidence or hype
- Only evidence should shape your timing decision

### Module 5 · Pick Your ONE Idea (FOMO-Proof)

With your pool filtered and signals checked, choose a single idea to take into Mission 2. The test that makes it FOMO-proof: **if I get zero results for 60 days, would I still enjoy working on this?** Enjoyment is your only guaranteed runway.

Write your one-liner and print it. Every mission after this serves that sentence.

## Learning Objectives

- Apply the 60-day enjoyment test
- Write a clear one-liner for your idea
- Commit to one idea for the course
- Make the one-liner the spine of your work

## The 60-Day Enjoyment Test

- 'If I get zero results for 60 days, would I still enjoy working on this?'
- Enjoyment is your only guaranteed runway
- Results may take longer than motivation lasts
### Why it matters
- Founders quit when the work is joyless
- Intrinsic interest sustains the hard months
- Passion is a filter, not a strategy

## Write the One-Liner

```text
The one-liner formula:
'I'm building [X] for [customer] because [pain],
and I'll profit via [pricing].'
Print it. Every mission after this serves that sentence.
```
- The formula forces clarity on product, customer, pain, and money
- Printing it keeps it visible
- It becomes the reference for every later decision

## Commit for the Course

- One idea for the whole 90-day runway
- Pivot the idea only when evidence says so, not on a whim
- The skills transfer even if the idea changes
### The mindset
- Focus beats optionality here
- Evidence drives pivots
- Commitment is temporary but total

## Key Takeaways

### Module 6 · The 90-Day Founder Runway

Commit to a focused sprint for the rest of the course. The runway moves from validation to build to launch to systemisation, with a clear check-in point at week 4.

If at week 4 evidence says 'no', you pivot the idea, not the method - the skills transfer.

## Learning Objectives

- Lay out the 90-day runway
- Map each phase to a mission
- Set a week-4 evidence check
- Commit to the sprint

## The Runway Phases

- **Weeks 1-2:** validate the idea (Mission 2) with real conversations
- **Weeks 3-4:** design the business model (Mission 3) and build a landing/MVP shell (Mission 4)
- **Weeks 5-6:** launch, get first users, and build pitch materials (Missions 5-6)
- **Weeks 7-8:** systems, metrics, and first revenue (Missions 7-8)
### Why a runway
- A fixed window creates urgency
- Each phase has one job
- Progress becomes visible

## The Week-4 Check

```text
The 90-day founder runway:
Weeks 1-2: validate (Mission 2)
Weeks 3-4: model + landing/MVP (Missions 3-4)
Weeks 5-6: launch + pitch (Missions 5-6)
Weeks 7-8: systems + metrics + revenue (Missions 7-8)
Week 4: evidence check - continue or pivot the idea, not the method.
```
- Week 4 is the honest checkpoint
- Pivot the idea if evidence says no
- Keep the method - the skills transfer

## Committing to the Sprint

- Block the time in your calendar
- Treat the runway as a real commitment
- Ship something every phase
### The payoff
- Focus converts effort into evidence
- Evidence converts into a real business or a real lesson
- Either way you move forward

## Key Takeaways

- The runway: validate, build, launch, systemise
- Weeks 1-2 validate; 3-4 model and MVP; 5-6 launch and pitch; 7-8 systems and revenue
- Week 4 is the evidence checkpoint
- Pivot the idea, not the method, if evidence says no
- Commit the time - ship something every phase

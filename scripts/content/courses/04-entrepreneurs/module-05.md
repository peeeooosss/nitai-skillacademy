# Mission 5: Pitch Deck & Investor Materials

### Module 1 · Pitch Deck Purpose: Make Them Want the Next Call

A pitch deck's job is not to raise money. Its job is to earn the next conversation. **The deck opens a door; the meeting does the rest.**

That single reframe changes everything: you stop trying to cram every fact in and start telling a clear, credible story that makes the reader want to keep talking.

## Learning Objectives

- Reframe the deck as a door-opener, not a money-raiser
- Aim for the next conversation
- Prioritise clarity and credibility over completeness
- Prepare the deck for the right audience

## The Real Job

- Earn the next conversation
- Open a door, not close a round
- Make them curious enough to meet
### Why
- Investors decide fast, then dig
- A meeting converts better than a document
- The deck is a hook, not a dossier

## Clarity Over Completeness

- Do not cram every fact in
- Tell a clear, credible story
- Leave room for questions
### The rule
- One idea per slide
- Numbers must be defensible
- Confusion kills curiosity

## The Audience

- Match the deck to who is reading it
- Investors, partners, and customers need different angles
- The same core story, tuned to the reader
### The payoff
- A deck that earns the meeting
- A story you can tell out loud
- Materials that support, not replace, you
```text
Help me reframe my pitch deck as a door-opener for
[audience]. Give me the one sentence I want remembered
and the 3 beliefs they must hold to take the next meeting.
```

## Key Takeaways

- A pitch deck's job is to earn the next conversation
- It opens a door; the meeting does the rest
- Prioritise clarity and credibility over completeness
- One idea per slide; numbers must be defensible
- Match the deck to its audience

## Practice Challenge

1. Write the one sentence you want remembered
2. List what the reader must believe to take the meeting
3. Cut anything that does not serve that belief

**Example Quiz**

1. What is a pitch deck's real job?
- A) Earn the next conversation
- B) Raise money on its own
- C) Replace the founder
- D) List every detail

**Answer:** A — The deck opens a door; the meeting does the rest.

2. What should the deck prioritise?
- A) Clarity and credibility
- B) Length
- C) Design flourishes
- D) Completeness

**Answer:** A — Clear, credible storytelling beats cramming in every fact.

3. How many ideas per slide?
- A) One
- B) Five
- C) Ten
- D) As many as fit

**Answer:** A — One idea per slide keeps the story clear.

4. Why match the deck to the audience?
- A) Investors, partners, and customers need different angles
- B) It is a rule
- C) To save paper
- D) To look busy

**Answer:** A — Tune the same core story to whoever is reading.

### Module 2 · The 10-Slide Standard (Scan the Funnel)

Investors read decks in minutes. The 10-slide standard gives them the funnel they are scanning for: problem, solution, market, traction, model, competition, team, and ask.

Follow the order. It mirrors how investors evaluate a business and makes your story easy to follow.

## Learning Objectives

- List the 10 standard slides
- Order them as an investor reads
- Fill each slide with one clear idea
- Build the deck to be scanned in minutes

## The 10 Slides

```text
The 10-slide standard:
1.  Title / one-liner
2.  Problem
3.  Solution
4.  Market size
5.  Traction / evidence
6.  Business model
7.  Competition / edge
8.  Team
9.  Roadmap / use of funds
10. Ask / contact
```
- Ten slides is a known, scannable structure
- Each slide answers one question
- The order mirrors investor evaluation

## Scanning the Funnel

- Investors scan problem, solution, market, traction first
- Then model, competition, team, ask
- Each slide must survive a five-second scan
### The discipline
- Headline first on every slide
- Numbers visible
- Details go in the appendix

## Filling Each Slide

- One clear idea per slide
- Headline states the takeaway
- Support with one number or image
### The payoff
- A deck that reads in minutes
- A story that survives skimming
- Room to go deep in the meeting

## Key Takeaways

- Use the 10-slide standard: problem to ask
- Order slides as an investor reads
- Each slide answers one question
- Headline first; one number or image to support
- Details belong in the appendix

## Practice Challenge

1. Draft your 10 slides with headlines first
2. Give each slide one idea and one number
3. Move details to an appendix

**Example Quiz**

1. How many slides are in the standard?
- A) 10
- B) 5
- C) 30
- D) 50

**Answer:** A — The 10-slide standard spans problem to ask.

2. Which slide comes first?
- A) Title / one-liner
- B) Team
- C) Ask
- D) Competition

**Answer:** A — Start with a title and one-liner, then problem, solution, and so on.

3. How fast should each slide read?
- A) In a five-second scan
- B) In ten minutes
- C) In an hour
- D) It should not

**Answer:** A — Each slide must survive a five-second scan with a headline and a number.

4. Where do details go?
- A) In the appendix
- B) On the title slide
- C) Nowhere
- D) In the ask

**Answer:** A — Keep slides scannable and move detail into the appendix.

### Module 3 · The Founder Story That Hooks

Investors bet on people as much as ideas. A short founder story explains why you, why this problem, and why now - in a way that builds trust fast.

The story is not your life history. It is the shortest credible reason you are the right person to solve this, backed by evidence.

## Learning Objectives

- Tell why you, why this, why now
- Build trust with evidence, not adjectives
- Keep the story short and credible
- Connect the story to the business

## Why You, Why This, Why Now

- **Why you:** the experience or obsession that qualifies you
- **Why this:** the insight only you seem to have
- **Why now:** the shift that makes this the moment
### Why it matters
- Investors bet on people
- A credible why reduces perceived risk
- It makes the idea memorable

## Evidence, Not Adjectives

- Replace 'passionate' with proof of action
- Show what you have built, shipped, or learned
- Use specific, checkable facts
### The rule
- Stories need receipts
- Specificity builds trust
- Adjectives do not

## The Story Prompt

```text
Draft a 60-second founder story: why me, why this problem,
why now. Use my background [details] and evidence [proof].
Keep it credible, no buzzwords. End with the one-line mission.
```
- Sixty seconds keeps it tight
- Buzzwords weaken credibility
- End on the mission

## Key Takeaways

- Tell why you, why this problem, why now
- The story is the shortest credible reason you are right for this
- Replace adjectives with evidence of action
- Specificity builds trust
- End on the one-line mission

## Practice Challenge

1. Draft your 60-second founder story
2. Replace every adjective with a fact
3. Practise it out loud until it is natural

**Example Quiz**

1. What three questions does the founder story answer?
- A) Why you, why this, why now
- B) Who, what, where
- C) When, how, how much
- D) Cost, price, profit

**Answer:** A — Why you, why this problem, and why now build investor trust.

2. What should replace adjectives?
- A) Evidence of action
- B) More adjectives
- C) Jargon
- D) Emotion

**Answer:** A — Show what you have built, shipped, or learned - stories need receipts.

3. How long should the founder story be?
- A) About 60 seconds
- B) Ten minutes
- C) An hour
- D) A page

**Answer:** A — A tight 60-second story keeps attention and credibility.

4. How should the story end?
- A) On the one-line mission
- B) With a joke
- C) With a request for money
- D) With your CV

**Answer:** A — Close on the one-line mission to leave a clear impression.

### Module 4 · Projections That Don't Look Painted

Bad projections are obvious: hockey-stick growth with no basis. Good projections are boring, bottom-up, and clearly explained.

Build them from **assumptions you can defend**, not from a target you wish for. Show the assumptions so the reader can test them.

## Learning Objectives

- Build projections bottom-up from assumptions
- Show assumptions transparently
- Avoid the hockey-stick cliche
- Make numbers an investor can defend

## Bottom-Up, Not Top-Down

- Start from customers and price, not market size
- Multiply realistic acquisition by conversion
- Show the arithmetic
### Why
- Top-down '1% of a big market' is not a plan
- Bottom-up numbers can be checked
- Assumptions are the real content

## Avoiding the Hockey Stick

- No hockey-stick growth with no basis
- Boring, defensible growth is more credible
- Show the constraint, then the growth
### The rule
- Under-promise on growth
- Over-deliver on evidence
- Explain every jump

## The Projections Prompt

```text
Build 12-month projections for [business] bottom-up:
acquisition [X]/month, conversion [Y]%, price [Z], churn [W]%.
Show the assumptions in a small table and the resulting
revenue and costs. Use [currency]. No hockey sticks.
```
- Small table of assumptions
- Resulting revenue and costs
- Defendable, not painted

## Key Takeaways

- Build projections bottom-up from customers and price
- Show your assumptions so they can be tested
- Avoid hockey-stick growth with no basis
- Boring, defensible numbers are more credible
- Explain every jump in the numbers

## Practice Challenge

1. Write your acquisition, conversion, price, and churn assumptions
2. Build 12-month bottom-up projections
3. Present the assumption table with the numbers

**Example Quiz**

1. What is the difference between bottom-up and top-down?
- A) Bottom-up starts from customers and price, not market size
- B) Bottom-up is bigger
- C) Top-down is more accurate
- D) They are the same

**Answer:** A — Bottom-up starts from acquisition, conversion, and price so it can be checked.

2. Why is a hockey stick bad?
- A) Growth with no basis destroys credibility
- B) It is too small
- C) It is illegal
- D) It is too boring

**Answer:** A — Unexplained explosive growth reads as painted, not projected.

3. What is the real content of projections?
- A) The assumptions
- B) The total
- C) The graph colour
- D) The font

**Answer:** A — Showing assumptions lets the reader test your numbers.

4. What should you show alongside the revenue?
- A) The assumption table and costs
- B) Only the final number
- C) A logo
- D) A testimonial

**Answer:** A — Show the assumptions and the resulting revenue and costs.

### Module 5 · The Meeting Kit (Have These Ready)

The meeting is where the deck gets tested. Bring a small kit so you can answer follow-ups without fumbling: the one-pager, financials, demo, and reference list.

The kit turns a pitch into a credible conversation. It shows you have done the work behind the slides.

## Learning Objectives

- Assemble the core meeting kit
- Prepare for common follow-up questions
- Bring proof and references
- Turn the pitch into a conversation

## The Kit

- The deck and a one-page summary
- Simple financials and assumptions
- A live demo or 60-second demo video
- A reference list (customers, advisors, evidence)
### Why
- Follow-ups decide the outcome
- Proof beats promises
- Prepared founders look credible

## Preparing for Follow-Ups

- Anticipate the hard questions on numbers and competition
- Have evidence for every claim on a slide
- Know your weakest assumption and address it
### The rule
- Honesty about risk builds trust
- 'I do not know, but here is how I will find out' is a good answer
- Never bluff a number

## The Kit Prompt

```text
Prepare a meeting kit for my [stage] startup: a one-pager,
a simple financial summary, a demo plan, and a reference list.
List the 10 hardest questions I should expect and short,
honest answers for each.
```
- Ten hard questions is a realistic prep set
- Honest answers build trust
- Rehearse out loud

## Key Takeaways

- Bring a meeting kit: one-pager, financials, demo, references
- Follow-up questions decide the outcome
- Have evidence for every claim on a slide
- Know and address your weakest assumption
- Honesty about risk builds trust; never bluff a number

## Practice Challenge

1. Assemble your one-pager, financials, demo, and references
2. List ten hard questions and honest answers
3. Rehearse the answers out loud

**Example Quiz**

1. What belongs in the meeting kit?
- A) One-pager, financials, demo, references
- B) Only the deck
- C) A gift
- D) A long report

**Answer:** A — The kit prepares you for follow-ups with proof and references.

2. What decides the meeting outcome?
- A) Follow-up questions and how you answer them
- B) The font
- C) The venue
- D) The gift

**Answer:** A — Follow-ups decide the outcome, so prepare evidence for every claim.

3. How should you handle an unknown?
- A) 'I do not know, but here is how I will find out'
- B) Bluff a number
- C) Change the subject
- D) Get defensive

**Answer:** A — Honesty about gaps builds trust; never bluff a number.

4. What must back every slide claim?
- A) Evidence
- B) Enthusiasm
- C) A promise
- D) A projection

**Answer:** A — Have evidence ready for every claim you make in the deck.

### Module 6 · Practise the Two Pitches

You need two pitches, not one: a 60-second elevator pitch and a 5-minute deep pitch. Each has a different job.

The elevator pitch earns the meeting; the deep pitch earns the trust. Practise both until they are natural, not memorised.

## Learning Objectives

- Deliver a 60-second elevator pitch
- Deliver a 5-minute deep pitch
- Know the job of each pitch
- Practise until natural, not robotic

## The 60-Second Elevator Pitch

- One sentence on the problem and who has it
- One sentence on your solution and edge
- One sentence on traction and the ask
### Its job
- Earn the next conversation
- Be repeatable by the listener
- Fit any hallway moment

## The 5-Minute Deep Pitch

- Walk the 10 slides with emphasis on traction and edge
- Leave room for questions
- Tell the founder story early
### Its job
- Earn trust
- Show depth and honesty
- Open the real conversation

## Practising Until Natural

- Rehearse out loud, not in your head
- Record yourself and cut the filler
- Aim for natural, not memorised
### The payoff
- Calm delivery under pressure
- A story you can adapt to the room
- Confidence built on preparation
```text
Give me a practice plan for both pitches: a 60-second
elevator pitch and a 5-minute deep pitch for [business].
Include a script skeleton and 5 filler words to cut.
```

## Key Takeaways

- You need two pitches: 60-second and 5-minute
- The elevator pitch earns the meeting; the deep pitch earns trust
- Tell the founder story early in the deep pitch
- Rehearse out loud and record yourself
- Aim for natural, not memorised

## Practice Challenge

1. Write and rehearse your 60-second pitch
2. Write and rehearse your 5-minute pitch
3. Record both and cut the filler

**Example Quiz**

1. What are the two pitches?
- A) 60-second elevator and 5-minute deep

- D) Formal and casual

**Answer:** A — A 60-second elevator pitch and a 5-minute deep pitch serve different jobs.

2. What is the elevator pitch's job?
- A) Earn the next conversation
- B) Close the round
- C) Explain every detail
- D) Show the appendix

**Answer:** A — The elevator pitch earns the meeting; the deep pitch earns trust.

3. When should the founder story appear?
- A) Early in the deep pitch
- B) Never
- C) At the very end
- D) In the elevator pitch only

**Answer:** A — Tell the founder story early in the 5-minute pitch to build trust.

4. How should you rehearse?
- A) Out loud, recording yourself, aiming for natural
- B) Silently
- C) Once
- D) By reading notes

**Answer:** A — Rehearse out loud, record, cut filler, and aim for natural delivery.

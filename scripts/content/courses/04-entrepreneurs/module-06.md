### Module 1 · Lean Ops = Systems Before They Hurt

Operations feel optional when you are small - until they do not. Lean ops means building simple systems **before** the pain arrives, not after.

A system is any repeatable way of doing something: a template, a checklist, a schedule, a folder. Small systems now save big chaos later.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why systems come before pain
- Recognise a system in everyday work
- Build simple systems early
- Keep ops lean and repeatable

## Systems Before Pain

- Ops feel optional when you are small
- Chaos arrives suddenly, usually with growth
- Build simple systems before the pain
### Why
- Growth exposes missing systems
- Firefighting costs more than preventing
- Calm founders make better decisions

## What a System Is

- Any repeatable way of doing something
- A template, checklist, schedule, or folder
- Documented so anyone can follow it
### The rule
- If you do it twice, systematise it
- Small and simple beats perfect and unused
- Systems free your attention

## Keeping Ops Lean

- Only build systems for real, recurring work
- Avoid bureaucracy you do not need yet
- Review and simplify regularly
### The payoff
- Less chaos as you grow
- More attention for the edge
- Operations that scale with you
```text
List the 5 operations systems a solo founder needs first
for [business], with a one-line template or checklist for
each. Keep them lean and avoid bureaucracy I do not need.
```

## Key Takeaways

- Build simple systems before the pain arrives
- A system is any repeatable way of doing something
- If you do it twice, systematise it
- Keep ops lean - small and simple beats perfect and unused
- Systems free your attention for the edge

## Practice Challenge

1. List three tasks you repeat weekly
2. Turn each into a simple checklist or template
3. Store them where you will actually find them

**Example Quiz**

1. When should you build systems?
- A) Before the pain arrives
- B) After a crisis
- C) Never
- D) Only when funded

**Answer:** A — Small systems built early prevent the chaos that arrives with growth.

2. What is a system?
- A) Any repeatable way of doing something
- B) Expensive software
- C) A big team
- D) A legal document

**Answer:** A — A template, checklist, schedule, or folder counts as a system.

3. When should you systematise a task?
- A) If you do it twice
- B) If you do it a hundred times
- C) Never
- D) Only if you enjoy it

**Answer:** A — If you do something twice, make it repeatable.

4. What is the goal of lean ops?
- A) Free your attention for the edge
- B) Build bureaucracy
- C) Impress investors
- D) Fill your calendar

**Answer:** A — Simple systems keep ops calm so your attention goes to the edge.

Pick one primary channel, one weekly rhythm, and one metric. AI helps you produce and schedule; you stay consistent.

## Learning Objectives

- Build a daily-habit marketing engine
- Choose one primary channel
- Set a weekly content rhythm
- Track one simple metric

## Daily Habits Beat Bursts

- Consistency beats occasional big pushes
- Small budget forces focus
- One channel done well beats five done poorly
### Why
- Algorithms reward consistency
- Habits compound
- Focus makes results readable

## One Channel, One Rhythm

- Choose one primary channel where your customers are

### The rule
- Do not spread thin

### Module 3 · Finance: Know Every Rupee (AI Assists, You Decide)

You do not need an accountant to run lean finances, but you do need to know every rupee or dollar moving in and out. **AI assists the tracking; you make the decisions.**

Keep a simple system: one place for records, a weekly review, and clear separation of business and personal money.

## Learning Objectives

- Track money in and out simply
- Run a weekly finance review
- Keep business and personal money separate
- Use AI for tracking, keep decisions

## Know Every Rupee

- Track every amount in and out
- One place for all records
- Review weekly, not yearly
### Why
- Cash surprises kill ventures
- Weekly review catches problems early
- Records make taxes and loans possible

## Separation and Simplicity

- Keep business and personal money separate
- Use one account and one simple ledger
- Categorise with AI, verify yourself
### The rule
- Simple and consistent
- Verify AI's categorisation
- Never mix the accounts

## The Finance Prompt

```text
Set up a simple finance system for a solo founder:
one account, a weekly review checklist, categories for
[business type], and a template to log income and expenses.
Show how AI can categorise entries while I verify.
```
- One account, one ledger
- Weekly checklist
- AI categorises, you verify

## Key Takeaways

- Know every rupee or dollar in and out
- Keep one place for records and review weekly
- Separate business and personal money
- AI assists tracking; you make the decisions
- Records enable taxes, loans, and confidence

## Practice Challenge

1. Set up one business account and a simple ledger
2. Create a weekly finance review checklist
3. Log this week's income and expenses

**Example Quiz**

2. Why separate business and personal money?
- A) To keep records clean and avoid cash surprises

- D) It is optional

**Answer:** A — Separation keeps records clear and taxes and loans possible.

3. What is AI's role in finance?

- D) Delete it

**Answer:** A — Categorise with AI but always verify the entries.

### Module 4 · Legal & Contracts: The First-Customer Safety Net

Legal basics are not about being paranoid; they are a safety net for your first customers. A simple contract, clear terms, and basic compliance prevent most early disasters.

You do not need a law firm for everything. Start with templates and plain-language terms, and get professional help only for real risk.

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the legal basics for a first customer
- Use simple contracts and clear terms
- Know when to get professional help
- Build trust with transparent terms

## The First-Customer Basics

- A simple agreement: scope, price, payment, and delivery
- Clear terms for refunds and cancellations
- Basic registration and tax compliance where required
### Why
- Clear terms prevent disputes
- Compliance avoids fines
- Transparency builds trust

## Templates and Plain Language

- Start with proven contract templates
- Write terms in plain language customers understand
- Customise for your offer, not a lawyer's draft
### The rule
- Simple and clear beats long and legalistic
- Both sides should understand it
- Keep signed copies

## When to Get Help

- Use professionals for real risk: large sums, IP, equity, regulated work
- For routine first customers, templates are enough
- Ask AI to explain clauses in plain language
### The boundary
- AI explains; it does not give legal advice
- Verify anything important
- Spend on lawyers only where risk is high
```text
Draft a plain-language customer agreement for [offer]:
scope, price, payment, delivery, refunds, and cancellation.
Then list the points where I should consult a professional.
```

## Key Takeaways

- Legal basics are a safety net for your first customers
- Start with a simple agreement: scope, price, payment, delivery
- Use templates and plain language
- Get professional help for real risk - large sums, IP, equity, regulated work
- AI explains clauses; it does not give legal advice

## Practice Challenge

1. Draft a simple customer agreement from a template
2. Write plain-language refund and cancellation terms
3. List where you need professional help

**Example Quiz**

1. What should a simple agreement cover?
- A) Scope, price, payment, delivery
- B) Only the price
- C) The founder's life story
- D) Marketing plans

**Answer:** A — Scope, price, payment, and delivery prevent most early disputes.

2. Why use plain language?
- A) Both sides should understand the terms
- B) It is shorter
- C) It looks modern
- D) It is required

**Answer:** A — Clear terms that customers understand build trust and prevent disputes.

3. When should you hire a professional?
- A) For real risk - large sums, IP, equity, regulated work
- B) For every customer
- C) Never
- D) Only at IPO

**Answer:** A — Routine first customers can use templates; get help where risk is high.

4. What can AI do with legal clauses?
- A) Explain them in plain language, not give legal advice
- B) Sign contracts
- C) Represent you in court
- D) Guarantee compliance

**Answer:** A — AI explains clauses; verify anything important with a professional.

### Module 5 · Automate Admin Before It Autopilots You

Admin creeps up on solo founders: invoices, follow-ups, scheduling, records. Left unmanaged, admin autopilots your day instead of you automating it.

Automate the recurring admin now with simple triggers, and reclaim hours every week.

## Learning Objectives

- Identify recurring admin tasks
- Automate with simple triggers
- Reclaim weekly hours
- Keep automations reliable

## Admin That Creeps Up

- Invoices and payment reminders
- Follow-ups and scheduling
- Records, receipts, and reporting
### Why it matters
- Admin expands to fill your time
- It feels urgent but is rarely important
- It steals hours from the edge

## Simple Triggers

- Trigger: new customer signs up, then send a welcome
- Trigger: invoice date, then send a reminder
- Trigger: form submitted, then log and follow up
### The rule
- One trigger, one action
- Start with the most repetitive task
- Add automations one at a time

## Keeping Automations Reliable

- Test each automation before trusting it
- Check outputs weekly at first
- Fix breakage fast
### The payoff
- Hours returned every week
- Fewer forgotten follow-ups
- Admin that runs while you build
```text
Design 3 simple automations for my admin: an invoice
reminder, a new-customer welcome, and a form-to-record
flow. For each, give the trigger, the action, and how to test it.
```

## Key Takeaways

- Admin autopilots your day unless you automate it first
- Identify recurring tasks: invoices, follow-ups, scheduling, records
- Automate with simple one-trigger, one-action flows
- Test each automation and check outputs weekly at first
- Reclaim hours for your edge

## Practice Challenge

1. List your recurring admin tasks
2. Build one simple automation for the most repetitive
3. Test it and check its output for a week

3. Which task should you automate first?

- B) The rarest one

- D) None

**Answer:** A — Start with the most repetitive task to reclaim the most hours.

4. How do you keep automations reliable?
- A) Test them and check outputs weekly at first

**Answer:** A — Test each automation and check outputs until you trust it.

### Module 6 · The "AI Does Operations" Boundaries

AI can do a lot of operations, but it cannot own judgement, relationships, or accountability. Knowing the boundaries keeps you out of trouble and keeps trust intact.

**AI drafts, sorts, and summarises. You decide, relate, and own the outcome.**

## Learning Objectives

- Draw the boundary between AI and you
- Know what AI must never decide alone
- Keep human accountability intact
- Use AI safely in operations

## What AI Can Do

- Draft, sort, summarise, and schedule
- Suggest replies and flag issues
- Handle repetitive internal work
### Why
- These tasks are fast and low-risk
- AI frees your time
- It never sleeps or forgets

## What AI Must Not Decide

- Money decisions and pricing
- Legal commitments and contracts
- Anything affecting a customer's trust or safety
- Final calls on hiring or firing
### The boundary
- AI drafts; you decide
- AI relates? No - you relate
- You own the outcome

## Staying Accountable

```text
Define AI boundaries for my operations:
tasks AI can fully handle, tasks it can draft but I must approve,
and tasks it must never touch. For each, note the check I will do.
```
- Three tiers: full, draft-and-approve, never
- Note the human check for each
- Accountability never transfers to AI

## Key Takeaways

- AI drafts, sorts, and summarises; you decide, relate, and own
- AI can handle repetitive, low-risk internal work
- AI must not decide money, legal, trust, or people matters alone
- Use three tiers: full, draft-and-approve, never
- Accountability never transfers to AI

## Practice Challenge

1. List your operations tasks
2. Sort them into full, draft-and-approve, and never
3. Write the human check for each drafted task

**Example Quiz**

1. What is AI's proper role in operations?
- A) Draft, sort, summarise, and schedule
- B) Own accountability
- C) Make final decisions
- D) Handle legal commitments

**Answer:** A — AI drafts and sorts; you decide, relate, and own the outcome.

2. Which must AI never decide alone?
- A) Money, legal, trust, and people matters
- B) Scheduling
- C) Summarising
- D) Sorting

**Answer:** A — Final calls on money, legal, trust, and people stay human.

3. What are the three tiers?
- A) Full, draft-and-approve, never
- B) Fast, medium, slow
- C) Cheap, costly, free
- D) Now, later, never

**Answer:** A — Classify tasks as fully automatable, draft-and-approve, or never touch.

4. Where does accountability live?
- A) With you, never with AI
- B) With AI
- C) With the customer
- D) With the tool vendor

**Answer:** A — Accountability never transfers to AI - you own the outcome.

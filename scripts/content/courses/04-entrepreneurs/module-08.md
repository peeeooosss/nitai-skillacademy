### Module 1 · Launch Isn't an Event — It's a Campaign

A launch is not a single day; it is a short campaign with a build-up, a push, and a follow-through. Treating it as one event wastes the moment.

The campaign has three phases: **before** (build anticipation), **during** (push hard for a few days), and **after** (follow up and convert).

## Learning Objectives

- Reframe launch as a campaign, not an event
- Plan before, during, and after phases
- Build anticipation before launch day
- Follow through to convert interest

## Campaign, Not Event

- A launch is a short campaign, not one day
- Build-up, push, and follow-through
- The after phase often matters most
### Why
- One-day launches waste the moment
- Anticipation multiplies attention
- Follow-through converts the spike

## The Three Phases

- **Before:** build anticipation and a waiting list
- **During:** push hard for a few days across your channel
- **After:** follow up, answer, and convert
### The rule
- Plan all three phases in advance
- Do not stop when launch day ends
- Consistency carries the campaign

## Building Anticipation

- Tease the problem and the promise
- Collect a waiting list before launch
- Give early supporters a reason to share
### The payoff
- A launch that lands on ready ears
- More signal in fewer days
- Momentum you can sustain
```text
Design a launch campaign for [business] with before,
during, and after phases. Give the anticipation tactics,
the launch-day push, and the follow-up plan.
```

## Key Takeaways

- A launch is a campaign, not a single event
- Three phases: before, during, after
- Build anticipation and a waiting list before launch day
- Follow through after the push to convert interest
- Plan all three phases in advance

## Practice Challenge

1. Plan your before, during, and after phases
2. Build a waiting list before launch day
3. Write your follow-through plan

**Answer:** A — Planning before, during, and after keeps momentum alive.

### Module 2 · Step 1: The 14-Day Launch Campaign

A 14-day campaign gives you a concrete plan: week one builds anticipation, launch day and the days after push and convert.

Keep it simple and specific: daily actions on your one channel, plus follow-up with everyone who shows interest.

## Learning Objectives

- Lay out a 14-day campaign
- Plan daily actions on one channel
- Time the launch push
- Follow up systematically

## The 14-Day Structure

- Days 1-6: anticipation content and waiting-list building
- Day 7: launch push - announce across your channel
- Days 8-14: follow-up, objections, and conversion
### Why 14 days
- Long enough to build momentum
- Short enough to stay focused
- Two weeks of daily habits is sustainable

## Daily Actions

- One content action per day on your channel
- One outreach action per day to warm contacts
- One follow-up action per day
### The rule
- Specific, small, daily
- One channel, one focus
- Track what you did

## The Campaign Prompt

```text
Write a 14-day launch campaign for [business] on [channel]:
daily actions for anticipation, launch day, and follow-up,
with one metric to watch. Keep it doable for a solo founder.
```
- Day-by-day makes it doable
- One metric keeps it honest
- Built for a solo founder

## Key Takeaways

- Run a 14-day campaign: 6 days anticipation, launch, 7 days follow-up
- Do one content, one outreach, and one follow-up action daily
- Keep to one channel and one focus
- Track what you did each day
- Watch one metric

## Practice Challenge

1. Write your 14-day plan day by day
2. Schedule daily content, outreach, and follow-up
3. Choose the one metric to watch

**Example Quiz**

**Answer:** A — Small daily actions on one channel build momentum.

- A) One

- D) Only revenue

**Answer:** A — One metric keeps the campaign focused and honest.

### Module 3 · Step 2: The 5 Numbers That Matter

After launch, you can drown in metrics. Ignore most of them. Five numbers tell you whether the business is working.

Track these weekly: **visitors, leads, conversion, customers, and revenue.** Together they show the funnel from attention to money.

## Learning Objectives

- Track the five core numbers
- See the funnel from attention to revenue
- Review the numbers weekly
- Use the numbers to make decisions

## The Five Numbers

```text
The 5 numbers that matter:
1. Visitors   - attention reaching your page
2. Leads      - signals captured
3. Conversion - leads to customers (%)
4. Customers  - paying people
5. Revenue    - money in
```
- Each number is one funnel stage
- Together they show what to fix
- Five is enough to run the business

## Reading the Funnel

- Low visitors? Fix the channel.
- Low leads? Fix the offer or page.
- Low conversion? Fix trust or price.
- Low customers or revenue? Fix follow-up.
### The rule
- Find the weakest number
- Fix one stage at a time
- Re-measure next week

## The Numbers Prompt

```text
Help me set up a weekly dashboard with the 5 numbers:
visitors, leads, conversion %, customers, revenue for [business].
For each, tell me what a low number means and the one action
to fix it. Keep it simple.
```
- A dashboard keeps the numbers visible
- Each number has a fix
- Simple beats comprehensive

## Key Takeaways

- Track five numbers: visitors, leads, conversion, customers, revenue
- Each number is one funnel stage
- Find the weakest number and fix one stage at a time
- Review the dashboard weekly
- Five numbers are enough to run the business

## Practice Challenge

1. Set up your five-number dashboard
2. Record this week's numbers
3. Identify the weakest number and its fix

**Example Quiz**

**Answer:** A — The five numbers map the funnel from attention to money.

2. What does low conversion mean?
- A) Fix trust or price
- B) Fix the channel

- D) Nothing

**Answer:** A — Low conversion points to trust or pricing issues.

3. How often should you review the numbers?
- A) Weekly
- B) Yearly
- C) Never
- D) Only at launch

**Answer:** A — A weekly review keeps the funnel visible and fixable.

4. How many stages should you fix at a time?
- A) One
- B) All at once
- C) Five
- D) None

**Answer:** A — Fix the weakest stage one at a time and re-measure.

### Module 4 · Step 3: The Repeatable Sales Loop (First 3 Customers)

Your first three customers should not come from luck; they should come from a loop you can repeat. A sales loop is a defined way of finding, pitching, and closing.

The goal is not three sales - it is a repeatable process. If you can write down how you got each customer, you can get the next ten.

## Learning Objectives

- Build a repeatable sales loop
- Define find, pitch, and close steps
- Learn from your first three customers
- Turn luck into a process

## The Loop

- **Find:** where you identify likely customers
- **Pitch:** how you present the offer
- **Close:** how you ask for the sale and payment
### Why a loop
- Three sales is luck; a loop is a business
- Writing it down makes it repeatable
- It reveals what to improve

## Learning From the First Three

- Note how each customer found you
- Note what convinced each one
- Note what almost stopped each one
### The rule
- Do not chase one-off tactics
- Find the common thread
- Double down on what works

## The Sales Prompt

```text
Design a repeatable sales loop to get my first 3 customers
for [offer]: where to find them, a short pitch, how to close
and take payment, plus a follow-up sequence. Keep it manual
and simple for a solo founder.
```
- Manual and simple first
- Include payment
- Include follow-up

## Key Takeaways

- First three customers should come from a repeatable loop
- The loop: find, pitch, close
- Three sales is luck; a written loop is a business
- Learn the common thread from your first customers
- Keep it manual and simple first

## Practice Challenge

1. Write your find, pitch, and close steps
2. Track how each of your first customers arrived
3. Identify the common thread to double down on

**Example Quiz**

1. What are the three loop steps?
- A) Find, pitch, close
- B) Buy, build, sell
- C) Plan, do, review
- D) Post, wait, hope

**Answer:** A — A sales loop is find, pitch, and close, repeatable.

2. Why write the loop down?
- A) Three sales is luck; a written loop is a business
- B) To look organised
- C) It is required
- D) To impress investors

**Answer:** A — Writing the loop makes it repeatable and reveals what to improve.

3. What should you note about each first customer?
- A) How they found you, what convinced them, what almost stopped them
- B) Their birthday
- C) Their favourite colour
- D) Their shoe size

**Answer:** A — These notes reveal the common thread that actually works.

4. How should the first loop be built?
- A) Manual and simple
- B) Fully automated
- C) Complex
- D) Outsourced

**Answer:** A — Keep it manual and simple first, then automate later.

### Module 5 · Step 4: The Evidence Meeting (Fork in the Road)

After launch, hold an evidence meeting with yourself: what did the numbers and customers actually show? This is the fork in the road.

Based on evidence, choose one path: **double down, adjust, or stop.** The decision is the point of the whole 90-day runway.

## Learning Objectives

- Hold an evidence-based review
- Choose double down, adjust, or stop
- Decide from data, not mood
- Commit to the next 90 days

## The Evidence Review

- What did the five numbers show?
- What did customers actually do?
- What surprised you, good and bad?
### Why
- Evidence beats mood
- The runway exists to produce this decision
- Honesty now saves months later

## The Fork

- **Double down:** evidence supports the idea - invest more
- **Adjust:** demand exists but the offer, price, or channel is off - pivot those
- **Stop:** no money behaviour and no channel - end it cleanly
### The rule
- Decide on the numbers, not feelings
- Write the reasons
- Commit to the next 90 days

## The Evidence Prompt

```text
Help me run an evidence meeting: here are my 5 numbers
[paste] and customer notes [paste]. Assess whether to double
down, adjust, or stop. List the evidence for each option and
the honest recommendation.
```
- Paste the real numbers
- Ask for evidence per option
- Demand an honest recommendation

## Key Takeaways

- Hold an evidence meeting after launch
- Choose double down, adjust, or stop
- Decide from numbers, not mood
- Write the reasons for the call
- Commit to the next 90 days

## Practice Challenge

1. Gather your five numbers and customer notes
2. Run the evidence meeting
3. Write your decision and three reasons

**Example Quiz**

1. What is the point of the evidence meeting?
- A) To choose double down, adjust, or stop
- B) To celebrate
- C) To raise money
- D) To hire

**Answer:** A — The meeting turns runway evidence into a clear decision.

2. What should the decision be based on?
- A) The numbers and customer behaviour
- B) Your mood
- C) Your friends' advice
- D) The weather

**Answer:** A — Decide on data, not feelings - write the reasons down.

3. What does 'adjust' mean?
- A) Demand exists but the offer, price, or channel is off
- B) Stop everything
- C) Double the budget
- D) Change nothing

**Answer:** A — Adjust means pivoting the offer, price, or channel while keeping the demand.

4. What do you commit to after the meeting?
- A) The next 90 days
- B) Nothing
- C) A new logo
- D) A longer runway

**Answer:** A — Commit to the next 90 days based on the decision.

### Module 6 · Step 5: The Weekly Founder Dashboard

The final system is a weekly founder dashboard: a single page that shows the five numbers, the week's actions, and the one decision you are facing.

Reviewing it every week turns running a business from guesswork into a habit. What gets measured gets improved.

## Learning Objectives

- Build a one-page weekly dashboard
- Track the five numbers and actions
- Review weekly without fail
- Turn running the business into a habit

## The Dashboard

- The five numbers, updated weekly
- The week's actions and what shipped
- The one decision you are facing
### Why one page
- One page gets used
- It forces clarity
- It closes the loop each week

## The Weekly Review

- Same day and time every week
- Read the numbers, note the trend
- Pick one action for the coming week
### The rule
- Never skip the review
- One decision at a time
- Progress compounds weekly

## The Dashboard Prompt

```text
Design a one-page weekly founder dashboard for [business]:
the 5 numbers (visitors, leads, conversion, customers, revenue),
a place for weekly actions, and one decision. Give me a short
weekly review checklist.
```
- One page, five numbers
- A weekly review checklist
- A place for the one decision

## Key Takeaways

- Build a one-page weekly founder dashboard
- Track the five numbers, actions, and one decision
- Review on the same day and time each week
- Never skip the review - what gets measured gets improved
- Turn running the business into a compounding habit

### Module 1 · The Business Model Is Your Operating Manual

Before building anything, decide how money moves. A one-page business model prevents building an unfundable gadget - a product nobody can pay for in a way that keeps you alive.

AI compresses the framing work; you still make the calls. The model is not a document for investors - it is your operating manual, the reference you test against reality.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why the model comes before the build
- Treat the one-page model as an operating manual
- Use AI for framing, keep the decisions
- Prepare the model to test in later missions

## Decide How Money Moves

- Before building, define how revenue and costs work
- A one-page model prevents an unfundable gadget
- Money movement is the core design decision
### Why first
- Building without a model wastes the runway
- The model guides scope, pricing, and channel
- It is cheap to change on paper, expensive in code

## AI Frames, You Decide

- AI compresses the framing work
- You make the calls on customer, price, and channel
- The model stays short and legible
### The division of labour
- AI drafts and asks clarifying questions
- You supply judgement and local knowledge
- You own every assumption

## The Operating Manual

```text
The model is your operating manual:
1. Customer - who pays
2. Problem - what hurts them
3. Solution - what you sell and how AI helps
4. Revenue - how money comes in
5. Costs - what delivery takes
6. Channel - where they find you
7. Edge - why you vs alternatives
```
- Seven blocks, one page
- Legible enough to test in Mission 8
- Every later decision references it

## Key Takeaways

- Decide how money moves before building anything
- A one-page model prevents an unfundable product
- AI frames; you make the calls
- The model is an operating manual, not an investor document
- Keep it short, legible, and testable

### Module 2 · Step 1: One-Page Business Model (AI-Assisted)

Use a lean canvas or the seven-block summary: customer, problem, solution, revenue, costs, channel, and edge. Each block is one or two lines, in your customer's language where possible.

The rule is **results format**: write it small and legible, because you will test it in Mission 8.

## Learning Objectives

- Fill the seven model blocks
- Use a prompt that asks clarifying questions first
- Write the model in results format
- Carry it forward to testing

## The Seven Blocks

- **Customer** - who pays (from Mission 2)
- **Problem** - what hurts them, in their words
- **Solution** - what you sell and how AI makes you cheaper, faster, or sharper
- **Revenue** - how money comes in
- **Costs** - what it actually takes to deliver
- **Channel** - where they find you
- **Edge** - why you versus the alternatives
### The point
- One page, seven blocks, no filler
- Legible enough to test
- Each block is a decision, not a paragraph

## The Model Prompt

```text
Help me fill a one-page business model for [idea]
[paste validation notes]. Cover customer, problem, solution,
revenue, costs, channel, edge. Ask me 5 clarifying questions
instead of guessing. Then draft the one-pager.
```
- Clarifying questions prevent AI from inventing facts
- Validation notes ground the model in reality
- The draft is a starting point you edit

## Results Format

- Write it small and legible
- Use the customer's own words in the problem block
- Keep assumptions visible so you can test them
### The payoff
- A model you can hold in your head
- A model you can test quickly
- A model that changes as evidence arrives

## Key Takeaways

- Fill seven blocks: customer, problem, solution, revenue, costs, channel, edge
- Ask AI for clarifying questions before it drafts
- Use customer language in the problem block
- Write in results format - small and legible
- You will test the model in Mission 8

### Module 3 · Step 2: Pick a Pricing Strategy (Not a Guess)

Pricing is a strategy decision, not a guess. There are four sane options for a lean startup: one-time, subscription, usage-based, and package/retainer.

**Pricing truths:** anchor tests beat opinions, the easiest price to charge is the one you can explain in one sentence, and raising later is hard while starting lower but clearly framed is fine.

## Learning Objectives

- Compare the four pricing models
- Choose one that fits your revenue goal
- Test price with anchors, not opinions
- Explain your price in one sentence

## The Four Options

- **One-time (product/gig):** simple, but needs constant new customers
- **Subscription (monthly):** predictable cash, must keep proving value
- **Usage-based (pay-per-result):** ties price to value; good for automation
- **Package/retainer (services):** fastest cash for skill-based offers
### Choosing
- Match the model to how value is delivered
- Match it to your cash needs
- There is no universally best model

## The Pricing Prompt

```text
For [solution] serving [customer], compare one-time vs
subscription vs usage pricing with realistic example prices
in [currency/market]. Which fits my revenue goal of [X]/month
given [how often they would use it]? List the risks of each.
```
- Realistic local prices ground the comparison
- Ask which fits your revenue goal
- Ask for the risks of each

## Pricing Truths

- Anchor tests beat opinions - 'would you pay?' measures little
- The easiest price to charge is the one you can explain in one sentence
- Raising later is hard; starting lower but clearly framed is fine
### The discipline
- Price where behaviour was observed (pre-orders, competitors)
- Write your one-sentence 'why this price'
- Validate with your Mission 2 customers

## Key Takeaways

- Four models: one-time, subscription, usage-based, package/retainer
- Choose the model that fits how value is delivered
- Anchor tests beat opinions
- Explain your price in one sentence
- Raising later is hard; starting clearly framed is fine

## Practice Challenge

1. Compare the four pricing models for your solution
2. Choose one and write your one-sentence 'why this price'
3. Validate the price with Mission 2 customers

**Example Quiz**

**Answer:** A — Subscription revenue is predictable, but you must keep proving value.

**Answer:** A — Anchor tests and observed behaviour (pre-orders, competitor prices) beat opinions.

### Module 4 · Step 3: Cashflow Before You Spend

Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. Start with a simple cashflow map: what you must spend before the first rupee/dollar comes in, and what you can defer or avoid.

**Cash rules:** spend only on what proves demand, avoid recurring costs until revenue exists, and prefer pay-as-you-go over subscriptions.

## Learning Objectives

- Map money out before money in
- Separate essential from deferrable spending
- Avoid recurring costs before revenue
- Use AI to sanity-check your cashflow

## The Cashflow Map

- Money out before money in is the danger zone
- List every cost before first revenue
- Separate essential from deferrable
### The essentials
- What directly proves demand
- What you legally must have
- Nothing else yet

## Cash Rules

- Spend only on what proves demand
- Avoid recurring costs until revenue exists
- Prefer pay-as-you-go over subscriptions
### Why
- Recurring costs outlive failed experiments
- Cash runway is your true timeline
- Frugality buys learning time

## The Cashflow Prompt

```text
Build a simple month-by-month cashflow map for [idea]
for 3 months: my costs before revenue, recurring vs one-time,
and what I can defer or avoid. Use [currency]. Flag anything
I should not spend on yet.
```
- Three months is enough to plan the runway
- Flag what to defer
- Keep it simple and honest

## Key Takeaways

- Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact
- Map money out before money in
- Spend only on what proves demand
- Avoid recurring costs until revenue exists
- Prefer pay-as-you-go; use AI to sanity-check the plan

## Practice Challenge

1. List every cost before your first revenue
2. Mark each as essential or deferrable
3. Build a three-month cashflow map

**Example Quiz**

1. What is the danger zone?
- A) Money out before money in
- B) High profit
- C) Low costs
- D) Fast growth

**Answer:** A — Spending before the first revenue arrives is what kills young ventures.

2. Which cost should you avoid before revenue?
- A) Recurring subscriptions
- B) One-time essentials
- C) Legal registration
- D) Demand tests

**Answer:** A — Recurring costs outlive failed experiments - avoid them until revenue exists.

3. Why is frugality useful?
- A) Cash runway is your true timeline
- B) It looks good
- C) It is required
- D) It impresses investors

**Answer:** A — Frugality buys you more learning time before the cash runs out.

4. What should AI do for your cashflow?
- A) Sanity-check the map and flag what to defer
- B) Spend the money
- C) Set prices
- D) Sign contracts

**Answer:** A — Use AI to check the plan and flag anything you should not spend on yet.

### Module 5 · Step 4: The Build / Buy / AI-Automate Decision

For every part of your business, decide: build it yourself, buy a tool, or have AI automate it. This is a leverage decision, not a purity test.

**Default rule: automate or buy the boring, build only the magic.** Your edge should get your hours; everything else should be rented or automated.

## Learning Objectives

- Apply the build/buy/automate lens to each task
- Identify your magic versus the boring
- Match each task to the cheapest viable option
- Record the decisions in your model

## The Three Lenses

- **Build:** only for your magic - the thing customers pay for and you uniquely do
- **Buy:** for anything standard - tools, templates, services
- **Automate with AI:** for repeatable admin, content, and follow-up
### The test
- Does this task create the edge? Build it.
- Is it standard? Buy it.
- Is it repetitive? Automate it.

## The Default Rule

- Automate or buy the boring
- Build only the magic
- Your edge gets your hours
### Why
- Building boring things burns runway
- Rented tools are faster than homemade ones
- Focus is the scarce resource

## The Decision Prompt

```text
For each task in [business], label Build / Buy / AI-Automate
for a solo founder: [list tasks]. For each, give the cheapest
viable option and a one-line reason. Flag anything that is my
real edge and should stay in-house.
```
- Label every task
- Ask for the cheapest viable option
- Protect the real edge

## Key Takeaways

- Decide build, buy, or automate for every task
- Automate or buy the boring; build only the magic
- Your edge should get your hours
- Rented tools are usually faster than homemade ones
- Record the decisions in your one-page model

## Practice Challenge

1. List your business tasks
2. Label each Build, Buy, or AI-Automate
3. Flag the tasks that are your real edge

**Example Quiz**

1. What should you build yourself?
- A) Only your magic - the unique edge
- B) Everything
- C) Nothing
- D) Standard tools

**Answer:** A — Build only what customers pay for and you uniquely do.

2. What should you do with boring, repetitive tasks?
- A) Automate or buy them
- B) Build them
- C) Ignore them
- D) Outsource your edge

**Answer:** A — Automate or buy the boring so your hours go to the magic.

3. What is the scarce resource?
- A) Focus
- B) Money
- C) Tools
- D) Ideas

**Answer:** A — Your hours and focus are scarce - spend them on the edge.

4. What should the decision prompt protect?
- A) The real edge that stays in-house
- B) Your budget
- C) Your logo
- D) Your schedule

**Answer:** A — Flag anything that is your real edge and keep it in-house.

### Module 6 · Step 5: Unit Economics for Your First Goal

Unit economics answer one question: does each sale make money, or lose it? Keep it simple - price per unit, cost per unit, contribution margin, and the number of units to hit your first goal.

Do not fake precision. A rough, honest calculation is better than a spreadsheet of guesses.

## Learning Objectives

- Calculate price, cost, and contribution margin
- Work out units needed for your first goal
- Keep the numbers rough and honest
- Check whether each sale makes money

## The Four Numbers

- **Price per unit** - what the customer pays
- **Cost per unit** - what delivery costs (including AI/tool costs)
- **Contribution margin** - price minus cost
- **Units to goal** - your monthly goal divided by margin
### Why these
- They show if each sale makes money
- They show what must be true to hit the goal
- They are simple enough to update

## The Unit Econ Prompt

```text
Help me calculate simple unit economics for [offer]:
price [X], cost per unit [Y], fixed monthly costs [Z].
Show contribution margin, break-even units, and the number
of customers for [goal]. Use [currency]. Keep it rough and honest.
```
- Rough beats fake precision
- Break-even units is the key output
- Recheck as prices and costs change

## Honesty Over Precision

- Do not fake precision with guesses
- A rough honest calculation guides decisions
- Update it when reality arrives
### The payoff
- You know if each sale makes money
- You know what must be true
- You can spot a broken model early

## Key Takeaways

- Unit economics ask whether each sale makes money
- Four numbers: price, cost, contribution margin, units to goal
- Break-even units is the key output
- Keep it rough and honest, not falsely precise
- Update the numbers when reality arrives

## Practice Challenge

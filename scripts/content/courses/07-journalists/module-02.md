# Mission 2: Research & Fact-Checking with AI

---
### Module 1 · AI as a Research Assistant, Not a Source

AI can point you toward leads and explain background quickly, but it cannot stand behind a claim. **AI is a research assistant, never a source you can cite.**

The distinction matters because AI can invent citations, misattribute quotes, and present old information as current. Your verification, not the tool's confidence, is what makes a story publishable.

## Learning Objectives

- Treat AI as a research assistant, not a source
- Understand why AI citations cannot be trusted
- Use AI to find leads and background
- Keep verification as the reporter's job

## Assistant, Not Source

- AI points to leads; it does not prove them
- It cannot be cited as a source
- Its confidence is not evidence
### Why
- It invents citations
- It misattributes quotes
- It presents stale facts as current

## What AI Is Good For

- Explaining background and jargon fast
- Suggesting search terms and angles
- Summarising documents you already trust
- Listing who to contact
### The rule
- Use AI to find leads
- Use primary sources to prove them
- Keep verification human

## The Research Prompt

```text
Act as a research assistant. For [topic], list background
context, 10 search terms, likely primary sources, and the
people or institutions to contact. Flag anything that must
be verified before use.
```
- Ask for primary sources
- Ask for contacts
- Flag what needs verification

## Key Takeaways

- AI is a research assistant, never a citable source
- AI invents citations and misattributes quotes
- Use AI for background, search terms, and contacts
- Use primary sources to prove every claim
- Verification stays the reporter's job

## Practice Challenge

1. Pick a story you are working on
2. Ask AI for 10 search terms and likely primary sources
3. Verify two AI suggestions against real sources

**Example Quiz**

**Answer:** A — AI points to leads; primary sources prove them.

2. Why can't you cite AI?

3. What should AI help you find?

**Answer:** A — Use AI for leads and background, then verify with primary sources.

4. Who verifies the story?
- A) The reporter
- B) The AI

- D) The algorithm

**Answer:** A — Verification remains the reporter's responsibility.

### Module 2 · Step 1: Scope the Question

Good fact-checking starts with a sharp question. Vague topics produce vague research; a precise question produces verifiable answers.

Write the claim you need to check as a single, testable sentence. Then decide what evidence would confirm or refute it.

## Learning Objectives

- Turn a topic into a testable question
- Define confirming and refuting evidence
- Narrow scope to what matters
- Prepare the research plan

## From Topic to Question

- A topic is broad; a question is testable
- Write the claim as one sentence
- Make it specific enough to check
### Why
- Vague questions produce vague research
- A testable claim has a clear answer
- Focus saves hours

## Defining Evidence

- What would confirm the claim?
- What would refute it?
- Who holds the authoritative record?
### The rule
- Name the evidence before searching
- Prefer primary, official sources
- Note the date of every source

## The Scoping Prompt

```text
Turn this claim into a testable fact-check question: [claim].
List what evidence would confirm it, what would refute it,
and the authoritative primary sources to check first.
```
- One claim per question
- Confirm and refute both defined
- Primary sources first

## Key Takeaways

- Turn a topic into one testable claim
- Define what evidence would confirm and refute it
- Identify who holds the authoritative record
- Prefer primary, official sources
- Note the date of every source

## Practice Challenge

1. Write a claim you need to check as one sentence
2. List confirming and refuting evidence
3. Name the authoritative source for each

**Example Quiz**

1. What makes a good fact-check question?
- A) It is specific and testable
- B) It is broad
- C) It is rhetorical
- D) It is long

**Answer:** A — A precise, testable claim has a clear answer.

2. What should you define before searching?
- A) Confirming and refuting evidence
- B) The headline
- C) The deadline
- D) The word count

**Answer:** A — Naming the evidence in advance focuses the research.

3. Which sources come first?
- A) Primary, official sources
- B) Social media

**Answer:** A — Prefer primary, official records over secondary accounts.

4. What must you note about every source?

### Module 3 · Step 2: Find Leads and Background Fast

AI speeds up the early hunt: background explainers, search terms, and lists of who to contact. The goal is a short list of leads worth chasing, not a finished story.

Use AI to cover ground quickly, then switch to primary sources for anything you will publish.

## Learning Objectives

- Generate background and search terms quickly
- Build a contact and source list
- Separate leads from verified facts
- Move fast without cutting corners

## Covering Ground Fast

- Ask for a plain-language background brief
- Ask for search terms and synonyms
- Ask for the key institutions and people
### Why
- The early hunt is time-consuming
- AI covers the basics in seconds
- You focus on the hard verification

## Building a Source List

- Institutions that hold the records
- Experts with relevant credentials
- People directly affected
### The rule
- A source list is a starting point
- Verify credentials, not claims
- Never publish an AI-suggested quote

## The Leads Prompt

```text
For [story], give me a background brief, 10 search terms,
and a source list grouped by institutions, experts, and
affected people. Mark each as a lead to verify, not a fact.
```
- Group sources by type
- Mark everything as a lead
- Verify before use

## Key Takeaways

- Use AI to cover background ground quickly
- Generate search terms, institutions, and contacts
- A source list is a starting point, not proof
- Verify credentials, not claims
- Never publish an AI-suggested quote

### Module 4 · Step 3: Verify Every Claim (The Two-Source Rule)

Verification is the heart of the job. The two-source rule is the standard: no significant claim goes to print without at least two independent, authoritative sources.

Independence matters - two outlets repeating the same wire story are not two sources. Trace every claim back to its origin.

## Learning Objectives

- Apply the two-source rule
- Test whether sources are truly independent
- Trace claims to their origin
- Document the verification

## The Two-Source Rule

- No significant claim without two independent sources
- Prefer authoritative, primary sources
- Keep the evidence for each claim
### Why
- One source can be wrong or lying
- Independence guards against shared error
- Documentation protects you later

## Testing Independence

- Two outlets citing the same wire = one source
- Two people with the same interest = weak
- Trace the claim to its origin
### The rule
- Independence is about origin, not count
- Beware circular reporting
- Confirm with a primary record

## The Verification Prompt

```text
Help me verify [claim]. List the primary records and
independent sources that would confirm or refute it,
and flag any circular reporting I should avoid.
```
- Ask for primary records
- Ask for independence
- Flag circular reporting

## Key Takeaways

- No significant claim without two independent sources
- Independence is about origin, not count
- Trace every claim to its origin
- Beware circular reporting from a shared wire
- Document the evidence for each claim

## Practice Challenge

1. Take a claim from your current story
2. Find two independent, authoritative sources
3. Document the evidence and any circular reporting you avoided

**Example Quiz**

1. What is the two-source rule?
- A) Two independent, authoritative sources per significant claim
- B) Two social posts
- C) Two quotes
- D) Two headlines

**Answer:** A — Significant claims need at least two independent, authoritative sources.

2. Are two outlets citing the same wire two sources?
- A) No - that is one source
- B) Yes
- C) Sometimes
- D) Only online

**Answer:** A — Independence is about origin; a shared wire is a single source.

3. What is circular reporting?
- A) Sources repeating the same origin as if independent
- B) A round-up story

- D) A correction

**Answer:** A — Circular reporting creates false confidence from one origin.

4. Why document verification?

- B) It is required by law
- C) It fills the file
- D) It impresses sources

**Answer:** A — Keeping the evidence supports the story and defends it later.

### Module 5 · Step 4: Spot AI Hallucinations and Fake Citations

AI hallucinations are confident, plausible, and wrong. They include invented studies, fake quotes, and citations to sources that do not exist.

Learn the warning signs and always check that a cited source exists and says what the AI claims.

## Learning Objectives

- Recognise common hallucination types
- Spot fake or misattributed citations
- Verify that sources exist and match
- Build a checking habit

## Common Hallucinations

- Invented studies, statistics, or reports
- Fake or misattributed quotes
- Citations to sources that do not exist
- Outdated facts stated as current
### Why they happen
- AI predicts plausible text
- It has no ground truth
- Confidence is not correctness

## Spotting Fake Citations

- Open every cited source
- Check the author, date, and title exist
- Confirm the source says what is claimed
### The rule
- No source opened = no source trusted
- If it cannot be found, it does not count
- Cross-check with an archive

## The Hallucination-Check Prompt

```text
For each fact and citation in this draft, tell me how to
verify it independently and what a fake or misattributed
source would look like. Then flag the riskiest claims.
```
- A verification method per claim
- Describe the failure mode
- Flag the riskiest claims

## Key Takeaways

- AI hallucinations are confident, plausible, and wrong
- Watch for invented studies, fake quotes, and nonexistent citations
- Open every cited source and confirm it exists
- No source opened means no source trusted
- Flag the riskiest claims for the closest checking

## Practice Challenge

1. Ask AI for three citations on a topic
2. Try to open each one and verify it exists
3. Note which were real and which were fabricated

**Example Quiz**

1. What is an AI hallucination?
- A) A confident, plausible, wrong claim
- B) A correct fact
- C) A verified quote
- D) A primary source

**Answer:** A — Hallucinations are confident but wrong, including fake citations.

2. What is the rule for citations?
- A) No source opened, no source trusted
- B) Trust the AI
- C) Count the citations
- D) Publish if it sounds right

**Answer:** A — Open every cited source and confirm it exists and matches.

3. Which is a hallucination type?
- A) Invented studies and fake quotes
- B) A real report
- C) A press release
- D) An official record

**Answer:** A — Invented studies, fake quotes, and nonexistent citations are common.

4. What should you flag for closest checking?
- A) The riskiest claims
- B) The shortest ones
- C) The oldest ones
- D) The ones you like

**Answer:** A — Flag the riskiest claims so the closest verification goes where it matters.

### Module 6 · Step 5: The Fact-Check Log

A fact-check log records every claim, its sources, and the verification status. It is your evidence trail and your protection.

The log also speeds up corrections and follow-ups, because you already know where every fact came from.

## Learning Objectives

- Keep a fact-check log for every story
- Record claims, sources, and status
- Use the log for corrections and follow-ups
- Make verification auditable

## What the Log Holds

- Each claim, stated precisely
- The sources used for it
- The verification status (verified, unverified, disputed)
- Notes on any uncertainty
### Why
- It is your evidence trail
- It protects against error and complaint
- It makes corrections faster

## Building the Log

```text
Create a fact-check log template for a newsroom story:
columns for claim, source(s), independence, date, status,
and notes. Keep it simple and usable at deadline.
```
- One row per claim
- Independence and date columns
- A status column

## Using the Log

- Update it as you report
- Never publish a claim marked unverified
- Reuse it for corrections and follow-ups
### The payoff
- Auditable verification
- Fewer corrections
- Faster follow-ups

## Key Takeaways

- Keep a fact-check log for every story
- Record each claim, its sources, and its status
- Never publish a claim marked unverified
- The log is your evidence trail and protection
- Reuse it for corrections and follow-ups

## Practice Challenge

**Example Quiz**

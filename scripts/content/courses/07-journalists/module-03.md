### Module 1 · The Story Architecture: News vs Feature

Stories have architecture. A news story front-loads the most important facts; a feature builds a narrative arc. Knowing which you are writing changes how you structure and how AI can help.

Match the structure to the story type before you draft, and the writing becomes far easier.

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish news structure from feature structure
- Match structure to story type
- Apply the right architecture before drafting
- Use AI to scaffold the structure

## News Structure

- Inverted pyramid: most important first
- Lede answers who, what, when, where, why
- Details and context follow in order of importance
### Why
- Readers may stop at any point
- Editors cut from the bottom
- The key facts must survive any cut

## Feature Structure

- A narrative arc with a beginning, middle, and end
- Characters, scenes, and tension
- The nut graf explains why it matters
### Why
- Features reward readers who stay
- Emotion and detail carry the story
- Structure creates momentum

## The Architecture Prompt

```text
I am writing a [news/feature] story about [topic].
Give me the right structure, the sections it needs,
and what each section must accomplish.
```
- Name the story type first
- Ask for sections and their jobs
- Scaffold before drafting

## Key Takeaways

- News uses the inverted pyramid; features use a narrative arc
- Match structure to story type before drafting
- The news lede answers who, what, when, where, why
- The feature nut graf explains why the story matters
- Use AI to scaffold structure, then write it yourself

## Practice Challenge

1. Choose a story you are working on
2. Decide if it is news or feature
3. Write the section scaffold it needs

**Example Quiz**

1. What structure does news use?
- A) The inverted pyramid
- B) A narrative arc

- D) A dialogue

**Answer:** A — News front-loads the most important facts in the inverted pyramid.

2. What does the news lede answer?
- A) Who, what, when, where, why
- B) Only who
- C) Only why
- D) Only when

**Answer:** A — The lede answers the core questions up front.

3. What does a feature nut graf do?
- A) Explains why the story matters
- B) Lists sources
- C) Gives the weather
- D) Ends the story

**Answer:** A — The nut graf tells readers why the story matters and why now.

4. Why match structure to type first?
- A) It makes drafting far easier
- B) It is required
- C) It impresses editors
- D) It fills space

**Answer:** A — Choosing the right architecture before drafting streamlines the writing.

### Module 2 · Step 1: Find the Angle

The angle is the specific slice of a topic that makes the story worth reading. Without one, you have a subject, not a story.

AI can help you generate candidate angles, but you choose the one that is true, useful, and new.

## Learning Objectives

- Turn a topic into a specific angle
- Generate candidate angles with AI
- Choose the angle that is true and new
- Test the angle before drafting

## Topic vs Angle

- A topic is broad; an angle is specific
- The angle is the story's point of view
- Without one, the piece wanders
### Why
- Readers want a reason to care
- A clear angle focuses reporting
- It shapes the lede and structure

## Generating Angles

```text
Give me 10 possible angles for a story about [topic]
for [audience]. For each, state the one-sentence point
and why it matters now. Flag which need more reporting.
```
- Ask for ten, choose one
- Demand a one-sentence point each
- Flag what needs reporting

## Choosing the Angle

- Is it true and supportable?
- Is it new or useful to readers?
- Can you report it fully?
### The rule
- Reject angles you cannot support
- Prefer the specific over the general
- Test it on a colleague

## Key Takeaways

- An angle is the specific slice that makes a story worth reading
- Generate candidate angles, then choose one
- Demand a one-sentence point for each angle
- Reject angles you cannot support with reporting
- Prefer the specific over the general

## Practice Challenge

1. Write your topic as a broad subject
2. Generate ten angles and pick one
3. State its point in one sentence

**Example Quiz**

1. What is an angle?
- A) The specific slice that makes the story worth reading
- B) The topic
- C) The headline
- D) The source list

**Answer:** A — The angle is the story's specific point of view.

2. What should each candidate angle include?
- A) A one-sentence point and why it matters now
- B) A full draft
- C) A source list
- D) A headline

**Answer:** A — Ask for a one-sentence point and its timeliness for each angle.

3. When should you reject an angle?
- A) When you cannot support it with reporting
- B) When it is specific
- C) When it is new
- D) When readers care

**Answer:** A — Reject angles you cannot fully report and support.

4. What should you prefer?
- A) The specific over the general
- B) The general
- C) The vague
- D) The popular

**Answer:** A — Specific angles are more compelling and easier to report.

### Module 3 · Step 2: The Outline That Holds

An outline is the story's skeleton. A good outline holds the facts in a logical order and shows where the evidence goes.

AI can draft an outline fast; you adjust it to fit the reporting you actually have.

## Learning Objectives

- Build an outline that fits the story type
- Place evidence in the outline
- Spot gaps before drafting
- Adapt the outline to the reporting

## The Skeleton

- Sections in a logical order
- The point of each section
- Where the key evidence goes
### Why
- An outline prevents wandering
- It reveals gaps early
- It speeds up drafting

## Placing Evidence

- Each claim maps to a section
- Each section has its supporting sources
- Unsupported sections are flagged
### The rule
- No claim without a home
- No section without evidence
- Gaps get reported, not filled with guesses

## The Outline Prompt

```text
Build an outline for my [news/feature] story on [angle].
For each section, give its purpose and the evidence it
needs. Flag any section where I may lack reporting.
```
- Purpose per section
- Evidence per section
- Flag reporting gaps

## Key Takeaways

- An outline holds facts in a logical order
- Give each section a purpose and its evidence
- Flag gaps before drafting
- Adapt the outline to the reporting you actually have
- No claim without a home; no section without evidence

## Practice Challenge

1. Outline your current story section by section
2. Map the evidence to each section
3. List the gaps you still need to report

**Example Quiz**

1. What is an outline?
- A) The story's skeleton in logical order
- B) The final draft
- C) The headline
- D) The source list

**Answer:** A — An outline orders the facts and shows where evidence goes.

2. What should each section have?
- A) A purpose and its evidence
- B) A headline
- C) A quote
- D) A picture

**Answer:** A — Give each section a purpose and the evidence it needs.

3. What do gaps in the outline mean?
- A) They need reporting, not guessing
- B) The story is done
- C) Cut the section
- D) Publish anyway

**Answer:** A — Flag gaps and report them rather than filling with guesses.

4. When should you adapt the outline?
- A) To fit the reporting you actually have
- B) Never
- C) After publication
- D) Only if forced

**Answer:** A — Adjust the outline to match real reporting, not wishes.

### Module 4 · Step 3: Draft Fast, Edit Slow

Separate drafting from editing. Draft fast to get the story on the page, then edit slow to make it accurate, clear, and tight.

AI is a drafting accelerator, not an editor of record. Use it to get words down, then apply your own judgment to every sentence.

## Learning Objectives

- Separate drafting from editing
- Use AI to draft quickly
- Edit slowly for accuracy and clarity
- Keep editorial judgment human

## Draft Fast

- Get the story on the page without polishing
- Use the outline as the guide
- Let AI produce a rough first pass if it helps
### Why
- Perfectionism stalls drafting
- A rough draft is easier to improve
- Speed frees time for editing

## Edit Slow

- Check every fact against the log
- Tighten every sentence
- Read it aloud for rhythm and clarity
### The rule
- Editing is where quality is made
- Never publish an unedited AI draft
- You are the editor of record

## The Drafting Prompt

```text
Using this outline [paste] and these verified facts [paste],
write a rough first draft in plain news style. Mark every
place that needs a fact-check or a missing detail.
```
- Feed the outline and verified facts
- Ask it to mark uncertain spots
- Then edit it yourself

## Key Takeaways

- Separate drafting from editing
- Draft fast to get the story down
- Edit slow for accuracy and clarity
- Never publish an unedited AI draft
- You are the editor of record

## Practice Challenge

1. Draft your story fast from the outline
2. Edit it slowly against your fact-check log
3. Read it aloud and tighten every sentence

**Example Quiz**

1. Why separate drafting and editing?
- A) Drafting fast and editing slow produces better work
- B) It is required
- C) It saves ink
- D) It looks organised

**Answer:** A — Separating the two speeds drafting and improves quality.

2. What is AI's role in drafting?
- A) A drafting accelerator, not an editor of record
- B) The final editor
- C) The fact-checker
- D) The publisher

**Answer:** A — Use AI to get words down; you edit and own the result.

3. What happens in the edit?
- A) Quality is made - accuracy, clarity, tightness
- B) Facts are invented
- C) Sources are chosen
- D) Deadlines are set

**Answer:** A — Editing is where accuracy and clarity are made.

4. Can you publish an unedited AI draft?
- A) No
- B) Yes
- C) Sometimes
- D) Only online

**Answer:** A — Never publish an unedited AI draft - you are the editor of record.

### Module 5 · Step 4: Headlines, Ledes, and Nut Grafs

Three elements decide whether a story gets read: the headline, the lede, and the nut graf. Each has a specific job.

AI is excellent at generating options; you choose the one that is accurate and compelling without overpromising.

## Learning Objectives

- Write headlines that are accurate and compelling
- Write ledes that answer the core questions
- Write nut grafs that explain why it matters
- Choose from AI-generated options

## The Headline

- Accurate first, compelling second
- No overpromising or clickbait
- Reflect the story's actual point
### Why
- The headline is the story's promise
- Breaking the promise breaks trust
- Accuracy is non-negotiable

## The Lede and Nut Graf

- Lede: the most important fact, up front
- Nut graf: why the story matters, and why now
- Together they hook and orient the reader
### The rule
- The lede earns attention
- The nut graf earns the read
- Both must be true

## The Headline Prompt

```text
Write 10 headline options for this story [paste], plus one
lede and one nut graf. Every option must be accurate - no
clickbait. Flag any that overstate the story.
```
- Ten options, choose one
- No clickbait
- Flag overstatement

## Key Takeaways

- The headline, lede, and nut graf decide whether a story gets read
- Headlines must be accurate first, compelling second
- The lede states the most important fact up front
- The nut graf explains why the story matters and why now
- Flag and reject any option that overstates the story

## Practice Challenge

1. Generate ten headlines and choose one
2. Write your lede and nut graf
3. Check both against the story for accuracy

**Example Quiz**

1. What comes first in a headline?

**Answer:** A — Headlines must be accurate first and compelling second.

2. What does the lede do?
- A) States the most important fact up front
- B) Explains why it matters
- C) Lists sources
- D) Ends the story

**Answer:** A — The lede earns attention by leading with the key fact.

3. What does the nut graf explain?
- A) Why the story matters and why now
- B) Who wrote it
- C) Where to subscribe
- D) How long it is

**Answer:** A — The nut graf tells readers why the story matters and why now.

4. What do you do with an overstated option?
- A) Reject it
- B) Publish it
- C) Soften the story
- D) Ignore the facts

**Answer:** A — Flag and reject options that overstate the story.

### Module 6 · Step 5: The Editing Pass (Cut, Clarify, Verify)

The final editing pass has three moves: cut what is unnecessary, clarify what is muddy, and verify what is uncertain.

Run it every time, in that order, before the story leaves your hands.

## Learning Objectives

- Run the cut, clarify, verify editing pass
- Cut unnecessary words and sections
- Clarify muddy passages
- Verify every remaining claim

## Cut

- Remove words that add nothing
- Cut sections that do not serve the angle
- Prefer the shorter word and sentence
### Why
- Tight writing is clearer
- Cuts reveal the story's core
- Readers reward brevity

## Clarify and Verify

- Rewrite muddy sentences
- Explain jargon for the audience
- Verify every claim against the log
### The rule
- Clarity is kindness to the reader
- Verification is duty to the truth
- Do both before publishing

## The Editing Prompt

```text
Act as a tough copy editor for this draft [paste].
First suggest cuts, then flag unclear passages, then list
every claim that still needs verification. Do not add facts.
```
- Cuts first, then clarity, then verification
- No new facts added
- You make the final call

## Key Takeaways

- The final pass is cut, clarify, verify
- Cut unnecessary words and sections first
- Clarify muddy passages and jargon
- Verify every remaining claim against the log
- Do not let AI add facts during editing

## Practice Challenge

1. Run the cut, clarify, verify pass on your draft
2. Cut at least ten percent of the words
3. Verify every remaining claim

**Example Quiz**

1. What are the three editing moves?
- A) Cut, clarify, verify
- B) Add, expand, repeat
- C) Write, post, share
- D) Plan, draft, publish

**Answer:** A — The final pass cuts, clarifies, and verifies.

2. What comes first in the pass?
- A) Cut
- B) Clarify
- C) Verify
- D) Publish

**Answer:** A — Cut unnecessary words and sections first.

3. What is clarity?
- A) Kindness to the reader
- B) A legal duty
- C) A style choice
- D) A headline rule

**Answer:** A — Clarity serves the reader by making the story easy to follow.

4. What should AI not do during editing?
- A) Add facts
- B) Suggest cuts
- C) Flag unclear passages
- D) List claims to verify

**Answer:** A — AI can suggest cuts and flags, but must not add facts.

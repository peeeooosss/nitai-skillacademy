### Module 1 · What AI Is (and Isn't) in the Newsroom

AI is not a source and not an editor. It is a very fast assistant that has read a great deal and follows instructions carefully. **You give the instructions, check the output, and own the result.**

In a newsroom the stakes are higher than in most jobs: a wrong claim is a correction, a libel risk, and a hit to trust. So treat AI as a drafting and organising tool, never as an authority.

## Learning Objectives

- Describe AI as an assistant, not a source
- Explain why journalists own the output
- Recognise where AI helps and where it harms
- Adopt a verification-first mindset

## An Assistant, Not an Authority

- AI has read a lot and follows instructions
- It does not know your beat, your sources, or your ethics
- It can be confidently wrong
### Why it matters
- Journalism runs on verified truth
- A confident error becomes a correction
- You are the responsible professional

## Where AI Helps

- Drafting, summarising, and outlining
- Translating and transcribing
- Brainstorming angles and headlines
### Where it harms
- Inventing quotes or citations
- Stating outdated facts as current
- Reproducing bias at scale

## The Verification-First Mindset

```text
Act as a newsroom AI coach. Explain, for a working
journalist, what AI can and cannot be trusted with,
and give 5 rules for using it without risking accuracy.
```
- Every AI claim is a lead, not a fact
- Verify before you publish
- Keep the human in the loop

## Key Takeaways

- AI is a fast assistant, not a source or an editor
- You own every word that gets published
- AI helps with drafting, summarising, translating, and ideation
- AI invents quotes, citations, and outdated facts
- Treat every AI claim as a lead to verify

### Module 2 · The Context–Task–Format Prompt Frame

Good prompts follow a simple three-part frame: **Context – Task – Format.** Tell the AI who you are and what the situation is, what you want done, and how you want the answer delivered.

Master this frame and you can apply it to almost every newsroom task - from a background brief to a headline set.

## Learning Objectives

- Use the Context–Task–Format frame
- Write prompts that produce usable output
- Iterate on a first draft
- Save prompts that work

## The Three Parts

- **Context:** who you are, the beat, the audience, the constraints
- **Task:** the exact job to do
- **Format:** headings, bullets, word count, tone
### Why it works
- Input quality decides output quality
- Structure makes output usable
- Clarity reduces editing time

## A Worked Example

```text
Context: I am a reporter covering local government for a
regional daily. Audience: general readers.
Task: summarise this 40-page council budget into the five
changes that most affect residents.
Format: five bullet points, one sentence each, plain English.
```
- Real context grounds the answer
- A specific task prevents drift
- A defined format makes it publishable-adjacent

## Iterating and Saving

- Treat the first answer as a draft
- Ask for revisions: shorter, sharper, more practical
- Save your best prompts as a personal toolkit
### The payoff
- Less time on routine drafting
- More consistency across stories
- A reusable newsroom asset

## Key Takeaways

- Use Context - Task - Format for every prompt
- Context names you, the beat, the audience, and constraints
- Format defines headings, bullets, length, and tone
- Treat the first answer as a draft and iterate
- Save the prompts that work as your toolkit

## Practice Challenge

### Module 3 · Building Your Newsroom Prompt Toolkit

The most valuable prompts are the ones you reuse. A prompt toolkit is a saved set of prompts for the tasks you do again and again: background briefs, interview prep, summaries, and headline options.

Build it once and your daily routine gets faster and more consistent without lowering your standards.

## Learning Objectives

- Identify your recurring newsroom tasks
- Turn each into a reusable prompt
- Organise the toolkit for quick access
- Improve prompts over time

## Which Tasks Recur

- Background briefs and explainers
- Interview question prep
- Summarising long documents
- Headline and social copy options
### Why start here
- Repetition is where time is lost
- Reuse multiplies the benefit
- Consistency builds reader trust

## Turning Tasks Into Prompts

```text
Help me build a reusable prompt for [recurring task].
Ask me 5 clarifying questions about my beat, audience,
and format, then write a fill-in-the-blank prompt template
I can reuse with different stories.
```
- Clarifying questions tailor the template
- Fill-in-the-blank makes it reusable
- One template per recurring task

## Organising and Improving

- Keep prompts in one document or notes app
- Name them by task, not by date
- Refine after each use
### The payoff
- A personal newsroom asset
- Faster routine work
- Higher, steadier quality

## Key Takeaways

- Build a reusable prompt for each recurring task
- Start with background briefs, interview prep, summaries, and headlines
- Use fill-in-the-blank templates
- Keep prompts in one place, named by task
- Refine prompts after each use

## Practice Challenge

1. List your five most repeated tasks
2. Write a reusable prompt for each
3. Store them in one document and refine after use

**Example Quiz**

### Module 4 · AI Tools for Reporters: What to Open

You do not need a dozen tools. Most newsroom AI work happens in a general chat assistant, a transcription tool, and a translation tool. Know what each is for and what each is bad at.

Pick tools you can use on your beat today, and check your organisation's policy before uploading any sensitive material.

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the core newsroom AI tool types
- Match tools to tasks
- Understand each tool's limits
- Check policy before uploading material

## The Core Tool Types

- **General chat assistant:** drafting, summarising, ideation
- **Transcription tool:** interviews, press conferences, recordings
- **Translation tool:** multilingual reporting and local-language audio
- **Research/verification aids:** search and archive tools
### The rule
- Few tools, used well
- Match the tool to the task
- Never upload sensitive material without policy clearance

## Matching Tool to Task

- Summarising a report? Chat assistant.
- Turning a 60-minute interview into notes? Transcription.
- Quoting a source in another language? Translation, then native check.
- Tracking a claim across sources? Search and archive aids.
### The habit
- Name the task first
- Then open the right tool
- Keep a human check at the end

## Limits and Policy

```text
Explain which AI tool types suit a reporter's daily tasks,
and for each, list its main limitation and the human check
required before publication. Include a note on sensitive data.
```
- Every tool has a failure mode
- Every output needs a human check
- Sensitive data needs clearance first

## Key Takeaways

- You need only a few tools: chat, transcription, translation
- Match the tool to the task, not the other way round
- Every tool has limits and a human check
- Never upload sensitive material without policy clearance
- Keep a human at the end of every workflow

## Practice Challenge

1. List the tools you will use and the task for each
2. Note each tool's main limitation
3. Check your organisation's AI policy before uploading anything

### Module 5 · The Ask–Check–Improve–Save Loop

Every AI task in this course follows the same loop: **Ask, Check, Improve, Save.** It turns a one-off prompt into a reliable newsroom habit.

The loop protects accuracy (Check), raises quality (Improve), and builds your toolkit (Save).

## Learning Objectives

- Run the Ask-Check-Improve-Save loop
- Explain why each step matters
- Apply the loop to a real task
- Make the loop automatic

## The Four Steps

- **Ask:** write a Context-Task-Format prompt
- **Check:** verify every fact, quote, and citation
- **Improve:** revise for accuracy, clarity, and tone
- **Save:** store the output and the prompt
### Why the loop
- Check is where accuracy is protected
- Improve is where quality rises
- Save is where time is banked

## Running the Loop

```text
Walk me through applying the Ask-Check-Improve-Save loop
to [task]. For the Check step, list the specific things
a journalist must verify before the output can be used.
```
- The loop applies to any task
- Check is task-specific
- Never skip Check under deadline

## Making It Automatic

- Practise the loop on small tasks first
- Soon it feels like second nature
- The loop is your safety net
### The payoff
- Faster work without losing accuracy
- A repeatable quality standard
- A growing personal toolkit

## Key Takeaways

- Every task follows Ask, Check, Improve, Save
- Check is where accuracy is protected
- Improve is where quality rises
- Save banks time for future stories
- Never skip Check, even under deadline

## Practice Challenge

1. Run the loop on one real task this week
2. Write down what you had to verify in the Check step
3. Save the prompt and output

**Example Quiz**

- A) Ask, Check, Improve, Save
- B) Plan, Do, Review, Stop
- C) Write, Edit, Post, Share
- D) Read, Think, Type, Send

**Answer:** A — The loop is Ask, Check, Improve, Save.

2. Which step protects accuracy?
- A) Check
- B) Ask
- C) Save
- D) Improve

**Answer:** A — Check is where every fact, quote, and citation is verified.

3. Which step banks time?
- A) Save
- B) Ask
- C) Check
- D) Improve

**Answer:** A — Saving the prompt and output builds a reusable toolkit.

4. When can you skip Check?
- A) Never
- B) Under deadline
- C) On small stories
- D) When AI is confident

**Answer:** A — Never skip Check - accuracy is the journalist's core duty.

### Module 6 · Newsroom Guardrails from Day One

Guardrails are the rules that keep AI use safe and ethical. Set them now, before the pressure of deadline, so good habits are automatic.

The core guardrails: verify everything, disclose when required, protect sources, and never let AI make a publishing decision.

## Learning Objectives

- State the core newsroom guardrails
- Apply them before deadline pressure
- Protect sources and sensitive data
- Keep publishing decisions human

## The Core Guardrails

- **Verify everything** AI produces
- **Disclose** AI use when your outlet or audience requires it
- **Protect sources** and never upload sensitive material carelessly
- **Keep publishing decisions human**
### Why now
- Deadline pressure erodes judgment
- Habits set early hold under stress
- Trust is hard to rebuild

## Protecting Sources

- Never paste a source's identity or private messages into a tool
- Anonymise before any AI processing
- Follow your outlet's data policy
### The rule
- Source safety is non-negotiable
- Anonymise first, process second
- When unsure, do not upload

## The Guardrails Prompt

```text
Draft a one-page AI guardrail checklist for a newsroom:
verification, disclosure, source protection, and human
sign-off. Keep it practical and usable at deadline.
```
- One page, usable at deadline
- Covers the four guardrails
- Reinforces human sign-off

## Key Takeaways

- Set guardrails before deadline pressure arrives
- Verify everything AI produces
- Disclose AI use when required
- Protect sources - anonymise before any AI processing
- Keep publishing decisions human

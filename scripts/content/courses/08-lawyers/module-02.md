# Mission 2: Drafting & Summarising Documents with AI

### Module 1 · Drafting as a First Draft, Not Final

AI drafting is a head start, not a finished document. It produces plausible language fast, but it does not know your client, your jurisdiction's latest amendments, or your firm's style.

The rule: **AI drafts, the lawyer finishes.** Every sentence that leaves your office is yours.

## Learning Objectives

- Treat AI drafting as a first draft
- Know what AI drafting misses
- Keep the lawyer as final author
- Build a drafting workflow

## A Head Start, Not the Finish

- AI produces plausible language fast
- It does not know your client's facts
- It may miss recent amendments
### Why
- Drafting is where errors become enforceable
- Style and nuance matter
- The lawyer is the author of record

## What AI Misses

- Client-specific facts and intent
- The latest statutory amendments
- Firm house style and precedent
- Jurisdiction-specific formalities
### The rule
- AI drafts the skeleton
- The lawyer adds the substance
- Verify every clause

## The Drafting Prompt

```text
Draft a first-pass [document type] for [matter] in
[jurisdiction]. Use clear plain language. Mark every place
that needs client-specific facts or a legal check.
```
- Name the document and jurisdiction
- Ask it to flag gaps
- Then finish it yourself

## Key Takeaways

- AI drafting is a first draft, never final
- AI misses client facts, amendments, and house style
- The lawyer is the author of record
- AI drafts the skeleton; the lawyer adds substance
- Verify every clause before it leaves your office

## Practice Challenge

1. Generate a first-pass document with AI
2. Mark every gap that needs facts or a legal check
3. Finish the document in your own words

**Example Quiz**

- A) A first draft, not final

**Answer:** A — AI produces a head start; the lawyer finishes and owns it.

2. What does AI miss?
- A) Client facts, amendments, and house style
- B) Grammar
- C) Formatting
- D) Headings

**Answer:** A — AI lacks client-specific facts and the latest legal changes.

3. Who is the author of record?
- A) The lawyer
- B) The AI
- C) The vendor
- D) The client

**Answer:** A — Every sentence that leaves your office is yours.

4. What should the prompt ask AI to do?
- A) Flag places needing facts or a legal check
- B) Sign the document
- C) File it
- D) Advise the client

**Answer:** A — Ask AI to mark gaps so you can complete and verify them.

### Module 2 · Step 1: Structure Before You Draft

Structure comes before language. A document's sections, order, and defined terms determine whether it works; the prose is secondary.

Plan the structure first, then let AI help fill it. Changing structure after drafting is expensive.

## Learning Objectives

- Plan document structure first
- Order sections logically
- Define terms consistently
- Use AI to scaffold structure

## Structure First

- Sections and their order
- Defined terms and their consistency
- Operative clauses and boilerplate
### Why
- Structure determines enforceability
- It prevents omissions
- It is expensive to change later

## Ordering and Definitions

- Parties, recitals, operative clauses, boilerplate
- Define every key term once
- Use defined terms consistently
### The rule
- One term, one meaning
- Logical order prevents ambiguity
- Boilerplate last, but not forgotten

## The Structure Prompt

```text
Propose the structure for a [document type] in [jurisdiction]:
the sections in order, the defined terms, and the boilerplate
needed. Note any mandatory formalities.
```
- Sections in order
- Defined terms listed
- Mandatory formalities flagged

## Key Takeaways

- Plan structure before drafting language
- Order sections logically: parties, recitals, operative, boilerplate
- Define every key term once and use it consistently
- Flag mandatory jurisdiction-specific formalities
- Changing structure after drafting is expensive

## Practice Challenge

1. Outline the structure of a document you need
2. List and define the key terms
3. Check for mandatory formalities

**Example Quiz**

1. What comes before language?
- A) Structure
- B) Headings
- C) Fonts
- D) Footnotes

**Answer:** A — Sections, order, and defined terms determine whether a document works.

2. What order do sections follow?
- A) Parties, recitals, operative clauses, boilerplate
- B) Boilerplate first
- C) Random order
- D) Alphabetical

**Answer:** A — A logical order prevents ambiguity and omissions.

3. How should defined terms be used?
- A) One term, one meaning, consistently
- B) Loosely
- C) Interchangeably
- D) Rarely

**Answer:** A — Define each key term once and use it consistently.

4. Why plan structure first?
- A) It is expensive to change after drafting
- B) It looks neat
- C) It is required
- D) It saves paper

**Answer:** A — Restructuring a drafted document is costly and error-prone.

### Module 3 · Step 2: Clause and Template Libraries

Most documents reuse clauses. A clause library saves time, improves consistency, and reduces risk - and AI can help you organise and adapt it.

Build your library from clauses you have vetted, and use AI to find and adapt the right one for each matter.

## Learning Objectives

- Build a clause and template library
- Organise clauses by matter type
- Adapt clauses with AI
- Keep the library current

## Why a Library

- Most documents reuse proven clauses
- Consistency reduces risk
- Reuse saves drafting time
### Why
- Vetted clauses are safer
- Consistency is professional
- Time goes to the bespoke parts

## Organising Clauses

- Group by matter type and clause function
- Tag each clause with jurisdiction and date
- Note the risk level of each
### The rule
- Only vetted clauses go in
- Tag for jurisdiction and date
- Review the library regularly

## The Library Prompt

```text
Help me organise a clause library for [practice area]:
suggest categories, tagging fields (jurisdiction, date, risk),
and a template structure I can reuse across matters.
```
- Categories and tags
- A reusable template
- Jurisdiction and risk noted

## Key Takeaways

- Most documents reuse proven clauses
- A library saves time and improves consistency
- Organise by matter type and clause function
- Tag clauses by jurisdiction, date, and risk
- Only vetted clauses enter the library

## Practice Challenge

1. Collect ten vetted clauses you reuse
2. Organise them by type with tags
3. Create one reusable template

**Example Quiz**

**Answer:** A — Reusing vetted clauses reduces risk and drafting time.

2. How should clauses be organised?

**Answer:** A — Group clauses by matter type and clause function.

3. What should each clause be tagged with?

### Module 4 · Step 3: Summarising Long Documents

Long documents - disclosure bundles, judgments, contracts, transcripts - are where hours disappear. AI summarising turns a day of reading into a structured first pass.

Use summaries to navigate and prioritise, then read the critical passages in full.

## Learning Objectives

- Summarise long documents efficiently
- Structure summaries for legal use
- Prioritise what to read in full
- Verify against the source

## Why Summarise

- Long documents consume hours
- Summaries help you navigate
- They surface what matters
### Why
- Triage directs your attention
- It reduces missed issues
- It speeds preparation

## Structuring the Summary

- Ask for sections: parties, dates, key terms, risks
- Ask for page references to the source
- Ask for anything unusual or missing
### The rule
- Summaries need page references
- Unusual terms get flagged
- Critical passages read in full

## The Summary Prompt

```text
Summarise this document [paste] into: parties, dates,
key obligations, unusual or risky terms, and gaps, with
page references. Flag the passages I must read in full.
```
- Structured sections
- Page references
- Flag passages to read fully

## Key Takeaways

- AI summarising turns a day of reading into a first pass
- Summaries help you navigate and prioritise
- Structure summaries: parties, dates, key terms, risks, gaps
- Include page references to the source
- Read critical passages in full

## Practice Challenge

1. Summarise a long document with AI
2. Ask for page references
3. Read the flagged passages in full

**Example Quiz**

1. What do summaries help you do?
- A) Navigate and prioritise
- B) Replace reading
- C) File documents
- D) Sign contracts

**Answer:** A — Summaries direct your attention to what matters most.

2. What should a summary include?
- A) Page references to the source
- B) The AI's opinion
- C) A conclusion
- D) A signature

**Answer:** A — Page references let you verify and read the critical passages.

3. What gets flagged?
- A) Unusual or risky terms and gaps
- B) Only the title
- C) Only dates
- D) Nothing

**Answer:** A — Flag unusual terms, risks, and gaps for closer reading.

4. What do you read in full?
- A) The critical passages
- B) Only the summary
- C) Nothing
- D) Only the first page

**Answer:** A — Critical passages must be read in full, not just summarised.

### Module 5 · Step 4: Tone, Register, and Jurisdiction

Legal writing has a register: formal, precise, and jurisdiction-specific. AI defaults to a generic tone that may not fit your court, client, or jurisdiction.

Adjust tone and register deliberately, and always check jurisdiction-specific terms and formalities.

## Learning Objectives

- Match tone and register to the audience
- Adapt language for the jurisdiction
- Avoid generic AI phrasing
- Keep the client's voice in mind

## Register Matters

- Court filings, client letters, and contracts differ
- Formal does not mean archaic
- Plain language is often clearer and safer
### Why
- The wrong register undermines credibility
- Jurisdiction expects specific forms
- Clients need to understand advice

## Jurisdiction and Formalities

- Terms differ across jurisdictions
- Formal requirements vary
- Check prescribed wording where required
### The rule
- Match the register to the reader
- Check jurisdiction-specific terms
- Avoid generic AI phrasing

## The Tone Prompt

```text
Rewrite this draft [paste] in the register of a [court
filing/client letter/contract] in [jurisdiction]. Keep the
meaning identical and flag any jurisdiction-specific terms.
```
- Register matched to the reader
- Meaning preserved
- Jurisdiction terms flagged

## Key Takeaways

- Legal writing has a register that AI defaults away from
- Match tone to the audience: court, client, or counterparty
- Plain language is often clearer and safer
- Check jurisdiction-specific terms and formalities
- Avoid generic AI phrasing

## Practice Challenge

1. Rewrite a draft for a specific audience and jurisdiction
2. Flag jurisdiction-specific terms
3. Remove generic AI phrasing

**Example Quiz**

1. Why does register matter?
- A) The wrong register undermines credibility
- B) It is fashionable
- C) It saves time
- D) It is required

**Answer:** A — Court filings, client letters, and contracts each need a different register.

2. What does AI default to?
- A) A generic tone
- B) The right register
- C) Plain language
- D) Your house style

**Answer:** A — AI defaults to a generic tone that may not fit the audience.

3. What should you check per jurisdiction?
- A) Terms and formalities
- B) The weather
- C) The fee
- D) The font

**Answer:** A — Jurisdiction-specific terms and formalities must be checked.

4. Is plain language good in legal writing?

**Answer:** A — Plain language is often clearer and safer than archaic formality.

### Module 6 · Step 5: The Review and Redline Pass

The final drafting pass is review and redline: check accuracy, consistency, and completeness, and mark changes clearly for the client or counterparty.

AI can suggest edits, but the redline that goes out is the lawyer's.

## Learning Objectives

- Run a review and redline pass
- Check accuracy, consistency, and completeness
- Mark changes clearly
- Keep the redline authoritative

## What the Review Checks

- Accuracy of facts and law
- Consistency of terms and numbering
- Completeness against the checklist
### Why
- Errors become enforceable
- Consistency prevents ambiguity
- Completeness prevents disputes

## The Redline

- Track every change
- Add comments explaining material edits
- Never send a clean copy when a redline is expected
### The rule
- Changes must be traceable
- Comments explain the why
- The redline is the lawyer's

## The Review Prompt

```text
Review this draft [paste] for accuracy, consistency, and
completeness against this checklist [paste]. Suggest
redline edits with reasons. Do not change the meaning.
```
- Accuracy, consistency, completeness
- Edits with reasons
- Meaning preserved

## Key Takeaways

- The final pass is review and redline
- Check accuracy, consistency, and completeness
- Track every change and comment on material edits
- Never send a clean copy when a redline is expected
- The redline that goes out is the lawyer's

## Practice Challenge

1. Review a draft against a checklist
2. Produce a redline with comments
3. Confirm accuracy, consistency, and completeness

**Example Quiz**

1. What does the review check?
- A) Accuracy, consistency, completeness
- B) Font and margins
- C) The fee
- D) The deadline

**Answer:** A — Review checks facts and law, term consistency, and completeness.

2. Why comment on material edits?
- A) To explain the why of each change
- B) To fill space
- C) To impress
- D) It is required

**Answer:** A — Comments explain material edits to the reader.

3. When do you send a clean copy?
- A) Only when a redline is not expected

**Answer:** A — Send a redline when one is expected; clean copies hide changes.

4. Whose redline goes out?
- A) The lawyer's
- B) The AI's
- C) The vendor's
- D) The client's

**Answer:** A — AI can suggest edits, but the outgoing redline is the lawyer's.

### Module 1 · AI as a Research Assistant, Not Authority

AI can search, summarise, and suggest faster than any junior, but it is not a legal authority. It has no duty to the court, no license, and no accountability. **You do.**

In legal work the cost of an AI error is severe: a fabricated citation can lead to sanctions, a missed issue to malpractice. Treat every AI output as a lead to verify against primary sources.

## Learning Objectives

- Treat AI as a research assistant, not authority
- Understand the risks of unverified AI output
- Use AI to find leads, not conclusions
- Keep professional accountability human

## Assistant, Not Authority

- AI has no license and no duty to the court
- It cannot stand behind a legal conclusion
- Its confidence is not correctness
### Why it matters
- Fabricated citations risk sanctions
- Missed issues risk malpractice
- The lawyer owns the advice

## What AI Is Good For

- Finding leads and search terms
- Summarising documents you provide
- Drafting first-pass language
- Explaining unfamiliar concepts
### The rule
- Use AI to find leads
- Verify against primary sources
- Never rely on AI alone

## The Research Prompt

```text
Act as a legal research assistant. For [issue], list the
relevant questions, search terms, and the primary sources
I should check. Do not state conclusions - mark everything
as a lead to verify.
```
- Ask for leads, not conclusions
- Ask for primary sources
- Mark everything to verify

## Key Takeaways

- AI is a research assistant, never a legal authority
- You carry the license, duty, and accountability
- AI errors can lead to sanctions or malpractice
- Use AI to find leads, not conclusions
- Verify every output against primary sources

## Practice Challenge

1. List three research tasks AI could speed up
2. List three tasks that must stay fully human
3. Write your personal verification rule

**Example Quiz**

### Module 2 · The Legal Prompt Frame (Context–Task–Format)

Legal prompts need more context than most. Jurisdiction, area of law, party position, and the desired output format all change the answer.

The frame is **Context – Task – Format**, with jurisdiction and position made explicit every time.

## Learning Objectives

- Use the Context-Task-Format frame in legal work
- Specify jurisdiction and party position
- Define the output format precisely
- Iterate on first drafts

## The Three Parts

- **Context:** jurisdiction, area of law, party position, facts
- **Task:** the exact research or drafting job
- **Format:** memo, bullet list, table, clause, word limit
### Why
- Jurisdiction changes the law
- Position changes the argument
- Format makes output usable

## A Worked Example

```text
Context: I act for the defendant in a contract dispute
governed by [jurisdiction] law. Task: identify the likely
defences to a breach-of-payment claim. Format: bullet list
of defences, each with the elements and a leading authority.
```
- State the position explicitly
- Name the jurisdiction
- Define the output shape

## Iterating and Saving

- Treat the first answer as a draft
- Ask for authority and elements
- Save the prompts that work
### The payoff
- Faster, more usable output
- Consistency across matters
- A reusable precedent library

## Key Takeaways

- Use Context - Task - Format in legal prompts
- Always specify jurisdiction and party position
- Define the output format precisely
- Treat the first answer as a draft and iterate
- Save prompts that work as a precedent library

## Practice Challenge

1. Write a legal Context-Task-Format prompt for a real matter
2. Include jurisdiction and position
3. Save it for reuse

**Example Quiz**

1. What must legal Context always include?
- A) Jurisdiction and party position
- B) The word count
- C) The fee
- D) The client's name

**Answer:** A — Jurisdiction and position change the legal answer.

2. What does Format define?
- A) Memo, list, table, clause, or word limit
- B) The facts
- C) The authorities
- D) The jurisdiction

**Answer:** A — Format makes the output usable in your workflow.

3. Why specify position?
- A) It changes the argument and analysis
- B) It is polite
- C) It is required
- D) It fills space

**Answer:** A — Acting for claimant or defendant changes the analysis.

4. How should you treat the first answer?
- A) As a draft to iterate on
- B) As final advice
- C) As a source
- D) As a judgment

**Answer:** A — Treat the first response as a draft and refine it.

### Module 3 · Finding Statutes and Precedents with AI

AI is useful for orienting you in unfamiliar areas: naming the relevant statutes, suggesting the leading cases, and outlining the elements of a claim.

But every statute and case must be opened and read. AI can name a case that does not exist or misstate what it holds.

## Learning Objectives

- Use AI to orient in unfamiliar areas
- Find statute and case leads
- Outline the elements of a claim
- Verify every authority

## Orienting Fast

- Name the relevant statutes and regulations
- Suggest leading cases by area
- Outline the elements of a claim or defence
### Why
- Unfamiliar areas take hours to map
- AI provides a starting map
- You verify the map

## Statutes and Precedents

- Ask for the statutory provisions by name
- Ask for the leading cases and what they hold
- Ask for the elements and their authorities
### The rule
- Open every statute
- Read every case
- Confirm the holding matches

## The Authority Prompt

```text
For [issue] in [jurisdiction], list the relevant statutory
provisions and leading cases, with the elements of the claim
and the authority for each. Mark all as leads to verify.
```
- Provisions by name
- Cases with holdings
- Elements with authority

## Key Takeaways

- Use AI to orient in unfamiliar areas of law
- Ask for statutes, leading cases, and elements
- Open every statute and read every case
- Confirm each holding matches the AI's description
- AI can name cases that do not exist

## Practice Challenge

1. Ask AI to map an unfamiliar area
2. Open each statute and case it names
3. Correct or discard anything fabricated

**Example Quiz**

**Answer:** A — AI gives a starting map that you verify.

2. What must you do with every case?

**Answer:** A — Open every statute and read every case to confirm the holding.

3. What can AI do with cases?

4. What should the prompt ask for?

### Module 4 · Verifying Citations and Spotting Hallucinations

Fabricated citations are the signature failure of AI in legal work. Courts have sanctioned lawyers who filed briefs citing cases that did not exist.

Every citation must be verified in a reliable database before it appears in any filed or advised document.

## Learning Objectives

- Recognise AI citation hallucinations
- Verify every citation in a database
- Spot misattributed or misstated cases
- Build citation checking into your workflow

## The Failure Mode

- AI invents case names, reporters, and pin cites
- It misattributes holdings to real cases
- It cites overruled or superseded law
### Why it happens
- AI predicts plausible citations
- It has no live legal database
- Confidence is not accuracy

## Verifying Every Citation

- Search each citation in a reliable database
- Confirm the case exists and the holding matches
- Check it has not been overruled or distinguished
### The rule
- No citation without a database check
- If it cannot be found, it does not exist
- Cite only what you have read

## The Citation-Check Prompt

```text
For each citation in this draft [paste], tell me the
specific verification steps: the database to search, what
to confirm about the holding, and how to check it is still
good law. Do not assert the citations are valid.
```
- A verification step per citation
- Check the holding
- Check it is still good law

## Key Takeaways

- Fabricated citations are the signature AI failure in law
- Courts have sanctioned lawyers for citing nonexistent cases
- Search every citation in a reliable database
- Confirm the holding and that it is still good law
- Cite only what you have personally read

## Practice Challenge

1. Ask AI for citations on a topic
2. Try to find each in a legal database
3. Note which were fabricated or misstated

**Example Quiz**

1. What is the signature AI failure in law?
- A) Fabricated citations
- B) Slow drafting
- C) Bad grammar
- D) Poor formatting

**Answer:** A — AI invents case names and pin cites that do not exist.

2. What must you do with every citation?
- A) Verify it in a reliable database
- B) Trust it
- C) Cite it directly
- D) Count it

**Answer:** A — No citation goes into a document without a database check.

3. What if a citation cannot be found?
- A) It does not exist - do not use it
- B) Use it anyway
- C) Ask the AI again
- D) Assume a typo

**Answer:** A — If it cannot be found, treat it as nonexistent.

4. What do you cite?
- A) Only what you have personally read
- B) AI summaries
- C) Anything plausible
- D) Unread cases

**Answer:** A — Cite only authorities you have read and confirmed.

### Module 5 · Case Law Summaries You Can Trust

AI summaries are useful for triage, not for advice. A summary you can trust is one you built from the actual judgment, with the holding and reasoning verified.

Use AI to summarise cases you provide, then check the summary against the text before relying on it.

## Learning Objectives

- Use AI summaries for triage only
- Build summaries from the actual judgment
- Verify holdings and reasoning
- Produce reliable case notes

## Summaries for Triage

- AI summaries help you decide what to read
- They are not a substitute for the judgment
- They can miss or distort the ratio
### Why
- The ratio decides the case
- Obiter can mislead
- Reading is the standard

## Building Trustworthy Notes

- Paste the judgment and ask for a structured summary
- Check the holding, reasoning, and outcome
- Note the jurisdiction and date
### The rule
- Summarise only what you provide
- Verify against the text
- Record the citation and date

## The Summary Prompt

```text
Summarise this judgment [paste] into: facts, issue, holding,
reasoning, and outcome. Quote the key passages. Flag anything
that is obiter or uncertain.
```
- Structured sections
- Key passages quoted
- Flag obiter and uncertainty

## Key Takeaways

- AI summaries are for triage, not for advice
- Build summaries from the actual judgment text
- Verify the holding, reasoning, and outcome
- Note jurisdiction and date
- A summary you can trust is one you have checked

## Practice Challenge

1. Paste a judgment and generate a structured summary
2. Verify the holding against the text
3. Write your own reliable case note

**Example Quiz**

1. What are AI summaries good for?
- A) Triage - deciding what to read
- B) Final advice
- C) Filing
- D) Signing

**Answer:** A — Summaries help you prioritise, not replace reading the judgment.

2. What can summaries miss?
- A) The ratio and key reasoning
- B) The date
- C) The parties
- D) The court

**Answer:** A — AI summaries can miss or distort the ratio decidendi.

3. What should you paste into the tool?
- A) The actual judgment text

- A) The citation and date

- D) The weather

**Answer:** A — Record the citation and date for every case note.

### Module 6 · The Research Memo Workflow

A research memo has a standard structure: question, brief answer, facts, analysis, and conclusion. AI can speed each part, but you assemble and verify the whole.

The workflow keeps AI in its place: it drafts and organises; you verify authorities and own the conclusion.

## Learning Objectives

- Structure a research memo
- Use AI to draft sections
- Verify every authority in the memo
- Own the conclusion

## The Memo Structure

- **Question presented:** the precise legal issue
- **Brief answer:** the short conclusion
- **Facts:** the relevant facts
- **Analysis:** the law applied to the facts
- **Conclusion:** the advice
### Why
- Structure makes advice usable
- It shows the reasoning
- It is the professional standard

## Using AI for Sections

- Ask for a first-pass analysis outline
- Ask for the counterarguments
- Never let AI state the final conclusion
### The rule
- AI drafts, you verify
- Every authority checked
- The conclusion is yours

## The Memo Prompt

```text
Draft a research memo outline for [question] in [jurisdiction]:
question, brief answer, facts, analysis with authorities,
counterarguments, and conclusion. Mark all authorities as
unverified leads.
```
- Full memo structure
- Authorities as unverified leads
- Counterarguments included

## Key Takeaways

- A research memo has question, answer, facts, analysis, and conclusion
- Use AI to draft sections and find counterarguments
- Verify every authority in the memo
- Never let AI state the final conclusion
- The advice is the lawyer's, not the tool's

## Practice Challenge

3. Write the conclusion yourself

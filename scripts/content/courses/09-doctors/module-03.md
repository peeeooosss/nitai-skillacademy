### Module 1 · Evidence First, AI Second

AI can help you find and digest literature, but it is not a source of evidence. The evidence is in the studies, not in the model.

Use AI for speed and structure; verify every claim against the actual paper.

## Learning Objectives

- Understand AI is not evidence
- Use AI for search and structure
- Verify claims against sources
- Avoid fabricated citations

## AI Is Not Evidence

- The evidence lives in studies, not models
- AI can misstate or invent findings
- Citations must be verified
### Why
- Clinical decisions need real evidence
- Fabricated citations are a known risk
- Verification is non-negotiable

## What AI Is Good For

- Building search strategies
- Screening and summarising abstracts
- Structuring a review
### The rule
- AI assists, sources decide
- Verify every citation
- Read the paper before you cite it

## The Evidence Prompt

```text
Help me build a search strategy for the clinical question
[question]: databases, keywords, and inclusion criteria.
Do not provide citations; I will verify sources myself.
```
- A search strategy
- Keywords and criteria
- No unverified citations

## Key Takeaways

- AI is not a source of evidence; studies are
- AI can misstate or fabricate findings and citations
- Use AI for search strategy, screening, and structure
- Verify every citation against the real paper
- Read the source before you rely on it

## Practice Challenge

1. Write one clinical question
2. Build a search strategy with AI
3. Verify one citation against the source

**Example Quiz**

**Answer:** A — Evidence is in the studies, not in the AI model.

**Answer:** A — AI can invent findings and citations that do not exist.

3. What is AI good for?
- A) Search strategy and structure

### Module 2 · Step 1: Turn a Clinical Question into a Search

A good literature search starts with a well-framed question. The PICO structure - population, intervention, comparison, outcome - turns a vague topic into a searchable question.

AI helps convert the question into keywords, synonyms, and a search strategy.

## Learning Objectives

- Frame a clinical question with PICO
- Build keywords and synonyms
- Choose databases
- Create a reproducible search

## Framing with PICO

- **P**opulation: who is studied
- **I**ntervention: what is tested
- **C**omparison: against what
- **O**utcome: what is measured
### Why
- A clear question focuses the search
- PICO makes it reproducible
- Good questions find good evidence

## Building the Search

- List keywords and synonyms per PICO element
- Combine with database operators
- Record the strategy so it can be repeated
### The rule
- Frame before you search
- Include synonyms
- Save the strategy

## The PICO Prompt

```text
Turn this clinical question into a PICO framework and a
search strategy for [database]: keywords, synonyms, and
Boolean combinations. Do not invent citations.
```
- A PICO frame
- Keywords and synonyms
- Boolean combinations

## Key Takeaways

- Frame the question with PICO before searching
- PICO means population, intervention, comparison, outcome
- List keywords and synonyms for each element
- Combine terms with database operators
- Save the strategy so the search is reproducible

## Practice Challenge

1. Frame one question with PICO
2. List synonyms for each element
3. Build and save the search strategy

**Example Quiz**

1. What does PICO stand for?
- A) Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome
- B) Patient, Illness, Care, Outcome
- C) Plan, Investigate, Compare, Observe
- D) Primary, Internal, Clinical, Overall

**Answer:** A — PICO frames a searchable clinical question.

2. Why use PICO?
- A) It focuses and makes the search reproducible
- B) It looks official
- C) It is required
- D) It is fast

**Answer:** A — A PICO frame keeps the search focused and repeatable.

3. What should you include per element?
- A) Keywords and synonyms
- B) Only one term
- C) Citations
- D) Nothing

**Answer:** A — List keywords and synonyms for each PICO element.

4. What should you save?
- A) The search strategy
- B) The font
- C) The logo
- D) The screenshot

**Answer:** A — Saving the strategy lets you repeat and update the search.

### Module 3 · Step 2: Screening Abstracts with AI

Screening is the slow part of a review: deciding which papers meet your criteria. AI can speed the first pass by sorting abstracts.

But screening decisions must be checked - AI can misclassify, and inclusion criteria are clinical judgments.

## Learning Objectives

- Screen abstracts faster with AI
- Apply inclusion criteria consistently
- Check AI screening decisions
- Keep a record of decisions

## The Screening Task

- Apply inclusion and exclusion criteria
- Decide include, exclude, or maybe
- Record the reason for each decision
### Why
- Criteria keep the review honest
- A record makes it reproducible
- AI can misclassify abstracts

## Using AI Safely

- Give AI the exact criteria
- Ask it to justify each decision
- Spot-check its classifications yourself
### The rule
- Criteria first, then screen
- Justify every decision
- Spot-check AI's work

## The Screening Prompt

```text
Screen these abstracts against these inclusion criteria
[criteria]: label each include, exclude, or maybe, and give
the reason. I will verify your decisions.
```
- Include, exclude, or maybe
- A reason per decision
- Verifiable output

## Key Takeaways

- Screening applies inclusion and exclusion criteria
- AI can speed the first pass through abstracts
- Give AI the exact criteria and ask for reasons
- Spot-check AI classifications yourself
- Record every decision so the review is reproducible

## Practice Challenge

1. Write your inclusion and exclusion criteria
2. Screen ten abstracts with AI
3. Spot-check and correct the decisions

**Example Quiz**

1. What does screening apply?
- A) Inclusion and exclusion criteria
- B) Random choice
- C) Journal rank
- D) Author fame

**Answer:** A — Screening decides which papers meet your stated criteria.

2. What can AI do?
- A) Speed the first pass
- B) Make final decisions
- C) Guarantee accuracy
- D) Find all papers

**Answer:** A — AI speeds screening but its decisions must be checked.

3. What should AI give per decision?
- A) A reason
- B) A score
- C) A citation
- D) Nothing

**Answer:** A — Ask AI to justify each include, exclude, or maybe decision.

4. What should you do with AI screening?
- A) Spot-check it
- B) Trust it fully
- C) Ignore it
- D) Publish it

**Answer:** A — Spot-check AI classifications because it can misclassify.

### Module 4 · Step 3: Appraising the Evidence

Appraisal is judging how much you can trust a study: its design, risk of bias, and applicability to your patients.

AI can explain appraisal concepts and prompt your thinking, but the appraisal judgment is yours.

## Learning Objectives

- Appraise study quality
- Check design and risk of bias
- Judge applicability to patients
- Keep appraisal judgment yours

## What to Appraise

- Study design and level of evidence
- Risk of bias and confounding
- Precision and effect size
- Applicability to your population
### Why
- Not all evidence is equal
- Bias can mislead decisions
- Applicability decides usefulness

## Using AI for Appraisal

- Ask AI to explain appraisal criteria
- Use it to structure your assessment
- Keep the judgment and final call yourself
### The rule
- Design and bias first
- Check applicability
- The appraisal is yours

## The Appraisal Prompt

```text
Help me appraise this study [paste details]: explain the
design, likely sources of bias, precision, and applicability
to [population]. Do not give a final quality verdict.
```
- Design explained
- Bias and precision noted
- Applicability to your population

## Key Takeaways

- Appraisal judges how much a study can be trusted
- Check design, risk of bias, precision, and applicability
- Not all evidence is equal
- AI can explain criteria and structure your assessment
- The appraisal judgment is always yours

## Practice Challenge

1. Appraise one study with AI's help
2. Note its design and main bias risk
3. Judge its applicability to your patients

**Example Quiz**

- A) Judging how much a study can be trusted

- C) Finding a study
- D) Citing a study

**Answer:** A — Appraisal assesses study quality and trustworthiness.

2. What do you check?
- A) Design, bias, precision, applicability
- B) Only the title
- C) Only the year
- D) Only the journal

**Answer:** A — Check design, risk of bias, precision, and applicability.

3. What should AI not give?
- A) A final quality verdict
- B) An explanation
- C) A structure
- D) A definition

**Answer:** A — AI can explain criteria, but the final appraisal is yours.

4. What decides usefulness?
- A) Applicability to your patients
- B) Journal fame
- C) Author fame
- D) Length

**Answer:** A — Applicability to your population decides how useful a study is.

### Module 5 · Step 4: Synthesising Findings for Your Team

A synthesis brings many studies into a clear answer: what is consistent, what conflicts, and what it means for practice.

AI can organise the findings, but you must ensure the synthesis reflects the actual evidence.

## Learning Objectives

- Synthesise findings across studies
- Show consistency and conflict
- Translate evidence into practice
- Keep synthesis faithful to sources

## Building a Synthesis

- Group studies by outcome and quality
- Note where findings agree and conflict
- State what it means for practice
### Why
- Teams need a clear answer
- Conflict must be shown, not hidden
- Practice implications drive decisions

## Keeping It Faithful

- Base every claim on a verified source
- Distinguish strong from weak evidence
- Flag uncertainty honestly
### The rule
- Cite only verified sources
- Show conflict, do not hide it
- Be honest about uncertainty

## The Synthesis Prompt

```text
Organise these verified findings [paste] into a synthesis:
where studies agree, where they conflict, strength of
evidence, and implications for [practice]. No new citations.
```
- Agreements and conflicts
- Strength of evidence
- Practice implications

## Key Takeaways

- A synthesis answers what the evidence overall shows
- Show where studies agree and where they conflict
- Distinguish strong from weak evidence
- State the implications for practice
- Base every claim on a verified source

## Practice Challenge

1. Synthesise three verified studies
2. Note one agreement and one conflict
3. Write the practice implication

**Example Quiz**

1. What does a synthesis answer?
- A) What the evidence overall shows
- B) One study's result
- C) A single opinion
- D) A guess

**Answer:** A — A synthesis brings many studies into a clear overall answer.

2. What must be shown?
- A) Conflict between studies
- B) Only agreement
- C) Nothing
- D) Only the conclusion

**Answer:** A — Show where studies conflict rather than hiding it.

3. What should you distinguish?
- A) Strong from weak evidence
- B) Fonts
- C) Journals only
- D) Authors only

**Answer:** A — Distinguish strong evidence from weak when synthesising.

4. What must every claim be based on?
- A) A verified source
- B) The AI's memory
- C) A guess
- D) A headline

**Answer:** A — Base every claim on a source you have verified.

### Module 6 · Step 5: Keeping a Reference and Notes System

A reference and notes system keeps your evidence organised and reproducible: every paper, decision, and note in one place.

AI can help you summarise and tag, but the system keeps the sources verifiable.

## Learning Objectives

- Keep a reference system
- Organise notes by topic
- Make the review reproducible
- Keep sources verifiable

## The System

- A reference manager for citations
- Notes linked to each source
- Tags for topic and quality
### Why
- Reproducible reviews need records
- Notes prevent re-reading
- Verifiability builds trust

## Organising Notes

- One note per paper with key findings
- Tag by PICO element and quality
- Link notes to citations
### The rule
- Every claim links to a source
- Notes are searchable
- Keep it current

## The System Prompt

```text
Design a reference and notes system for a clinical literature
review: fields per paper, tagging scheme, and how to link
notes to citations so the review is reproducible.
```
- Fields per paper
- A tagging scheme
- Linking notes to citations

## Key Takeaways

- A reference and notes system keeps evidence organised
- Use a reference manager for citations
- Keep one note per paper with key findings
- Tag notes by topic and quality
- Link every claim to a verifiable source

## Practice Challenge

1. Set up a reference manager
2. Write notes for three papers
3. Tag and link them to citations

**Example Quiz**

1. What keeps evidence organised?

2. What should you keep per paper?
- A) A note with key findings

**Answer:** A — One note per paper captures key findings for reuse.

3. How should notes be tagged?

4. What must every claim link to?

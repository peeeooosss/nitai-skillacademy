# Mission 4: Patient Communication & Education with AI

---
### Module 1 · Plain Language Is a Clinical Skill

Patients who understand their condition and plan are safer and more engaged. Plain language is a clinical skill, not a simplification.

AI is good at rewriting clinical text into plain language, but you must check accuracy and tone.

## Learning Objectives

- Understand plain language as clinical
- Communicate clearly with patients
- Use AI to rewrite clinical text
- Check accuracy and tone

## Why Plain Language

- Understanding improves adherence
- Clear information reduces anxiety
- It supports informed consent
### Why
- Patients act on what they understand
- Jargon excludes and confuses
- Clarity is part of good care

## Using AI to Rewrite

- Give AI the clinical text and the reading level
- Ask it to keep the meaning exact
- Review for accuracy and warmth
### The rule
- Meaning stays exact
- Aim for a low reading level
- Keep a warm, respectful tone

## The Plain-Language Prompt

```text
Rewrite this clinical text [paste] into plain language at a
[grade] reading level for a patient. Keep the meaning exact
and use a warm, respectful tone.
```
- Plain-language text
- Meaning preserved
- Warm tone

## Key Takeaways

- Plain language is a clinical skill, not a simplification
- Patients act on what they understand
- AI can rewrite clinical text to a target reading level
- Keep the meaning exact and the tone warm
- Review every rewrite for accuracy

## Practice Challenge

1. Take one clinical sentence and rewrite it plainly
2. Use AI at a grade level you choose
3. Check the meaning is unchanged

**Example Quiz**

1. What is plain language?
- A) A clinical skill
- B) A simplification
- C) Optional
- D) Unprofessional

**Answer:** A — Plain language is a clinical skill that improves understanding.

2. Why does it matter?
- A) Patients act on what they understand
- B) It sounds nice
- C) It is required
- D) It saves paper

**Answer:** A — Understanding improves adherence and informed consent.

3. What should stay exact?
- A) The meaning
- B) The jargon
- C) The length
- D) The font

**Answer:** A — Rewriting to plain language must preserve the clinical meaning.

4. What tone should you keep?
- A) Warm and respectful
- B) Cold and formal
- C) Technical
- D) Vague

**Answer:** A — Keep a warm, respectful tone in patient communication.

### Module 2 · Step 1: Translate Clinical Jargon into Plain Language

Clinical terms like myocardial infarction or idiopathic are barriers. Translating them into everyday words helps patients understand and act.

AI can translate jargon quickly, but some terms have no simple equivalent and need careful explanation.

## Learning Objectives

- Translate clinical jargon
- Find plain equivalents
- Explain terms with no simple equivalent
- Check the translation is accurate

## Translating Terms

- Replace jargon with everyday words
- Explain rather than just shorten
- Keep the clinical meaning intact
### Why
- Jargon blocks understanding
- Explanation builds comprehension
- Accuracy keeps it safe

## When There Is No Simple Word

- Use a short explanation
- Give an example if helpful
- Check the patient understands
### The rule
- Explain, do not just replace
- Keep meaning exact
- Confirm understanding

## The Jargon Prompt

```text
Translate these clinical terms [paste] into plain language
for a patient. Where there is no simple equivalent, give a
short explanation and an example.
```
- Plain translations
- Explanations where needed
- Examples

## Key Takeaways

- Clinical jargon is a barrier to understanding
- Replace jargon with everyday words and explanations
- Some terms need a short explanation rather than a replacement
- Keep the clinical meaning exact
- Confirm the patient understands

## Practice Challenge

1. List ten terms from your specialty
2. Translate them with AI
3. Check which need explanations

**Example Quiz**

1. What does jargon do?
- A) Blocks understanding
- B) Builds trust
- C) Saves time
- D) Improves consent

**Answer:** A — Jargon confuses patients and blocks understanding.

2. What should you do with hard terms?
- A) Explain them
- B) Repeat them
- C) Ignore them
- D) Shorten them only

**Answer:** A — Explain terms that have no simple everyday equivalent.

3. What must stay intact?
- A) The clinical meaning
- B) The jargon
- C) The length
- D) The format

**Answer:** A — Keep the clinical meaning exact when translating jargon.

4. What should you confirm?
- A) That the patient understands
- B) The font
- C) The date
- D) The price

**Answer:** A — Confirm understanding rather than assuming it.

### Module 3 · Step 2: Patient Education Handouts

Handouts let patients take information home. A good handout is short, clear, and actionable, with the key points first.

AI can draft handouts from your clinical content, but every claim must be verified and tailored to the patient.

## Learning Objectives

- Create patient education handouts
- Keep them short and actionable
- Verify every clinical claim
- Tailor to the patient

## What a Handout Needs

- The key points first
- Simple, actionable steps
- What to watch for and when to seek help
### Why
- Patients forget verbal advice
- Actionable steps improve adherence
- Safety-netting reduces risk

## Drafting with AI

- Give AI the clinical content and reading level
- Ask for key points, steps, and red flags
- Verify every clinical claim
### The rule
- Short and clear
- Always include red flags
- Verify every claim

## The Handout Prompt

```text
Draft a patient education handout on [condition] from this
clinical content [paste]: key points, action steps, and red
flags for when to seek help. Plain language, [grade] level.
```
- Key points
- Action steps
- Red flags

## Key Takeaways

- Handouts help patients take information home
- Keep them short, clear, and actionable
- Put the key points first
- Always include red flags and when to seek help
- Verify every clinical claim before sharing

## Practice Challenge

1. Draft a handout for one condition
2. Include action steps and red flags
3. Verify every claim with a clinical source

**Example Quiz**

1. What does a handout help with?
- A) Taking information home
- B) Billing
- C) Scheduling
- D) Referrals

**Answer:** A — Handouts let patients take clear information home.

2. What should come first?
- A) The key points
- B) The references
- C) The disclaimer
- D) The author

**Answer:** A — Put the key points first so patients get the essentials.

3. What must a handout always include?
- A) Red flags and when to seek help
- B) Statistics
- C) Latin terms
- D) Citations

**Answer:** A — Safety-netting with red flags reduces patient risk.

4. What must you verify?
- A) Every clinical claim
- B) Only the title
- C) Only the date
- D) Nothing

**Answer:** A — Verify every clinical claim before sharing a handout.

### Module 4 · Step 3: Preparing for Difficult Conversations

Difficult conversations - bad news, poor prognosis, errors - need preparation. AI can help you plan what to say, but the conversation itself is human.

Preparation means knowing the key messages, likely questions, and the patient's values before you sit down.

## Learning Objectives

- Prepare for difficult conversations
- Plan key messages
- Anticipate patient questions
- Keep the conversation human

## What to Prepare

- The key messages in plain language
- Likely questions and answers
- The patient's values and goals
### Why
- Preparation reduces harm
- Anticipating questions keeps calm
- Values guide the conversation

## Keeping It Human

- AI helps you prepare, not deliver
- Keep empathy at the centre
- Follow the patient's pace
### The rule
- Prepare, then be present
- Lead with empathy
- The conversation is yours

## The Preparation Prompt

```text
Help me prepare to discuss [situation] with a patient: key
messages in plain language, likely questions, and how to
lead with empathy. I will deliver it myself.
```
- Key messages
- Likely questions
- An empathetic approach

## Key Takeaways

- Difficult conversations need preparation
- Plan key messages in plain language
- Anticipate likely questions
- Know the patient's values and goals
- AI helps you prepare; the conversation stays human

## Practice Challenge

1. Prepare key messages for one difficult conversation
2. List likely patient questions
3. Plan how to lead with empathy

**Example Quiz**

1. What do difficult conversations need?
- A) Preparation
- B) Speed
- C) Jargon
- D) Paperwork

**Answer:** A — Preparation reduces harm and helps the conversation go well.

2. What should you plan?
- A) Key messages and likely questions
- B) Only the diagnosis
- C) Only the treatment
- D) Nothing

**Answer:** A — Plan key messages and anticipate the patient's questions.

3. What guides the conversation?
- A) The patient's values and goals
- B) The doctor's schedule
- C) The protocol
- D) The AI

**Answer:** A — The patient's values and goals should guide the conversation.

4. What does AI do here?
- A) Helps you prepare
- B) Delivers the news
- C) Signs off
- D) Examines the patient

**Answer:** A — AI helps you prepare; you deliver the conversation yourself.

### Module 5 · Step 4: Multilingual and Low-Literacy Communication

Patients who speak another language or have low literacy face extra barriers. Clear, translated, and visual communication helps close the gap.

AI can draft translations and simple versions, but clinical accuracy and cultural appropriateness must be checked by a human.

## Learning Objectives

- Communicate across language barriers
- Support low-literacy patients
- Use AI for translations and simple text
- Check accuracy and culture

## Closing the Gap

- Translate key information accurately
- Simplify text and add visuals
- Use interpreters for conversations
### Why
- Language barriers cause harm
- Low literacy hides misunderstanding
- Equity depends on comprehension

## Using AI Safely

- AI drafts translations and simple versions
- A human checks accuracy and culture
- Use professional interpreters for consent
### The rule
- Check every translation
- Respect culture
- Interpreters for key conversations

## The Access Prompt

```text
Rewrite this patient information [paste] in simple language
and draft a [language] translation. Flag anything that needs
a professional interpreter or cultural check.
```
- Simple-language version
- A draft translation
- Flags for interpreter or culture

## Key Takeaways

- Language and literacy barriers cause harm
- Translate accurately and simplify text with visuals
- AI drafts translations and simple versions
- A human checks accuracy and cultural appropriateness
- Use professional interpreters for key conversations

## Practice Challenge

1. Simplify one patient information sheet
2. Draft a translation and check it
3. Identify when an interpreter is needed

**Example Quiz**

1. What do language barriers cause?
- A) Harm
- B) Trust
- C) Speed
- D) Savings

**Answer:** A — Language barriers cause misunderstanding and harm.

2. What can AI draft?
- A) Translations and simple versions
- B) Final consent
- C) Interpretation
- D) Diagnosis

**Answer:** A — AI can draft translations and simplified text for review.

3. Who checks accuracy and culture?
- A) A human
- B) The AI
- C) The vendor
- D) The patient only

**Answer:** A — A human must check translation accuracy and cultural fit.

4. What should be used for key conversations?
- A) Professional interpreters
- B) AI only
- C) Family members only
- D) Written notes only

**Answer:** A — Use professional interpreters for consent and key conversations.

### Module 6 · Step 5: The Shared Decision-Making Conversation

Shared decision-making means deciding with the patient, not for them. It combines the best evidence with the patient's values and preferences.

AI can prepare the options and trade-offs, but the decision is made together, in conversation.

## Learning Objectives

- Practise shared decision-making
- Present options and trade-offs
- Elicit patient preferences
- Decide together

## Shared Decision-Making

- Present the options clearly
- Explain benefits, harms, and uncertainty
- Ask about the patient's values and preferences
### Why
- Patients differ in what matters to them
- Evidence alone does not decide
- Shared decisions improve adherence

## Preparing with AI

- Ask AI to lay out options and trade-offs
- Prepare a plain-language comparison
- Bring the patient's preferences into the choice
### The rule
- Options, not orders
- Elicit preferences
- Decide together

## The Shared-Decision Prompt

```text
Help me prepare a shared decision-making conversation about
[decision]: the options, benefits, harms, uncertainty, and
questions to elicit the patient's values and preferences.
```
- Options and trade-offs
- Uncertainty stated
- Questions to elicit values

## Key Takeaways

- Shared decision-making decides with the patient, not for them
- Present options, benefits, harms, and uncertainty clearly
- Ask about the patient's values and preferences
- Evidence alone does not decide
- AI prepares the options; the decision is made together

## Practice Challenge

1. Prepare options for one clinical decision
2. List questions to elicit patient values
3. Practise a shared decision conversation

**Example Quiz**

1. What is shared decision-making?
- A) Deciding with the patient
- B) Deciding for the patient
- C) Following protocol only
- D) Letting AI decide

**Answer:** A — Shared decision-making decides with the patient, not for them.

2. What do you present?
- A) Options, benefits, harms, uncertainty
- B) Only the best option
- C) Only the risks
- D) Only the cost

**Answer:** A — Present options and trade-offs clearly, including uncertainty.

3. What do you elicit?
- A) The patient's values and preferences
- B) Their insurance
- C) Their address
- D) Their schedule

**Answer:** A — Ask about the patient's values and preferences before deciding.

4. Who makes the decision?
- A) Clinician and patient together
- B) The AI
- C) The protocol
- D) The family alone

**Answer:** A — The decision is made together by clinician and patient.

# Mission 5: Diagnostics Support & Safety with AI

---
### Module 1 · AI as a Second Opinion, Never the First

AI can act like a well-read colleague who suggests possibilities you might not have considered. But it is never the first opinion and never the decision-maker.

Use AI after you have formed your own clinical assessment, to challenge and widen your thinking.

## Learning Objectives

- Use AI as a second opinion
- Form your own assessment first
- Widen your differential safely
- Keep the decision yours

## Second, Not First

- Form your own assessment first
- Then ask AI to challenge it
- Compare, do not defer
### Why
- Anchoring on AI is dangerous
- Your assessment is the anchor
- AI widens, it does not decide

## How to Use It

- Ask what else could explain the findings
- Ask what would change the diagnosis
- Weigh suggestions against the patient
### The rule
- You assess first
- AI challenges second
- You decide finally

## The Second-Opinion Prompt

```text
My clinical assessment is [assessment] based on these
findings [paste]. As a second opinion, what else could
explain this, and what would change the diagnosis?
```
- Alternative explanations
- Discriminating findings
- For clinician consideration

## Key Takeaways

- AI is a second opinion, never the first
- Form your own clinical assessment before asking AI
- Use AI to challenge and widen your thinking
- Compare suggestions against the patient, do not defer
- The diagnostic decision is always yours

## Practice Challenge

1. Write your assessment before consulting AI
2. Ask AI for alternatives
3. Decide which, if any, change your view

**Example Quiz**

1. What is AI in diagnosis?
- A) A second opinion
- B) The first opinion
- C) The decision-maker
- D) A replacement

**Answer:** A — AI acts as a second opinion after your own assessment.

2. What should you do first?
- A) Form your own assessment
- B) Ask the AI
- C) Order tests
- D) Refer

**Answer:** A — Form your own clinical assessment before consulting AI.

3. What does AI do?
- A) Challenges and widens your thinking
- B) Decides
- C) Signs off
- D) Examines

**Answer:** A — AI helps challenge and widen your differential, not decide it.

4. Who makes the final call?
- A) The clinician
- B) The AI
- C) The vendor
- D) The patient alone

**Answer:** A — The diagnostic decision is always the clinician's.

### Module 2 · Step 1: Framing the Diagnostic Question

AI is only as useful as the question you ask. A vague presentation gets vague suggestions; a well-framed diagnostic question gets useful ones.

Frame the question with the key findings, the patient context, and what you are trying to decide.

## Learning Objectives

- Frame a diagnostic question
- Include key findings and context
- State what you are deciding
- Get focused suggestions

## What to Include

- The presenting problem and key findings
- Relevant history and risk factors
- The decision you are facing
### Why
- Context sharpens suggestions
- A clear decision focuses the output
- Missing context misleads

## Framing Well

- De-identify before you paste
- Give the salient positives and negatives
- State your working hypothesis
### The rule
- De-identify first
- Include key negatives
- State the decision

## The Framing Prompt

```text
Help me think through this presentation [de-identified
findings]: key positives, relevant negatives, history, and
the diagnostic decision I am facing. Suggest considerations.
```
- Key positives and negatives
- Relevant history
- The decision stated

## Key Takeaways

- AI is only as useful as the question you ask
- Include the presenting problem and key findings
- Give relevant history and risk factors
- State the decision you are facing
- De-identify the data before you paste it

## Practice Challenge

1. Frame one diagnostic question
2. Include key positives and negatives
3. De-identify before pasting

**Example Quiz**

1. What determines AI's usefulness?
- A) The question you ask
- B) The tool
- C) The price
- D) The speed

**Answer:** A — A well-framed diagnostic question produces useful suggestions.

2. What should you include?
- A) Key findings, history, the decision
- B) Only the diagnosis
- C) Only the age
- D) Nothing

**Answer:** A — Include findings, relevant history, and the decision you face.

3. What must you do before pasting?
- A) De-identify the data
- B) Add the name
- C) Add the address
- D) Nothing

**Answer:** A — De-identify patient data before pasting it into any tool.

4. Why include key negatives?
- A) They sharpen the differential
- B) They look thorough
- C) They are required
- D) They save space

**Answer:** A — Relevant negatives help focus the differential reasoning.

### Module 3 · Step 2: Using AI for Differential Diagnosis

AI is useful for generating a broad differential: possibilities you may have under-weighted, and findings that would distinguish them.

The value is in breadth and the discriminating features, not in a single answer.

## Learning Objectives

- Generate a differential with AI
- Ask for discriminating features
- Weigh likelihood and danger
- Keep clinical judgment central

## Generating a Differential

- Ask for a broad list of possibilities
- Ask which findings discriminate between them
- Ask what must not be missed
### Why
- Breadth reduces premature closure
- Discriminators guide testing
- Dangerous diagnoses matter most

## Weighing the List

- Consider likelihood and severity
- Prioritise can't-miss diagnoses
- Match suggestions to the patient
### The rule
- Breadth first
- Discriminators next
- Judgment stays yours

## The Differential Prompt

```text
Given these findings [de-identified paste], give a broad
differential, the findings that discriminate between them,
and the can't-miss diagnoses. For clinician consideration.
```
- A broad differential
- Discriminating findings
- Can't-miss diagnoses

## Key Takeaways

- AI can generate a broad differential
- Ask which findings discriminate between possibilities
- Prioritise can't-miss diagnoses
- Weigh likelihood and severity against the patient
- Clinical judgment stays central

## Practice Challenge

1. Generate a differential for one case
2. List the discriminating findings
3. Identify the can't-miss diagnoses

**Example Quiz**

1. What does AI generate well?
- A) A broad differential
- B) A final diagnosis
- C) A prescription
- D) A discharge

**Answer:** A — AI helps generate a broad differential for consideration.

2. What should you ask for?
- A) Discriminating findings
- B) Only the answer
- C) Only treatment
- D) Nothing

**Answer:** A — Ask which findings discriminate between the possibilities.

3. What diagnoses matter most?
- A) Can't-miss diagnoses
- B) Rare ones only
- C) Common ones only
- D) None

**Answer:** A — Prioritise can't-miss, dangerous diagnoses in the differential.

4. What stays central?
- A) Clinical judgment

- D) The algorithm

**Answer:** A — Clinical judgment remains central when using AI for differentials.

### Module 4 · Step 3: Spotting Automation Bias and Anchoring

Automation bias is over-trusting the machine; anchoring is fixating on the first idea. Both are common when using AI in diagnosis.

Recognising these biases is how you stay the decision-maker rather than a rubber stamp.

## Learning Objectives

- Recognise automation bias
- Recognise anchoring
- Counter over-trust in AI
- Keep independent judgment

## The Two Biases

- **Automation bias:** trusting AI too readily
- **Anchoring:** fixating on the first diagnosis
- Both lead to missed alternatives
### Why
- Over-trust causes errors
- Anchoring closes the differential early
- Awareness is the first defence

## Countering Them

- Form your assessment before seeing AI's
- Ask AI for alternatives, not confirmation
- Actively look for disconfirming evidence
### The rule
- Assess first, then compare
- Seek disconfirmation
- Reconsider when evidence shifts

## The Bias-Check Prompt

```text
Here is my assessment and the AI suggestion [paste]. Help
me check for automation bias and anchoring: what evidence
would disconfirm each, and what alternatives are missing?
```
- Bias risks identified
- Disconfirming evidence
- Missing alternatives

## Key Takeaways

- Automation bias is over-trusting AI; anchoring is fixating early
- Both biases lead to missed alternatives
- Form your assessment before seeing AI's suggestion
- Ask for alternatives, not confirmation
- Actively seek disconfirming evidence

## Practice Challenge

1. Recall a case where you anchored early
2. Identify one automation bias risk in your workflow
3. Write a habit to counter each

**Example Quiz**

1. What is automation bias?
- A) Over-trusting AI
- B) Ignoring AI
- C) Typing too fast
- D) Reading too slowly

**Answer:** A — Automation bias is trusting AI output too readily.

2. What is anchoring?
- A) Fixating on the first diagnosis
- B) Ignoring data
- C) Skipping tests
- D) Rushing

**Answer:** A — Anchoring is fixating on the first idea and closing too early.

3. What should you seek?
- A) Disconfirming evidence
- B) Confirmation only
- C) Nothing
- D) Praise

**Answer:** A — Actively look for evidence that disconfirms your working diagnosis.

4. When should you reconsider?
- A) When evidence shifts
- B) Never
- C) Only at discharge
- D) Randomly

**Answer:** A — Reconsider when new evidence shifts the picture.

### Module 5 · Step 4: When to Escalate to a Colleague

AI can widen your thinking, but some situations need a human colleague: uncertainty, complexity, or risk beyond your scope.

Knowing when to escalate is a safety skill, not a weakness.

## Learning Objectives

- Recognise when to escalate
- Identify high-risk situations
- Escalate promptly
- Keep patient safety first

## When to Escalate

- Uncertainty that persists after review
- Complexity beyond your scope
- High-risk or deteriorating patients
- Any situation where AI suggests a serious diagnosis
### Why
- Timely escalation prevents harm
- Colleagues add expertise
- Safety outweighs pride

## How to Escalate

- Summarise the case clearly and briefly
- State the specific question
- Share your reasoning and uncertainty
### The rule
- Escalate early when unsure
- Be specific about the ask
- Safety first, always

## The Escalation Prompt

```text
Help me write a clear escalation summary for a colleague
about [case, de-identified]: the key findings, my reasoning,
my uncertainty, and the specific question I need answered.
```
- A brief case summary
- Your reasoning and uncertainty
- A specific question

## Key Takeaways

- Some situations need a human colleague, not AI
- Escalate persistent uncertainty and high-risk cases
- Escalate anything beyond your scope promptly
- Be specific about the question you need answered
- Safety outweighs pride

## Practice Challenge

1. List your personal escalation triggers
2. Write an escalation summary template
3. Rehearse the specific ask

**Example Quiz**

1. What needs escalation?
- A) Persistent uncertainty and high-risk cases
- B) Routine cases

**Answer:** A — Escalate persistent uncertainty, complexity, and high-risk patients.

2. When should you escalate?
- A) Early when unsure

- C) Never
- D) After discharge

**Answer:** A — Escalate early when you are unsure to prevent harm.

3. What should you state?
- A) The specific question
- B) Nothing

- D) Only the treatment

**Answer:** A — Be specific about the question you need your colleague to answer.

4. What outweighs pride?
- A) Patient safety
- B) Speed

- D) Cost

**Answer:** A — Patient safety always outweighs professional pride.

### Module 6 · Step 5: Documenting AI-Assisted Reasoning

When AI informs your thinking, the record should reflect it: what you considered, what you rejected, and why. This keeps the reasoning transparent and defensible.

Documentation of AI-assisted reasoning protects patients, supports continuity, and keeps accountability clear.

## Learning Objectives

- Document AI-assisted reasoning
- Record what was considered and rejected
- Keep accountability clear
- Make reasoning transparent

## What to Record

- That AI was used as a support tool
- The alternatives considered and rejected
- The reasoning for the final decision
### Why
- Transparency supports continuity
- It keeps accountability clear
- It helps if the case is reviewed

## How to Record It

- Keep it brief and clinical
- Focus on reasoning, not the tool's output verbatim
- State that the decision was clinically made
### The rule
- Note that AI assisted
- Record your reasoning
- The decision is yours

## The Documentation Prompt

```text
Help me document my AI-assisted reasoning for this case
[de-identified]: that AI was used as support, the
alternatives considered and rejected, and my rationale.
```
- AI use noted
- Alternatives and rationale
- A brief, clinical record

## Key Takeaways

- Document when AI informed your reasoning
- Record the alternatives considered and rejected
- Keep the record brief and clinical
- State that the clinical decision was yours
- Transparent reasoning supports continuity and accountability

## Practice Challenge

1. Add AI-assisted reasoning to one note
2. Record alternatives considered
3. Keep it brief and clinical

**Example Quiz**

1. What should the record note?
- A) That AI was used as support
- B) The whole AI output
- C) Nothing
- D) The tool's version

**Answer:** A — Note that AI assisted and record your own reasoning.

2. What should you record?
- A) Alternatives considered and rejected
- B) Only the final answer
- C) Only the tool name
- D) Nothing

**Answer:** A — Record the alternatives you considered and why you rejected them.

3. How should it read?
- A) Brief and clinical
- B) Long and technical
- C) Verbatim AI output
- D) Informal

**Answer:** A — Keep AI-assisted reasoning documentation brief and clinical.

4. Whose decision is it?
- A) The clinician's
- B) The AI's
- C) The vendor's
- D) The patient's alone

**Answer:** A — The clinical decision and its documentation remain the clinician's.

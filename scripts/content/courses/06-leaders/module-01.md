### Module 1 · AI Is a Leadership Topic

Many leaders hear 'AI' and file it under IT. That is a costly category error. AI is a **strategy, cost, talent, and risk decision wearing a technical coat**. Who uses it, where, on what data, and under what controls are all leadership calls - not engineering ones.

You do not need to build AI to lead it. You need to ask the right questions and hold the people who answer them accountable. That is the same skill you use for any major capital or operating decision.

**The leader's stance:** understand enough to set direction, fund the right things, and govern the risks - not to write code.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why AI is a leadership topic, not just IT
- Identify the strategy, cost, talent, and risk dimensions
- State what a leader must do versus what they can delegate
- Adopt the leader's stance on AI

## Why It Is a Leadership Topic

- AI decisions - use cases, data, controls, budget - are strategy and risk calls
- They shape cost structure, customer experience, and competitive position
- They touch talent: who is hired, retrained, and redeployed
- They carry legal, reputational, and security risk
### The four dimensions
- **Strategy** - where AI creates advantage
- **Cost** - what to fund and what to stop
- **Talent** - who builds, uses, and governs it
- **Risk** - privacy, accuracy, bias, security

## What a Leader Must Do

- Set direction: which problems matter and which do not
- Fund the right things and kill the wrong ones early
- Govern the risks with clear ownership and rules
- Hold teams accountable for measurable outcomes
### What you can delegate
- Model selection, prompt engineering, infrastructure
- Day-to-day tooling decisions within your guardrails
- Technical evaluation - but you own the final call

## The Leader's Stance

```text
A leader's AI posture:
1. Understand enough to ask good questions
2. Set direction and fund outcomes, not tools
3. Name an owner for every use case
4. Govern data, accuracy, and security
5. Review results on a clock (quarterly, not yearly)
You lead AI by asking strong questions - not by coding.
```
- Understanding enough beats understanding everything
- Questions expose hype faster than expertise
- Accountability, not authorship, is the leader's role

## Key Takeaways

- AI is a strategy, cost, talent, and risk decision - a leadership topic
- Leaders set direction, fund the right things, and govern risk
- You do not need to build AI to lead it
- Delegate technical work; own the outcomes and the guardrails
- Strong questions are a leader's most valuable AI skill

3. Write one question you should be asking that you are not

**Answer:** A — AI decisions - use cases, data, controls, budget - are strategy and risk calls for leaders.

2. What does a leader need to do about AI?

### Module 2 · The Four Capabilities Behind AI

Almost every AI product you will evaluate reduces to four capabilities: **classification, prediction, generation, and retrieval**. When a vendor says 'AI-powered', the honest answer to 'which of these four is doing the work?' determines what the product can actually do for your business.

This one question cuts through most marketing. A tool that classifies is not a tool that generates; a tool that retrieves is not a tool that predicts. Confusing them leads to buying the wrong thing.

## Learning Objectives

- Name the four capabilities behind almost all AI
- Ask vendors which capability is doing the work
- Match each capability to a business use
- Avoid buying the wrong capability for the problem

## The Four Capabilities

- **Classification** - sorting things into categories (spam, intent, risk level)
- **Prediction** - forecasting (demand, churn, maintenance needs)
- **Generation** - creating text, images, code, and video from prompts
- **Retrieval** - finding the right information fast (search, Q&A over your documents)
### Why the list matters
- It explains nearly every product you will see
- It converts marketing into a concrete claim
- It tells you what to test and measure

## Asking the Capability Question

```text
When a vendor says 'AI-powered', ask:
1. Which of the four capabilities does the product use?
2. Classification, prediction, generation, or retrieval?
3. What data does that capability need from us?
4. What accuracy has it achieved on work like ours?
5. What can it NOT do today?
The honest answer reveals real utility vs marketing.
```
- The capability answer is falsifiable and specific
- It exposes products that only do one thing
- It reveals the data and accuracy requirements early

## Matching Capability to Use

- Sorting and routing work → classification
- Forecasting demand, churn, or failures → prediction
- Drafting, summarising, and coding → generation
- Answering over your documents → retrieval
### The common mismatch
- Buying generation for a classification problem
- Buying retrieval when you need prediction
- The mismatch shows up as poor accuracy and wasted budget

## Key Takeaways

- Four capabilities: classification, prediction, generation, retrieval
- Ask every vendor which capability is doing the work
- Match the capability to the business problem
- The capability answer reveals real utility versus marketing
- Mismatched capabilities show up as poor accuracy and wasted budget

## Practice Challenge

1. Take one AI tool you already use and identify its capability
2. Write the capability question for a tool you are considering
3. Match one business problem to the right capability

**Example Quiz**

- A) Classification, prediction, generation, retrieval
- B) Colour, speed, memory, price
- C) App, cloud, phone, tablet
- D) None of these

**Answer:** A — Almost every AI product reduces to classification, prediction, generation, or retrieval.

2. Why ask which capability a product uses?
- A) It tells you what the product can really do
- B) It proves the vendor is rich
- C) It affects the logo
- D) It is a legal requirement

**Answer:** A — The honest capability answer reveals real business utility vs marketing.

3. What capability lets a chatbot answer over your documents?
- A) Retrieval
- B) Classification
- C) Prediction
- D) Generation alone

**Answer:** A — Q&A over your documents works through retrieval - find the right information.

4. What does a capability mismatch look like?
- A) Poor accuracy and wasted budget
- B) A cheaper invoice
- C) A faster demo
- D) A better logo

**Answer:** A — Buying the wrong capability for the problem shows up as poor results and wasted spend.

### Module 3 · Three Places AI Creates Value

Value from AI concentrates in three places: **efficiency, experience, and growth**. Every credible business case should sit clearly in one of them. If a proposal is not in a named bucket, keep asking until it is - value you cannot name, you cannot fund.

The bucket test is a discipline tool. It forces proposals to be specific about where the money or advantage comes from, and it lets you compare wildly different ideas on a common scale.

## Learning Objectives

- Name the three value buckets
- Place any proposal into efficiency, experience, or growth
- Use the bucket test to screen proposals
- Explain why unnamed value cannot be funded

## The Three Buckets

- **Efficiency** - do the same work faster or cheaper (automation, drafting, triage)
- **Experience** - serve customers better (personalisation, faster answers, smoother journeys)
- **Growth** - enter new markets, products, or pricing (new offers, insight-led strategy)
### Why exactly three
- They cover cost, customer, and new revenue
- They are mutually understandable across functions
- They let you compare unlike ideas fairly

## Applying the Bucket Test

```text
The bucket test for any AI proposal:
1. Is it efficiency, experience, or growth?
2. What is the measurable change? (reduce X by Y in Z time)
3. Who owns the outcome?
4. What would we do without AI - and does the problem still deserve money?
If it does not sit in a bucket with a measure, do not fund it yet.
```
- Naming the bucket forces specificity
- A measure makes the claim testable
- An owner makes it accountable

## Value You Cannot Name

- 'It will help us be more innovative' is not a bucket
- 'The board expects AI' is not a bucket
- 'It is the future' is not a bucket
### The funding rule
- Value you cannot name, you cannot fund
- Keep asking until the proposal names its bucket and measure
- Most weak proposals die at this step - correctly

## Key Takeaways

- Value concentrates in efficiency, experience, and growth
- Keep every business case in one named bucket
- Name the measurable change and its owner
- Value you cannot name, you cannot fund
- The bucket test screens proposals on a common scale

## Practice Challenge

1. Take three current AI ideas and assign each to a bucket
2. Write the measurable change for each
3. Cut one idea that cannot name its bucket

**Example Quiz**

1. What are the three value buckets?
- A) Efficiency, experience, growth
- B) Speed, size, colour
- C) Cost, ego, fashion
- D) Pilot, demo, blog

**Answer:** A — Value concentrates in efficiency (faster/cheaper), experience (better serving), or growth (new business).

2. Which bucket is 'chatbot answers 80% of support tickets'?
- A) Efficiency
- B) Growth
- C) Fashion
- D) None

**Answer:** A — Offloading repetitive support is an efficiency (and partly experience) play.

3. Why must every business case sit in a named bucket?
- A) Value you cannot name, you cannot fund
- B) It looks good in slides
- C) Vendors require it
- D) It shortens meetings

**Answer:** A — Named value keeps funding disciplined and outcomes measurable.

4. What should accompany the bucket in a proposal?
- A) A measurable change and a named owner
- B) A logo
- C) A hashtag
- D) A thank-you slide

**Answer:** A — A bucket plus a measure and an owner makes the case accountable.

### Module 4 · Cutting Through the Hype

Vendors use words loosely: 'AI-native', 'machine learning', 'large language models'. The words are chosen to impress, not to inform. Your defence is not expertise but a short set of clear questions that hype cannot survive.

Hype survives vague questions. It does not survive specific ones. Ask the four questions below and the room gets honest fast.

## Learning Objectives

- Use four questions to cut through vendor hype
- Ask for today's capability, not future promises
- Cover data, outcome, and failure explicitly
- Make the room honest with specificity

## The Four Hype-Cutting Questions

- **What exactly does it do today, for whom, at what accuracy?** (not what it will do)
- **What data does it need from us, and what will it touch?**
- **What is the measurable business outcome and timeframe?**
- **What happens when it fails - who is liable, how do we recover?**
### Why these four
- They cover capability, data, outcome, and risk
- They are answerable with facts, not adjectives
- They expose products that only exist in slide decks

## Using the Questions

```text
Run the four questions in order:
1. Capability: what does it do today, for whom, at what accuracy?
2. Data: what does it need, and what will it touch?
3. Outcome: what measurable result, by when?
4. Failure: who is liable, how do we recover?
Then ask: 'What happened in your last three failed deployments?'
```
- The last question reveals true maturity
- Vendors who speak freely about failures are usually honest
- Vague answers are themselves the answer

## What Good Answers Sound Like

- Specific accuracy numbers on work like yours
- A clear data map with safeguards
- A measurable outcome with a timeframe
- A written failure and exit plan
### The signal
- Specific answers mean a real product
- Vague answers mean a pitch
- Do not fund a pitch

## Key Takeaways

- Ask four questions: capability, data, outcome, failure
- Demand today's capability and accuracy, not future promises
- Insist on a measurable outcome with a timeframe
- Ask what happens when it fails - liability and recovery
- Hype survives vague questions, not specific ones

## Practice Challenge

1. Write the four hype-cutting questions on a card
2. Use them in your next vendor conversation
3. Note which answers were specific and which were vague

**Example Quiz**

1. Which question undercuts vendor hype fastest?
- A) What exactly does it do today, for whom, at what accuracy?
- B) When was the company founded?
- C) Do you have a mobile app?
- D) What font is in the deck?

**Answer:** A — Ask for today's capability, audience, and accuracy - not future promises.

2. Which question covers risk and failure?
- A) What happens when it fails and who is liable?
- B) What colour is the brand?
- C) How many employees do you have?
- D) Is there a demo video?

**Answer:** A — Failure handling - liability and recovery - separates real products from pitch decks.

3. What should a business outcome include?
- A) A measurable result and a timeframe
- B) A logo
- C) A hashtag
- D) A thank-you slide

**Answer:** A — Fund only measurable outcomes with timeframes; vague promises are not business cases.

4. What does a vendor's answer about past failures reveal?
- A) Their true maturity and honesty
- B) Their marketing budget
- C) Their office size
- D) Nothing useful

**Answer:** A — How a vendor speaks about failures reveals far more than polished demos.

### Module 5 · Classification vs Generative - What Your Business Needs

Two conversations dominate the AI market, and they are very different. **Predictive/classification AI** is reliable, narrow, and proven - pricing, fraud, demand, sorting. **Generative AI (LLMs)** is flexible, broad, and dramatic - drafting, search, assistants, code.

Many businesses should start with the boring one where the numbers are clear, and pilot the dramatic one where the use case is safe. Senior leaders usually overheat on generative and undervalue the classification wins right under their nose.

## Learning Objectives

- Contrast predictive and generative AI
- Identify proven, high-ROI classification wins
- Recognise where generative needs more controls
- Balance attention between the two

## The Two Conversations

- **Predictive/classification AI** - reliable, narrow, proven: pricing, fraud, demand, sorting
- Great ROI today, boring to pitch
- **Generative AI (LLMs)** - flexible, broad, dramatic: drafting, search, assistants, code
- Fast to deploy, needs more controls
### The contrast
- Predictive is measured in accuracy and cost
- Generative is measured in usefulness and control
- Both are real; they solve different problems

## Choosing Where to Start

```text
A balanced start:
1. List the boring, high-ROI classification wins (pricing, fraud, demand, sorting)
2. List safe generative use cases (drafting, internal search, summarising)
3. Start where the numbers are clear (classification)
4. Pilot generative where the use case is safe and reversible
5. Add controls before scaling generative
```
- Classification often funds the journey
- Generative builds capability and enthusiasm
- Controls scale generative safely

## The Common Mistake

- Leaders overheat on generative and under-value classification
- The classification win is often already sitting in your data
- The dramatic demo crowds out the profitable project
### The rule of thumb
- Start boring where the numbers are clear
- Pilot dramatic where the use case is safe
- Fund both on their merits, not their novelty

## Key Takeaways

- Predictive/classification AI is proven, narrow, and high-ROI
- Generative AI is flexible and fast, but needs more controls
- Start where the numbers are clear; pilot generative where safe
- Leaders tend to overheat on generative and miss classification wins
- Fund both on merits, not novelty

## Practice Challenge

1. List two classification wins and two safe generative use cases in your business
2. Pick the classification win with the clearest numbers
3. Identify one control needed before scaling generative

**Example Quiz**

1. What is predictive/classification AI good at?
- A) Pricing, fraud, demand, sorting - reliable and proven
- B) Making jokes
- C) Designing logos
- D) Vague promises

**Answer:** A — Predictive/classification AI delivers proven, high-ROI wins in pricing, fraud, and demand.

2. What is generative AI best for?
- A) Drafting, search, assistants, code - flexible and fast
- B) Guaranteed business decisions
- C) Cheap predictions at scale
- D) Nothing

**Answer:** A — Generative AI shines at drafting, search, assistants, and code - with more controls needed.

3. Where do many leaders misplace their attention?
- A) Overheating on generative and under-valuing classification wins
- B) Focusing only on boring problems
- C) Ignoring digitalisation
- D) Funding nothing

**Answer:** A — Leaders often overvalue the dramatic and miss the proven classification wins.

4. Where should you start?
- A) Where the numbers are clear
- B) With the flashiest demo
- C) With the cheapest vendor
- D) Nowhere

**Answer:** A — Start with the boring, clear-number classification wins; pilot generative where safe.

### Module 6 · Your First 30 Days as an AI Leader

You do not need a strategy document on day one. You need a focused first 30 days that builds your own literacy, surfaces real problems, and produces one small pilot. Thirty days of this beats a year of reading AI newsletters.

The plan is deliberately small: learn a little daily, interview teams, audit your data, watch a real demo, and choose one safe measurable pilot.

## Learning Objectives

- Run a focused first-30-days plan
- Build personal AI literacy in small daily doses
- Interview teams about costly, repetitive work
- Choose one small, safe, measurable pilot

## The Five Steps

- **Learn** - fifteen minutes a day on one capability and one business use case
- **Ask** - interview two teams about their most repetitive, costly, or error-prone work
- **Audit** - list the disorganised data you already hold (your hidden asset)
- **Observe a demo** - ask one vendor the four hype-cutting questions in person
- **Choose a pilot** - one small, safe, measurable use case to run in 90 days
### Why this order
- Learning and listening precede deciding
- The data audit surfaces the real constraints
- The pilot produces evidence, not opinion

## Running the Steps

```text
A leader's first 30 days:
1. 15 min/day learning - one capability, one use case
2. Interview 2 teams about repetitive, costly, error-prone work
3. List the disorganised data you already hold
4. Take one vendor demo and ask the four truth questions
5. Pick one small, safe, measurable pilot (90 days)
```
- Small daily learning compounds fast
- Team interviews reveal the real problems
- The demo tests your questions, not the vendor's slides

## What You Have at Day 30

- A shortlist of real problems worth solving
- A picture of your data's state
- One vendor conversation you controlled
- One funded pilot with a measure
### The payoff
- You are now literate enough to direct and govern
- You have evidence rather than enthusiasm
- You avoided the expensive tool-first trap

## Key Takeaways

- First 30 days: learn, ask teams, audit data, see a demo, pick a pilot
- Fifteen minutes a day beats weekend sprints
- Team interviews reveal the real, costly problems
- Existing data is the hidden asset your use cases need
- Scope the pilot small, safe, and measurable over 90 days

## Practice Challenge

1. Block 15 minutes a day for AI learning this month
2. Interview two teams about their most repetitive or costly work
3. Write your one-paragraph pilot proposal with a measure

# Mission 3: Evaluating AI Tools, Vendors & Build vs Buy

---
### Module 1 · Define Needs Before Vendors

Never invite a vendor to define your problem - they will define it in their favour. Write your needs first, in business language, before any demo shapes your thinking.

Evaluation is then scored against your criteria, not against the vendor's claims. The needs statement has four elements: the job, the user, the constraints, and the measures.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why needs come before vendors
- Write the four-element needs statement
- Define measures before seeing demos
- Keep vendors from shaping the problem

## The Four Elements

- **The job** - what business outcome must the tool move?
- **The user** - who uses it, how often, how skilled?
- **The constraints** - budget, data rules, security, integration, timeline
- **The measures** - how you will know it worked (reduce X by Y in Z time)
### Why write them down
- Written needs are defensible and stable
- They let you disqualify vendors quickly
- They anchor the eventual decision

## Writing the Needs Statement

```text
A needs-first statement:
1. The job: the business outcome the tool must move
2. The user: who uses it, how often, how skilled
3. The constraints: budget, data rules, security, integration, timeline
4. The measures: reduce X by Y in Z time
Write it before any vendor meeting.
```
- Start with the outcome, not the brand
- Constraints prevent wishful thinking
- Measures make success testable

## Why This Discipline Wins

- Vendors cannot move criteria they did not set
- Two of four vendors often disqualify themselves in the first meeting
- Decisions get faster and less political
### The outcome
- Better fit, fewer surprises
- Faster implementation
- A rationale you can defend

## Key Takeaways

- Define needs before any vendor shapes the conversation
- Four elements: job, user, constraints, measures
- Predefine the measurable outcome and timeframe
- Written needs disqualify vendors quickly
- Evaluation is scored against your criteria, not claims

### Module 2 · Build vs Buy vs Integrate

There are three honest routes, not two: buy, integrate, and build. Most organisations' sweet spot is integration - wiring proven AI services into existing systems.

**The trap to avoid:** 'Let's build our own LLM.' Unless your data moat and scale are exceptional, build is a vanity project dressed as strategy.

## Learning Objectives

- Name the three honest routes
- Identify most organisations' sweet spot
- Explain when building is justified
- Avoid the build-your-own-LLM trap

## The Three Routes

- **Buy** - a mature product off the shelf. Fastest, proven, but limited fit
- **Integrate** - wire proven AI services (APIs from large providers) into your existing systems. Most organisations' sweet spot
- **Build** - train or host your own models. Requires the deepest data, talent, and cost - rarely justified at the start
### Comparing them
- Buy trades fit for speed
- Integrate trades some control for speed and fit
- Build trades speed and cost for maximum control

## Choosing the Route

```text
Choosing build vs buy vs integrate:
1. Is there a mature product that fits? -> Buy
2. Do we need it inside our systems? -> Integrate (usual answer)
3. Do we have an exceptional data moat AND scale? -> Consider build
4. Otherwise: build is a vanity project dressed as strategy
Default to integrate; buy for narrow needs; build only with a moat.
```
- Integration is the fastest path to business value for most firms
- Buy for narrow, well-served needs
- Build only with an exceptional moat and scale

## The Build Trap

- 'Let's build our own LLM' is usually ego, not strategy
- It consumes scarce data talent and budget
- The frontier models improve faster than you can catch up
### When build is real
- Proprietary data that no vendor can access
- Scale that justifies the fixed cost
- A capability that is genuinely core to advantage

## Key Takeaways

- Three routes: buy, integrate, build
- Integrate is most organisations' sweet spot
- Buy for narrow, well-served needs
- Build only with an exceptional data moat and scale
- Otherwise build is a vanity project dressed as strategy

## Practice Challenge

1. Classify three current AI needs as buy, integrate, or build

3. Identify your default route

- B) Build everything in-house
- C) Buy five overlapping tools

- B) Always at the start
- C) Never, for any reason

4. What is the default route for most needs?

**Answer:** A — Default to integrate; buy for narrow needs; build only with a genuine moat.

### Module 3 · Vendor Questions That Reveal Truth

A short, sharp question set outperforms any demo. Demos are designed to impress; questions are designed to reveal. Five questions will tell you more about a vendor than an hour of slides.

The last question is the quiet killer. Vendors talk freely about failures they have learnt from, and their answer tells you their true maturity.

## Learning Objectives

- Use five questions that reveal real capability
- Ask about data, outcomes, and failures
- Read a vendor's maturity from their answers
- Control the demo instead of being dazzled

## The Five Questions

- 'What exactly does it do today, at what measured accuracy?'
- 'Which of the four capabilities does the product use?'
- 'What data does it touch, where does it live, who can see it?'
- 'What does a successful deployment look like at month 3 and month 12?'
- 'What happened in your last three failed deployments - and why?'
### Why they work
- They demand facts, not adjectives
- They cover capability, data, outcomes, and failure
- They are hard to fake without a real product

## Running the Question Set

```text
Run these in order, live:
1. What does it do today, at what measured accuracy?
2. Which of the four capabilities does it use?
3. What data does it touch, where does it live, who sees it?
4. What does success look like at month 3 and month 12?
5. What happened in your last three failed deployments?
The last answer tells you their true maturity.
```
- Month-3 success must be measurable outcomes, not tool adoption
- The failure question separates honest vendors from pitch decks
- Specific answers are the signal

## Reading the Answers

- Specific accuracy numbers → a real product
- A clear data map → they take security seriously
- Honest failure post-mortems → maturity
- Vague reassurance → a pitch, not a partner
### The decision
- Record the answers and compare vendors on the same questions
- Let the answers, not the charisma, drive the shortlist

## Key Takeaways

- Five questions: accuracy, capability, data, outcomes, failures
- Ask about month-3 and month-12 success
- The failure question reveals true vendor maturity
- Specific answers signal a real product
- Compare vendors on the same questions, not on charisma

## Practice Challenge

1. Write the five questions on one card
2. Use them in a live vendor conversation
3. Record and compare answers across two vendors

**Example Quiz**

1. Which question asks about today's real capability?
- A) What exactly does it do today, at what measured accuracy?
- B) What are your brand colours?
- C) Do you offer discounts?
- D) When was the website launched?

**Answer:** A — Demand real, current, measured capability - not roadmaps and demos.

2. Why ask about the last three failed deployments?
- A) It reveals true maturity and honest self-awareness
- B) It is a trick with no value
- C) It makes vendors angry
- D) It wastes meeting time

**Answer:** A — How a vendor speaks about failures reveals far more than polished demos.

3. What should a successful deployment look like at month 3?
- A) Measurable business outcomes, not just using the tool
- B) Everyone attending training
- C) The tool installed
- D) A new dashboard

**Answer:** A — Month-3 success is about measurable outcomes the business can see.

4. What signals a pitch rather than a product?
- A) Vague reassurance and no specifics
- B) Specific accuracy numbers
- C) A clear data map
- D) Honest failure stories

**Answer:** A — Specific, falsifiable answers signal a real product; vague reassurance is a pitch.

### Module 4 · Compare Costs Honestly

The sticker price is the smallest number on the invoice. Vendors sell on license price because it is easy to compare, while the costs that actually matter - migration, training, running, and exit - stay hidden until later.

**The test:** ask for total cost of ownership over three years and an exit plan in writing. Most vendor comparisons fail on those two alone.

## Learning Objectives

- Name the five cost components
- Explain why sticker price misleads
- Ask for three-year total cost of ownership
- Demand a written exit plan

## What the Invoice Hides

- License price is the headline everyone compares
- Migration, training, running, and exit cost the real money
- Hidden costs surface after the decision is locked in
### The consequence
- The 'cheap' option is often the expensive one
- Comparisons made on license price alone are wrong
- TCO is the honest number

## The Five Cost Components

```text
Compare total cost, not sticker price:
1. License - per seat, per usage, or per outcome
2. Migration - data, integration, and change effort
3. Training - who learns it, at what cost, for how long
4. Running - infrastructure, prompts, maintenance after go-live
5. Exit - how hard to leave, export data, and cancel
Ask for three-year TCO and a written exit plan.
```
- Each component can dwarf the license price
- Migration and exit are the most under-estimated
- Running costs continue forever

## The Three-Year Test

- Ask every vendor for three-year total cost of ownership
- Ask for the exit plan in writing
- Compare on TCO, never on sticker price
### Why it works
- TCO reveals lock-in risk
- It makes hidden costs visible before commitment
- It changes which vendor wins - often the more expensive-looking one

## Key Takeaways

- Sticker price is the smallest number on the invoice
- Five costs: license, migration, training, running, exit
- Ask for three-year total cost of ownership
- Demand an exit plan in writing
- Compare on TCO - the cheap option is often the expensive one

## Practice Challenge

1. List the five cost components for a tool you are considering
2. Request a three-year TCO and written exit plan
3. Compare two vendors on TCO rather than license price

**Example Quiz**

1. What is the smallest number on most invoices?
- A) The sticker price
- B) The exit fee
- C) Training cost
- D) Migration cost

**Answer:** A — License price is the headline; migration, training, running, and exit cost the real money.

2. Why ask for a written exit plan?
- A) It reveals true cost and lock-in risk
- B) It is a legal formality
- C) It is unused paper
- D) It slows deals usefully

**Answer:** A — An exit plan in writing reveals migration cost and how hard it is to change later.

3. What horizon should cost comparison use?
- A) Three-year total cost of ownership
- B) The first month only
- C) The demo month
- D) No horizon at all

**Answer:** A — Three-year TCO captures license, migration, training, running, and exit.

4. Which two costs are most under-estimated?
- A) Migration and exit
- B) License and seats
- C) Training and support
- D) Prompts and storage

**Answer:** A — Migration and exit are the most commonly under-estimated costs in AI purchases.

### Module 5 · The Vendor Scorecard

Score every candidate against the same criteria, with weights you chose before seeing demos. Weights make the decision transparent and defensible.

When the board asks 'why this one?', you show the scorecard, not an opinion. That is the difference between a procurement and a preference.

## Learning Objectives

- Set weighted criteria before demos
- Score candidates consistently
- Use the scorecard to defend the decision
- Avoid vendor-driven criteria

## Why Weights First

- Pre-chosen weights make the choice transparent
- They prevent the best demo from moving the criteria
- They let you answer 'why this one?' with evidence
### The discipline
- Choose criteria and weights before any demo
- Score every vendor against the same sheet
- The math replaces the argument

## A Sample Scorecard

```text
Weighted vendor scorecard (example):
Criterion                 Weight   Vendor A   Vendor B
Business impact            30%        8          5
Fit with our systems       25%        6          9
Data & security            20%        9          7
Total cost (3yr)           15%        7          8
Evidence & track record    10%        8          6
Weighted total             100%      ~7.3       ~6.9
```
- Business impact usually carries the most weight
- Fit, data/security, cost, and evidence follow
- The weighted total makes the choice obvious

## Defending the Decision

- Show the weighted scorecard, not a preference
- Cite the evidence behind each score
- Record the rationale in one paragraph
### The result
- Decisions become auditable
- Vendors compete on the criteria you set
- Future evaluations get faster and fairer

## Key Takeaways

- Set weighted criteria before seeing any demo
- Score every candidate on the same sheet
- Typical criteria: impact, fit, data/security, cost, evidence
- The weighted total makes the choice transparent
- Show the scorecard to defend the decision

## Practice Challenge

1. Set five weighted criteria for a live evaluation
2. Score two vendors against the same sheet
3. Write a one-paragraph rationale for the leader

**Example Quiz**

1. Why weight the scorecard criteria first?
- A) It makes decisions transparent and defensible
- B) It fills time
- C) It impresses vendors
- D) It guarantees the cheapest price

**Answer:** A — Pre-chosen weights make the choice transparent and answerable to anyone.

2. What does a good scorecard carry?
- A) Business impact, fit, data/security, cost, evidence
- B) Logos, fonts, colours
- C) Only price
- D) Only marketing claims

**Answer:** A — Score on business impact, system fit, data and security, three-year cost, and evidence.

3. How do you defend the choice to a board?
- A) Show the weighted scorecard
- B) Cite the best demo
- C) Name-drop the vendor
- D) Quote the discount

**Answer:** A — The weighted scorecard is the defensible answer to 'why this one?'.

4. What usually carries the most weight?
- A) Business impact
- B) Brand colour
- C) Company age
- D) Demo quality

**Answer:** A — Business impact typically leads, followed by fit, data/security, cost, and evidence.

### Module 6 · Run a Real Evaluation This Week

Close the mission with a live evaluation on one tool you are genuinely considering. Real evaluations are how you learn to buy AI well; the first one changes the way you see every future demo.

The five steps move you from needs to a recorded decision, with the vendor questions and cost analysis built in.

## Learning Objectives

- Run a five-step evaluation
- Write needs before the demo
- Ask the truth questions live
- Record a scorecard and rationale

## The Five Steps

- **Write needs** - one outcome, one user, constraints, measures
- **Score** - against your weights, before the demo
- **Demo with questions** - ask the five truth questions live
- **Cost** - get three-year TCO and exit terms in writing
- **Decide and record** - scorecard + a one-paragraph rationale
### Why this order
- Needs and scores precede the demo
- Questions and costs happen in the room
- The decision is recorded, not remembered

## Running the Evaluation

```text
A real evaluation this week:
1. Write needs: outcome, user, constraints, measures
2. Set weights and score candidates BEFORE the demo
3. In the demo, ask the five truth questions live
4. Request three-year TCO and exit terms in writing
5. Record the scorecard and a one-paragraph rationale
```
- Scoring before the demo keeps vendors from moving your criteria
- The live questions test the product, not the slide deck
- Writing down the decision anchors it in evidence

## Recording the Decision

- Save the scorecard and the rationale together
- Note what you would test differently next time
- Share the learning with the team
### The compounding skill
- Each evaluation sharpens your questions
- You learn to spot vague answers instantly
- Buying AI well becomes a repeatable capability

## Key Takeaways

- Five steps: needs, score, demo with questions, cost, record
- Write needs and scores before the demo
- Ask the five truth questions live
- Get three-year TCO and exit terms in writing
- Record the scorecard and a one-paragraph rationale

## Practice Challenge

1. Pick one tool and write its needs statement
2. Set weights and score it before the demo
3. Complete the scorecard and rationale after the demo

# Mission 2: Building Your AI Adoption Roadmap

## Learning Objectives

- Start the roadmap from business problems, not from shiny tools
- Portfolio thinking: quick wins, core improvements, experiments
- Understand the data and infrastructure preconditions
- Build in governance, risk, and compliance from day one
- Budget and sequence work in 90-day execution cycles
- Produce a one-page roadmap you can use on Monday

---
### Module 1 · Start from Business Problems, Not Tools

The most common roadmap failure is tool-first: 'we must adopt AI'. That gives you a portfolio of purchases with no outcomes.

Instead, start from problems you would solve anyway:

- Where is our cost-to-serve too high?
- Where do we lose customers due to slow or poor service?
- Where do errors or rework hurt us most?
- Where do competitors serve faster than us?

**The rule:** if the problem does not deserve money without AI, it does not deserve money with AI.

**Example Quiz**

1. What is the most common roadmap failure?
- A) Tool-first thinking - 'we must adopt AI'
- B) Starting from problems
- C) Asking customers
- D) Measuring outcomes

**Answer:** A — Tool-first roadmaps produce purchases without outcomes; start from problems.

2. Which is a good roadmap starting question?
- A) Where is cost-to-serve too high?
- B) Which AI product is trendy?
- C) What did a competitor's blog say?
- D) What is our vendor count?

**Answer:** A — Roadmaps start from real problem statements - cost, service, errors, competition.

3. What is the funding rule for AI projects?
- A) A problem that does not merit money without AI does not merit it with AI
- B) Every problem needs AI money
- C) Only trendy problems get funding
- D) AI projects never need ROI

**Answer:** A — The business problem justifies the money; AI is the chosen mechanism.

### Module 2 · Portfolio Thinking: Quick Wins, Core, Experiments

A healthy portfolio holds three kinds of work at once:

1. **Quick wins** - small, safe, 90-day, proven tech (document Q&A, drafting, automation). Keep 2-3 running.
2. **Core improvements** - bigger, higher value, longer (forecasting, pricing, personalisation engine). One at a time, funded properly.
3. **Experiments** - cheap, high-ambiguity bets with a kill criterion (agents, new use cases). Budgeted small.

Balance rule of thumb: strong in quick wins, disciplined on core, capped spending on experiments.

**Example Quiz**

1. What are the three portfolio pieces?
- A) Quick wins, core improvements, experiments
- B) Expensive, mid, cheap
- C) App, web, cloud
- D) Hire, buy, rent

**Answer:** A — Balance quick wins, one funded core improvement, and capped small experiments.

2. What should a quick win look like?
- A) Small, safe, proven tech, deliverable in 90 days
- B) Company-wide transformation
- C) Five-year plan
- D) Untested research

**Answer:** A — Quick wins are small, safe, proven, and deliverable in roughly 90 days.

3. How much should you spend on experiments?
- A) A small capped budget with a kill criterion
- B) Whatever the vendor suggests
- C) Most of the AI budget
- D) Nothing at all

**Answer:** A — Experiments are cheap and capped, with a clear kill criterion.

### Module 3 · Data: Your Hidden Prerequisite

AI models eat data. Your roadmap's real precondition is not the model - it is what the model can see.

Before a use case, ask:

- Do we have the data? (structured, labelled, or files to retrieve from)
- Is it accessible? (in one place, in a usable format)
- Is it lawful and safe to use it? (consent, PII, internal secrets)

**The honest truth:** most AI pilots fail on data quality or access, not model capability. Fix the data foundations and the pilots get cheaper and better.

**Example Quiz**

1. What is the real precondition for most AI projects?
- A) The data the model can see
- B) The newest model
- C) The most expensive vendor
- D) The biggest GPU

**Answer:** A — Models eat data - availability, quality, and access decide pilot success.

2. Which three data questions precede a use case?
- A) Availability, accessibility, lawfulness
- B) Colour, size, speed
- C) Logo, price, demo
- D) None of these

**Answer:** A — Check you have the data, can access it, and may lawfully use it.

3. Why do most AI pilots fail?
- A) Data quality and access, not model capability
- B) The models are weak
- C) Employees refuse AI
- D) Regulation always stops them

**Answer:** A — Pilots fail on data foundations far more often than model capability.

### Module 4 · Governance, Risk, and Compliance from Day One

Bake governance in early - retrofitting it is painful:

- **Privacy & confidentiality** - what data can enter which tools, with what safeguards.
- **Accuracy & accountability** - who owns the output and the decision when AI is involved.
- **Bias & fairness** - who reviews outcomes for harmful patterns.
- **Security** - who controls access, exports, and integrations.

**Minimum to start:** a one-page policy naming allowed tools, a data rule (no secrets in third-party tools), and an owner for each use case. That is enough to begin safely.

**Example Quiz**

1. When should governance be introduced?
- A) From day one - retrofitting is painful
- B) After the third project
- C) Never - it slows things
- D) Only if legally forced

**Answer:** A — Introducing governance early is far cheaper than retrofitting it.

2. Which is a minimum to start governing AI?
- A) A one-page policy, a no-secrets data rule, and a use-case owner
- B) A legal team per project
- C) A full risk model
- D) No controls until prod

**Answer:** A — Start with a one-page policy, a data rule, and clear ownership.

3. Who should own the decision when AI is involved?
- A) A named human accountable for the outcome
- B) The AI model
- C) The vendor
- D) The algorithm itself

**Answer:** A — People own decisions; AI is a tool inside them.

### Module 5 · Budgeting and Sequencing in 90-Day Cycles

Roadmaps in months fail; roadmaps in 90-day cycles deliver.

**Sequence rule:** fund quick wins first (cheap credibility and learning), push money into one core improvement, cap experiments.

**Budget rules:**

- Give the quick-win programme a modest recurring pool so it is always running.
- Fund the core improvement fully for 2-3 cycles before judging it.
- Give experiments a fixed envelope with a kill criterion, not a blank card.

Review every 90 days: what worked, what died, what continues.

**Example Quiz**

1. Why plan in 90-day cycles?
- A) They deliver learning and momentum where month-plans stall
- B) They match the fiscal year exactly
- C) They avoid all meetings
- D) They are longer than needed

**Answer:** A — 90-day cycles force delivery, review, and re-funding discipline.

2. What is the sequence rule for funding?
- A) Quick wins first, one core improvement, capped experiments
- B) Experiments first, everywhere
- C) Fund everything equally
- D) Core last, if ever

**Answer:** A — Quick wins build credibility; one core effort gets serious money; experiments stay capped.

3. When should you judge a core improvement?
- A) After 2-3 funded cycles, not after one
- B) After one week
- C) Never - it always continues
- D) Only when resold

**Answer:** A — Core improvements need 2-3 cycles to prove themselves - resist early kill.

### Module 6 · Your One-Page Roadmap

Condense everything into one page, four boxes:

1. **Now (next 90 days)** - 2-3 quick wins + prep for the core improvement.
2. **Next (90-180 days)** - the core improvement running + lessons applied.
3. **Later (180-365 days)** - scale the wins, second core candidate.
4. **Governance block** - policy owner, data rules, risk review dates.

Keep it to one page: it forces choices and keeps the whole organisation reading the same plan. Larger documents gather dust; one page gets followed.

**Example Quiz**

1. What belongs in the 'Now' box?
- A) 2-3 quick wins and prep for the core improvement
- B) All experiments
- C) Nothing until approved
- D) The five-year vision

**Answer:** A — The next 90 days focus on quick wins and on preparing the core effort.

2. Why keep the roadmap to one page?
- A) It forces choices and is actually read and followed
- B) Shorter is always better
- C) Regulators require it
- D) It hides complexity from the board

**Answer:** A — One page forces priorities and gets followed instead of filed.

3. What is in the governance block?
- A) Policy owner, data rules, risk review dates
- B) Logos and colours
- C) The company history
- D) Vendor prices

**Answer:** A — A named owner, data rules, and scheduled risk reviews keep adoption safe.

## Real-World Examples

**The problem-first portfolio —** A logistics firm wrote a roadmap around 'slow quote turnaround', not 'do AI'. Quick wins attacked document handling, one core project rebuilt pricing, experiments stayed capped. Twelve months later, turnaround was down 70%%.
**The data wake-up —** A bank picked a chatbot use case, then discovered the documents lived across five systems in conflicting formats. A month of data consolidation preceded the AI build - and the once-failing pilot then sailed.
**The 90-day review discipline —** A retailer reviews its roadmap every quarter, kills two experiments, re-funds one core project, and keeps three quick wins running. Momentum came from the discipline, not from any single tool.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Start from business problems; if a problem deserves no money without AI, it deserves none with it.
- Run a balanced portfolio: quick wins, one funded core improvement, capped experiments.
- Data quality and access are the real preconditions - fix foundations first.
- Introduce governance on day one: one-page policy, data rule, named owners.
- Budget and sequence in 90-day cycles with honest reviews.
- Keep the roadmap on one page so it gets read and followed.

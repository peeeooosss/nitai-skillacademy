### Module 1 · Start from Business Problems, Not Tools

The most common roadmap failure is tool-first: 'we must adopt AI'. That produces a portfolio of purchases with no outcomes - budget spent, capability bought, nothing changed. The alternative is to start from problems you would solve anyway.

**The rule:** if the problem does not deserve money without AI, it does not deserve money with AI. AI is the chosen mechanism, not the justification.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain the tool-first failure
- Start roadmaps from real business problems
- Use four diagnostic questions
- Apply the funding rule

## The Tool-First Failure

- 'We must adopt AI' gives you purchases without outcomes
- Tool-first roadmaps are driven by fear or fashion
- They produce overlapping products and no ownership
### Why it happens
- The board expects AI
- Competitors announced something
- Vendors are persuasive

## Starting from Problems

- Where is our cost-to-serve too high?
- Where do we lose customers due to slow or poor service?
- Where do errors or rework hurt us most?
- Where do competitors serve faster than us?
### The four lenses
- Cost, service, errors, competition
- Each lens points to a measurable problem
- Problems, not tools, drive the roadmap

## The Funding Rule

```text
The roadmap funding rule:
1. Name the problem in business terms
2. Ask: would we fund this problem without AI?
3. If no -> do not fund it with AI either
4. If yes -> state the measurable change and owner
5. Then choose the mechanism (AI or not)
AI is the mechanism, not the justification.
```
- The business problem justifies the money
- AI is one possible mechanism among several
- This rule kills weak proposals early

## Key Takeaways

- Tool-first roadmaps produce purchases without outcomes
- Start from problems you would solve anyway
- Use four lenses: cost, service, errors, competition
- If a problem deserves no money without AI, it deserves none with it
- AI is the mechanism, not the justification

## Practice Challenge

1. Write your three biggest problems in cost, service, or errors
2. Apply the funding rule to each
3. Pick the one problem that clearly deserves money on its own

**Answer:** A — Tool-first roadmaps produce purchases without outcomes; start from problems.

### Module 2 · Portfolio Thinking: Quick Wins, Core, Experiments

A healthy AI portfolio holds three kinds of work at once: quick wins, core improvements, and experiments. The balance prevents you from betting everything on one long project or drowning in scattered pilots.

**Balance rule of thumb:** strong in quick wins, disciplined on core, capped spending on experiments.

## Learning Objectives

- Name the three portfolio pieces
- Define what each piece looks like
- Apply the balance rule of thumb
- Set a kill criterion for experiments

## The Three Pieces

- **Quick wins** - small, safe, 90-day, proven tech (document Q&A, drafting, automation). Keep 2-3 running
- **Core improvements** - bigger, higher value, longer (forecasting, pricing, personalisation). One at a time, funded properly
- **Experiments** - cheap, high-ambiguity bets with a kill criterion (agents, new use cases). Budgeted small
### Why three
- Quick wins build credibility and learning
- Core improvements move the numbers
- Experiments keep options open cheaply

## Balancing the Portfolio

```text
A balanced AI portfolio:
1. Quick wins (2-3 running): small, safe, proven, 90 days
2. Core improvement (one): funded fully for 2-3 cycles
3. Experiments (capped): small budget + kill criterion
4. Review every 90 days: what worked, what died, what continues
Strong in quick wins, disciplined on core, capped on experiments.
```
- Quick wins are always running
- Only one core effort gets serious money
- Experiments stay small and time-boxed

## Kill Criteria and Discipline

- Every experiment needs a kill criterion before it starts
- A kill criterion is a pre-agreed stop condition, not a mood
- Core improvements get 2-3 cycles before judgement
### The discipline
- Killing experiments fast is a success, not a failure
- Protecting core work from early panic is maturity
- The review cadence enforces both

## Key Takeaways

- Hold three pieces at once: quick wins, core, experiments
- Keep 2-3 quick wins running for credibility and learning
- Fund one core improvement fully for 2-3 cycles
- Cap experiment spending and set a kill criterion
- Review the portfolio every 90 days

## Practice Challenge

1. List your current work as quick wins, core, or experiments
2. Write a kill criterion for one experiment
3. Decide which core improvement deserves full funding

**Example Quiz**

1. What are the three portfolio pieces?
- A) Quick wins, core improvements, experiments

**Answer:** A — Balance quick wins, one funded core improvement, and capped small experiments.

2. What should a quick win look like?
- A) Small, safe, proven tech, deliverable in 90 days
- B) Company-wide transformation
- C) Five-year plan
- D) Untested research

**Answer:** A — Quick wins are small, safe, proven, and deliverable in roughly 90 days.

3. How much should you spend on experiments?
- A) A small capped budget with a kill criterion
- B) Whatever the vendor suggests
- C) Most of the AI budget
- D) Nothing at all

**Answer:** A — Experiments are cheap and capped, with a clear kill criterion.

4. How long should a core improvement run before judging?
- A) 2-3 funded cycles
- B) One week

**Answer:** A — Core improvements need 2-3 cycles to prove themselves - resist early kill.

### Module 3 · Data: Your Hidden Prerequisite

AI models eat data. Your roadmap's real precondition is not the model - it is what the model can see. Most AI pilots fail on data quality or access, not model capability.

**The honest truth:** fix the data foundations and the pilots get cheaper and better. Before any use case, ask three questions about the data.

## Learning Objectives

- Name the three data questions before a use case
- Explain why pilots fail on data, not models
- Recognise your existing data as an asset
- Plan data foundations before build

## The Three Data Questions

- Do we have the data? (structured, labelled, or files to retrieve from)
- Is it accessible? (in one place, in a usable format)
- Is it lawful and safe to use it? (consent, PII, internal secrets)
### Why these three
- Availability, accessibility, lawfulness
- Each can stop a pilot dead
- Each is fixable with planning

## Why Pilots Fail on Data

- Models are capable enough for most business problems
- The data is scattered, unlabelled, or inaccessible
- Access and quality decide pilot success far more than model choice
### The wake-up
- A use case can look easy and stall for a month on data
- Consolidation before build is normal, not a failure
- Data work is the unglamorous foundation

## Fixing the Foundations

```text
Data-foundation checklist before a use case:
1. Do we have the data we need?
2. Is it in one accessible place in a usable format?
3. Are we lawfully allowed to use it (consent, PII)?
4. Who stewards it and keeps it clean?
5. What is the minimum consolidation needed to start?
```
- Answer these before committing budget
- Assign a data steward early
- Fix the minimum, then iterate

## Key Takeaways

- Data availability, accessibility, and lawfulness precede any use case
- Most pilots fail on data, not model capability
- Existing data is your hidden asset
- Consolidation before build is normal
- Assign a data steward and fix the minimum first

## Practice Challenge

1. Audit one use case's data: available, accessible, lawful?
2. Identify the minimum consolidation needed
3. Name a data steward for the use case

**Example Quiz**

1. What is the real precondition for most AI projects?
- A) The data the model can see
- B) The newest model
- C) The most expensive vendor
- D) The biggest GPU

**Answer:** A — Models eat data - availability, quality, and access decide pilot success.

2. Which three data questions precede a use case?
- A) Availability, accessibility, lawfulness
- B) Colour, size, speed
- C) Logo, price, demo
- D) None of these

**Answer:** A — Check you have the data, can access it, and may lawfully use it.

3. Why do most AI pilots fail?
- A) Data quality and access, not model capability
- B) The models are weak
- C) Employees refuse AI
- D) Regulation always stops them

**Answer:** A — Pilots fail on data foundations far more often than model capability.

4. What is data consolidation before a build?
- A) Normal and necessary groundwork
- B) A sign of failure
- C) A waste of time
- D) Optional

**Answer:** A — Consolidation before build is normal; fix the minimum foundations and iterate.

### Module 4 · Governance, Risk, and Compliance from Day One

Bake governance in early - retrofitting it is painful. The instinct to 'move fast and fix later' fails badly with AI, because data, accuracy, and security problems compound quietly until they surface as incidents.

**Minimum to start:** a one-page policy naming allowed tools, a data rule (no secrets in third-party tools), and an owner for each use case. That is enough to begin safely.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why governance starts on day one
- Cover privacy, accuracy, bias, and security
- Draft the minimum viable governance
- Assign ownership for every use case

## The Four Governance Areas

- **Privacy & confidentiality** - what data can enter which tools, with what safeguards
- **Accuracy & accountability** - who owns the output and the decision when AI is involved
- **Bias & fairness** - who reviews outcomes for harmful patterns
- **Security** - who controls access, exports, and integrations
### Why day one
- Retrofitting controls is expensive and disruptive
- Early governance builds trust and speeds adoption later
- It prevents incidents before they happen

## The Minimum Viable Governance

```text
Minimum AI governance to start safely:
1. A one-page policy naming allowed and banned tools
2. A data rule: no secrets or personal data in third-party tools
3. A named owner for every use case
4. A review date for risks and outcomes
That is enough to begin - expand as you scale.
```
- A one-page policy is enough to start
- The data rule prevents the worst breaches
- Named ownership makes accountability real

## People Own Decisions

- A named human is accountable for each outcome
- The AI model, the vendor, and the algorithm do not own decisions
- AI is a tool inside a human decision
### Why this matters
- Accountability is what makes governance enforceable
- It prevents 'the algorithm decided' excuses
- It aligns AI use with existing risk practice

## Key Takeaways

- Introduce governance from day one - retrofitting is painful
- Cover privacy, accuracy/accountability, bias, and security
- Minimum: one-page policy, a no-secrets data rule, a named owner
- People own decisions; AI is a tool inside them
- Expand governance as you scale

## Practice Challenge

1. Draft a one-page AI policy for your team
2. Write the no-secrets data rule
3. Assign an owner to one live use case

**Example Quiz**

1. When should governance be introduced?
- A) From day one - retrofitting is painful
- B) After the third project
- C) Never - it slows things
- D) Only if legally forced

**Answer:** A — Introducing governance early is far cheaper than retrofitting it.

2. Which is a minimum to start governing AI?
- A) A one-page policy, a no-secrets data rule, and a use-case owner
- B) A legal team per project
- C) A full risk model
- D) No controls until prod

**Answer:** A — Start with a one-page policy, a data rule, and clear ownership.

3. Who should own the decision when AI is involved?
- A) A named human accountable for the outcome
- B) The AI model
- C) The vendor
- D) The algorithm itself

**Answer:** A — People own decisions; AI is a tool inside them.

4. What are the four governance areas?
- A) Privacy, accuracy/accountability, bias, security
- B) Speed, cost, colour, size
- C) Hire, buy, rent, build
- D) None of these

**Answer:** A — Privacy, accuracy and accountability, bias and fairness, and security.

### Module 5 · Budgeting and Sequencing in 90-Day Cycles

Roadmaps in months fail; roadmaps in 90-day cycles deliver. A quarter is long enough to produce a real result and short enough to keep attention and re-funding honest.

**Sequence rule:** fund quick wins first (cheap credibility and learning), push money into one core improvement, cap experiments.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why 90-day cycles work
- Apply the sequence rule for funding
- Set budget rules for each portfolio piece
- Run a quarterly review

## Why 90-Day Cycles

- They force delivery, review, and re-funding discipline
- They match how organisations actually sustain attention
- They surface failure early enough to correct
### The rhythm
- Plan a quarter, deliver, review, re-fund
- Each cycle produces evidence
- Momentum comes from the cadence

## Budget Rules

- Give the quick-win programme a modest recurring pool so it is always running
- Fund the core improvement fully for 2-3 cycles before judging it
- Give experiments a fixed envelope with a kill criterion, not a blank card
### The sequence
- Quick wins first - cheap credibility and learning
- Then one core improvement with serious money
- Experiments stay capped throughout

## The Quarterly Review

```text
Every 90-day review:
1. What quick wins delivered measurable value?
2. What did the core improvement prove so far?
3. Which experiments hit their kill criterion?
4. What do we re-fund, stop, or start?
5. Update the one-page roadmap
```
- Review is where discipline lives
- Kill failed experiments without drama
- Re-fund only what shows evidence

## Key Takeaways

- Plan and fund in 90-day cycles, not months
- Sequence: quick wins first, one core improvement, capped experiments
- Quick wins get a modest recurring pool
- Core gets full funding for 2-3 cycles before judgement
- Review every 90 days: fund, stop, or start

## Practice Challenge

1. Write your next 90-day plan with the three portfolio pieces
2. Set the experiment envelope and kill criterion
3. Schedule your quarterly review date

**Example Quiz**

1. Why plan in 90-day cycles?
- A) They deliver learning and momentum where month-plans stall
- B) They match the fiscal year exactly
- C) They avoid all meetings
- D) They are longer than needed

**Answer:** A — 90-day cycles force delivery, review, and re-funding discipline.

2. What is the sequence rule for funding?
- A) Quick wins first, one core improvement, capped experiments
- B) Experiments first, everywhere
- C) Fund everything equally
- D) Core last, if ever

**Answer:** A — Quick wins build credibility; one core effort gets serious money; experiments stay capped.

3. When should you judge a core improvement?
- A) After 2-3 funded cycles, not after one
- B) After one week
- C) Never - it always continues
- D) Only when resold

**Answer:** A — Core improvements need 2-3 cycles to prove themselves - resist early kill.

4. What does the quarterly review decide?
- A) What to re-fund, stop, or start
- B) Only the budget colour
- C) The vendor list
- D) Nothing

**Answer:** A — Review quick wins, core progress, and experiment kills, then update the roadmap.

### Module 6 · Your One-Page Roadmap

Condense everything into one page with four boxes. Larger documents gather dust; one page gets followed. The constraint forces choices and keeps the whole organisation reading the same plan.

The four boxes are Now, Next, Later, and a governance block. Together they turn the mission's ideas into a document you can use on Monday.

## Learning Objectives

- Build a one-page roadmap with four boxes
- Fill Now, Next, and Later with the right work
- Include a governance block
- Explain why one page beats a long document

## The Four Boxes

- **Now (next 90 days)** - 2-3 quick wins + prep for the core improvement
- **Next (90-180 days)** - the core improvement running + lessons applied
- **Later (180-365 days)** - scale the wins, second core candidate
- **Governance block** - policy owner, data rules, risk review dates
### Why four boxes
- They cover the near, mid, and long term
- Governance sits alongside delivery, not after it
- Each box has a clear owner and measure

## Writing the Roadmap

```text
One-page AI roadmap:
NOW (90 days): 2-3 quick wins + core prep
NEXT (90-180): core improvement live + lessons applied
LATER (180-365): scale wins + second core candidate
GOVERNANCE: policy owner, data rules, risk review dates
One page. Four boxes. Clear owners. Review every 90 days.
```
- Keep it to a single page
- Name owners and measures in each box
- Review and update at each cycle

## Why One Page

- It forces choices and priorities
- It is actually read and followed
- It keeps the whole organisation aligned
### The alternative
- Long documents look thorough and gather dust
- They hide indecision in detail
- One page is a leadership tool; a binder is not

## Key Takeaways

- A one-page roadmap has four boxes: Now, Next, Later, Governance
- Now: 2-3 quick wins plus core prep
- Next: core improvement live; Later: scale and next candidate
- Governance block: owner, data rules, risk review dates
- One page forces choices and gets followed

## Practice Challenge

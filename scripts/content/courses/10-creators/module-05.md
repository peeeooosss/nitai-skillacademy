### Module 1 · The Numbers That Matter for Creators

Vanity metrics like follower count feel good but say little. The numbers that matter show whether people watch, stay, and come back.

AI can help you read your analytics and turn numbers into decisions.

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the metrics that matter
- Look past vanity metrics
- Turn numbers into decisions
- Use AI to read analytics

## Metrics That Matter

- Retention: how long people watch
- Watch time and completion rate
- Saves, shares, and comments
- Returning viewers
### Why
- These show real value
- Followers can be passive
- Decisions need the right numbers

## Past Vanity Metrics

- Follower count is a lagging number
- Views without retention are noise
- Engagement quality beats raw reach
### The rule
- Watch retention first
- Value saves and shares
- Judge with context, not one number

## The Metrics Prompt

```text
Here are my [platform] analytics [paste]. Tell me which
numbers matter most for growth, what they suggest, and
3 actions to take. Ignore vanity metrics.
```
- Metrics that matter
- What they suggest
- Three actions

## Key Takeaways

- Vanity metrics say little; retention says a lot
- Track retention, watch time, saves, shares, and comments
- Follower count is a lagging indicator
- Engagement quality beats raw reach
- Use AI to turn analytics into decisions

## Practice Challenge

1. List your five most important metrics
2. Ignore follower count for a week
3. Read one report with AI

**Answer:** A — Follower count lags behind real engagement and retention.

3. What beats raw reach?

### Module 2 · Step 1: Read Your Retention Curve

The retention curve shows how many viewers are still watching at each moment. Where it drops tells you exactly what to fix.

Reading the curve is the single most useful analytics skill for short and long video alike.

## Learning Objectives

- Read a retention curve
- Find where viewers drop off
- Diagnose weak hooks and middles
- Fix content from data

## The Curve

- Starts high and shows drop-off over time
- A sharp early drop means a weak hook
- A slow middle drop means pacing issues
### Why
- It shows exactly where you lose people
- Early and mid drops have different fixes
- Data beats guessing

## Diagnosing Drops

- Early drop: rewrite the hook
- Mid drop: tighten pacing and cut filler
- Late drop: the payoff may be too late
### The rule
- Read the curve per video
- Match the fix to the drop
- Test one change at a time

## The Retention Prompt

```text
Here is my retention data [paste]. Identify where viewers
drop off, diagnose likely causes (hook, pacing, payoff),
and suggest fixes to test next time.
```
- Drop-off points
- Likely causes
- Fixes to test

## Key Takeaways

- The retention curve shows where viewers leave
- A sharp early drop means a weak hook
- A slow middle drop means pacing issues
- A late drop means the payoff comes too late
- Match the fix to the drop and test one change at a time

## Practice Challenge

1. Read the retention curve of your last three videos
2. Identify the biggest drop
3. Write one fix to test

**Example Quiz**

1. What does a sharp early drop mean?
- A) A weak hook
- B) A bad ending
- C) Too short
- D) Too many ads

**Answer:** A — Viewers leaving in the first seconds points to a weak hook.

2. What does a slow middle drop mean?
- A) Pacing issues
- B) A weak hook
- C) A bad thumbnail
- D) Too many comments

**Answer:** A — Mid-video drop-off usually means pacing or filler problems.

3. What does a late drop suggest?
- A) The payoff comes too late
- B) The hook is bad
- C) The title is bad
- D) The audio is bad

**Answer:** A — If viewers leave near the end, the payoff may be too late.

4. How many changes should you test at once?
- A) One
- B) Ten
- C) None
- D) As many as possible

**Answer:** A — Test one change at a time so you know what worked.

### Module 3 · Step 2: Find What Actually Works

With data, patterns emerge: certain topics, hooks, and formats consistently outperform others. Finding them tells you where to focus.

AI can analyse a set of posts and surface the patterns behind the winners.

## Learning Objectives

- Find patterns in your best posts
- Identify winning topics and formats
- Separate signal from noise
- Focus on what works

## Finding Patterns

- Compare top posts against the rest
- Look for shared topics and hooks
- Note formats and lengths that win
### Why
- Patterns reveal what to repeat
- Winners are repeatable
- Focus beats random effort

## Signal vs Noise

- One good post can be luck
- Repeating patterns are signal
- Judge across many posts, not one
### The rule
- Compare top vs rest
- Trust patterns, not one-offs
- Focus effort on winners

## The Pattern Prompt

```text
Here are my top 5 and bottom 5 posts with metrics [paste].
Find the patterns that separate them: topics, hooks,
formats, lengths. Tell me what to do more and less of.
```
- Patterns behind winners
- What to do more of
- What to do less of

## Key Takeaways

- Patterns emerge when you compare your best posts to the rest
- Winning topics, hooks, and formats are repeatable
- One good post can be luck; patterns are signal
- Judge across many posts, not one
- Focus your effort on what actually works

### Module 4 · Step 3: Double Down on Winners

Once you know what works, the fastest growth comes from making more of it, not from constantly chasing new formats.

Double down by turning winning topics into series and expanding on proven ideas.

## Learning Objectives

- Double down on winning content
- Turn winners into series
- Expand proven ideas
- Avoid constant reinvention

## Why Double Down

- Proven ideas reduce risk
- Series build returning viewers
- Audiences reward consistency
### Why
- Winners are lower risk
- Series deepen loyalty
- Focus compounds faster

## How to Expand

- Turn a winning post into a series
- Explore sub-topics of the winner
- Reuse the hook style that worked
### The rule
- Make more of what works
- Build series from winners
- Reinvent only when needed

## The Double-Down Prompt

These posts performed best [paste]. Suggest how to double
down: a series idea, 5 sub-topic posts, and the hook style
to reuse for each.

- A series idea

## Key Takeaways

- The fastest growth comes from making more of what works
- Turn winning posts into series
- Explore sub-topics of proven ideas
- Reuse hook styles that performed
- Reinvent only when the data calls for it

### Module 6 · Step 5: The Growth Experiment Loop

Growth is a loop: form a hypothesis, test it, read the result, keep what works, repeat. It turns content into a series of small, informed bets.

AI helps design experiments and analyse results so each week is smarter than the last.

## Learning Objectives

- Run a growth experiment loop
- Form testable hypotheses
- Measure and keep winners
- Improve every cycle

## The Loop

- **Hypothesis:** 'This hook or topic will do better'
- **Test:** publish and measure it
- **Result:** compare to your baseline
- **Keep or drop:** repeat what works
### Why
- Small bets reduce risk
- Testing replaces guessing
- The loop compounds learning

## Designing Tests

- Change one variable at a time
- Compare against a baseline
- Give each test enough time
### The rule
- One variable per test
- Always compare to baseline
- Keep winners, drop losers

## The Experiment Prompt

```text
Design a 4-week growth experiment plan for my [platform]
channel on [niche]: one hypothesis per week, what to
change, what to measure, and how to judge success.
```
- Four weekly hypotheses
- What to change and measure
- How to judge success

## Key Takeaways

- Growth is a loop: hypothesise, test, measure, keep, repeat
- Change one variable per test
- Always compare results to a baseline
- Keep winners and drop losers
- Small informed bets compound into growth

## Practice Challenge

1. Write one hypothesis for next week
2. Define what to measure
3. Set a baseline to compare against

**Example Quiz**

1. What are the loop steps?
- A) Hypothesise, test, measure, keep or drop
- B) Post, forget, repeat
- C) Buy, boost, hope
- D) Write, film, delete

**Answer:** A — The growth loop is hypothesise, test, measure, and keep or drop.

2. How many variables per test?
- A) One
- B) Ten
- C) None
- D) As many as possible

**Answer:** A — Change one variable at a time to learn what caused the result.

3. What should you compare against?
- A) A baseline
- B) Nothing
- C) Competitors only
- D) Your mood

**Answer:** A — Always compare test results against a baseline.

4. What do you do with winners?
- A) Keep them
- B) Drop them
- C) Ignore them
- D) Delete them

**Answer:** A — Keep what works and drop what does not.
